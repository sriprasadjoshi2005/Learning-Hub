# Lesson 9: Informal Fallacies and Errors in Reasoning

A fallacy is an error in reasoning that weakens an argument. Some fallacies involve incorrect logical structures, while others arise from irrelevant evidence, misleading language or unsupported assumptions.

Fallacies are common in everyday conversations, advertising, political debates and social media.

Learning to recognize them helps us evaluate arguments more carefully and avoid being misled by persuasive but weak reasoning.

## 1. What Is a Fallacy?

A fallacy is a pattern of reasoning that contains a defect.

A fallacious argument may appear convincing even though its premises do not adequately support its conclusion.

Important distinction:

- A false statement is not automatically a fallacy.
- A fallacy concerns a problem in reasoning.
- A conclusion can be true even when the argument supporting it is fallacious.

## 2. Formal and Informal Fallacies

### 2.1 Formal Fallacies

Formal fallacies involve errors in the logical structure of an argument.

Example: Affirming the Consequent.

Structure:

\[
P \rightarrow Q
\]

\[
Q
\]

\[
\therefore P
\]

This is invalid because Q may have other explanations.

Example:

- If it rains, the ground becomes wet.
- The ground is wet.
- Therefore, it rained.

The ground could be wet because of irrigation.

### 2.2 Informal Fallacies

Informal fallacies involve problems related to content, relevance, assumptions or language.

They cannot always be identified by examining symbolic structure alone.

## 3. Fallacies of Relevance

These occur when an argument introduces information that does not adequately support the conclusion.

### 3.1 Ad Hominem

An ad hominem argument attacks the person presenting an argument instead of addressing the argument itself.

Example:

"Her economic proposal is incorrect because she has no sense of style."

The person's appearance is irrelevant to the proposal's logical quality.

Criticizing someone's expertise or credibility can sometimes be relevant, but merely attacking personal characteristics does not establish that an argument is false.

### 3.2 Straw Man

A straw man fallacy misrepresents an opponent's position to make it easier to attack.

Example:

Person A: "We should reduce unnecessary plastic consumption."

Person B: "You want to ban every plastic product."

Person B has exaggerated the original position.

### 3.3 Appeal to Authority

An appeal to authority becomes fallacious when a person's status or reputation is treated as sufficient proof of a claim without appropriate justification.

Example:

"A famous actor says this medical treatment works, so it must be effective."

The actor's popularity does not establish medical effectiveness.

Expert testimony can be relevant when the authority has genuine expertise in the subject and the claim is appropriately supported.

### 3.4 Red Herring

A red herring introduces an irrelevant issue that distracts from the original question.

Example:

Question: "Why was the project completed late?"

Response: "Our company has an excellent reputation and many satisfied customers."

The response avoids addressing the delay.

## 4. Fallacies of Presumption

These arguments rely on questionable or unsupported assumptions.

### 4.1 Hasty Generalization

A hasty generalization draws a broad conclusion from insufficient evidence.

Example:

"Two people from that region were rude. Therefore, everyone from that region is rude."

The sample is insufficient to support the universal conclusion.

### 4.2 False Dilemma

A false dilemma presents only two choices when additional possibilities exist.

Example:

"Either you agree with this decision or you are against progress."

A person may support progress while disagreeing with the specific decision.

### 4.3 Circular Reasoning

Circular reasoning uses the conclusion itself as support for the conclusion.

Example:

"This law is fair because it is a just law."

The statement does not provide independent justification.

### 4.4 Slippery Slope

A slippery slope argument claims that one action will inevitably lead to a chain of increasingly serious consequences without sufficient evidence for the predicted chain.

Example:

"If students are allowed to use calculators, they will stop learning mathematics, and eventually nobody will understand numbers."

A chain of consequences needs justification rather than mere assertion.

## 5. Fallacies of Ambiguity

These occur when unclear or changing meanings create misleading reasoning.

### 5.1 Equivocation

Equivocation occurs when a word is used in different senses within the same argument.

Example:

- A feather is light.
- What is light cannot be dark.
- Therefore, a feather cannot be dark.

The word "light" changes meaning from low weight to brightness.

### 5.2 Amphiboly

Amphiboly arises from grammatical ambiguity.

Example:

"Visiting relatives can be exhausting."

This sentence could mean that relatives who visit are exhausting or that the activity of visiting relatives is exhausting.

## 6. Causal Fallacies

These involve incorrect assumptions about cause-and-effect relationships.

### 6.1 Post Hoc Fallacy

The post hoc fallacy assumes that because one event happened before another, it caused the later event.

Example:

"I wore a particular shirt and then passed an examination. Therefore, the shirt caused my success."

Sequence alone does not establish causation.

### 6.2 Correlation-Causation Confusion

Correlation means that two variables are statistically associated.

Causation means that changes in one variable produce or contribute to changes in another.

Example:

Ice cream sales and drowning incidents may both increase during summer.

This does not mean ice cream consumption causes drowning. Temperature and seasonal activities may influence both.

## 7. Other Common Fallacies

### 7.1 Appeal to Popularity

A claim is treated as true simply because many people believe it.

Example:

"Millions of people believe this theory, so it must be correct."

Popularity does not guarantee truth.

### 7.2 Appeal to Emotion

An argument attempts to establish a conclusion primarily through emotional reactions rather than relevant evidence.

Example:

"You must support this proposal because otherwise you do not care about children."

Emotional considerations can be relevant to moral decisions, but they cannot replace adequate reasoning.

### 7.3 False Analogy

A false analogy assumes that two things are sufficiently similar in relevant respects when the similarities do not support the conclusion.

Example:

"Managing a country is exactly like managing a household, so every household rule should become national policy."

The analogy overlooks important differences in scale, institutions and responsibilities.

## 8. How to Identify Fallacies

Use the following questions when examining an argument:

1. Does the evidence actually support the conclusion?
2. Is the argument attacking the claim or attacking the person?
3. Is the conclusion broader than the available evidence?
4. Are important alternatives being ignored?
5. Are words being used consistently?
6. Is a causal relationship being assumed from mere correlation?
7. Is the argument relying on popularity or emotional pressure?
8. Are hidden assumptions justified?

## 9. Practical Application: Social Media Claims

Consider the statement:

"Everyone online agrees that this product is excellent, so it must be the best product available."

Possible reasoning errors:

- Appeal to popularity.
- Unrepresentative evidence.
- Lack of independent comparison.
- Confusion between popularity and quality.

A better evaluation would examine independent reviews, product specifications, testing methods and alternative products.

## 10. Summary

- Fallacies are errors in reasoning.
- Formal fallacies involve invalid logical structures.
- Informal fallacies involve problems with content, relevance, assumptions or language.
- Ad hominem attacks the person rather than the argument.
- Straw man misrepresents an opposing position.
- Hasty generalization draws broad conclusions from insufficient evidence.
- False dilemma ignores additional possibilities.
- Equivocation uses ambiguous or changing meanings.
- Post hoc reasoning mistakes sequence for causation.
- Recognizing fallacies improves critical thinking and argument evaluation.
