# Lesson 45: Logic, Argumentation and Philosophical Fallacies

Logic is the systematic study of valid reasoning. It provides tools for evaluating arguments, identifying contradictions, distinguishing strong evidence from weak reasoning, and recognizing errors in everyday discussions and philosophical debates.

## 1. What Is an Argument?

An argument is a collection of statements in which some statements support another statement.

The supporting statements are called premises.

The statement being supported is called the conclusion.

Example:

Premise 1: All humans are mortal.

Premise 2: Socrates is human.

Conclusion: Socrates is mortal.

Arguments should be evaluated based on their logical structure and the truth of their premises.

## 2. Deductive and Inductive Arguments

### 2.1 Deductive Arguments

A deductive argument attempts to establish a conclusion that necessarily follows from its premises.

A deductive argument is valid if its conclusion cannot be false when all its premises are true.

A valid argument with true premises is called sound.

### 2.2 Inductive Arguments

Inductive arguments attempt to establish conclusions that are probable based on available evidence.

Their strength depends on:
- Quality of evidence.
- Number of observations.
- Representativeness of samples.
- Alternative explanations.

## 3. Formal Logic

Formal logic studies the structure of arguments independently of their specific subject matter.

### 3.1 Propositions

A proposition is a statement that can be true or false.

Examples:

\[
P: \text{It is raining}
\]

\[
Q: \text{The road is wet}
\]

### 3.2 Logical Connectives

Common logical connectives include:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| \(\neg P\) | NOT P |
| \(P \land Q\) | P AND Q |
| \(P \lor Q\) | P OR Q |
| \(P \rightarrow Q\) | If P, then Q |
| \(P \leftrightarrow Q\) | P if and only if Q |

### 3.3 Modus Ponens

Modus ponens is a valid argument form.

\[
P \rightarrow Q
\]

\[
P
\]

\[
\therefore Q
\]

Example:

If a number is divisible by 4, it is even.

The number 12 is divisible by 4.

Therefore, 12 is even.

### 3.4 Modus Tollens

Modus tollens is another valid argument form.

\[
P \rightarrow Q
\]

\[
\neg Q
\]

\[
\therefore \neg P
\]

Example:

If the device is functioning, its indicator light is on.

The indicator light is not on.

Therefore, the device is not functioning.

This conclusion follows only if the original conditional premise is accepted.

## 4. Common Logical Fallacies

A fallacy is an error in reasoning that weakens an argument.

### 4.1 Ad Hominem

Attacking the person presenting an argument rather than addressing the argument itself.

Example:

"Your conclusion about climate policy is wrong because you are inexperienced."

### 4.2 Straw Man

Misrepresenting another person's argument to make it easier to attack.

Example:

Person A: "We should reduce unnecessary spending."

Person B: "You want to eliminate all public services."

### 4.3 False Dilemma

Presenting only two options when additional possibilities exist.

Example:

"Either you agree with me completely or you oppose progress."

### 4.4 Hasty Generalization

Drawing a broad conclusion from insufficient evidence.

Example:

"I met two dishonest people from this town, so everyone there must be dishonest."

### 4.5 Appeal to Authority

Treating an authority's statement as conclusive proof without examining relevant evidence.

Expert testimony can be valuable, but authority alone does not guarantee truth.

### 4.6 Circular Reasoning

Using a conclusion as one of the premises that supposedly proves it.

Example:

"This rule is correct because it is the right rule."

## 5. Formal and Informal Fallacies

| Formal fallacy | Informal fallacy |
|---|---|
| Involves an invalid logical structure | Involves a problem with content, relevance, or reasoning |
| Can often be identified through logical form | Requires examination of context and meaning |
| Example: affirming the consequent | Example: ad hominem |

## 6. Evaluating Arguments

A systematic approach to evaluating an argument involves:

1. Identify the conclusion.
2. Identify the premises.
3. Examine whether the premises are relevant.
4. Check the logical structure.
5. Evaluate the evidence.
6. Consider alternative explanations.
7. Determine whether the conclusion is justified.

## 7. Critical Thinking in Everyday Life

Logical reasoning is useful in:
- Scientific research.
- News evaluation.
- Political discussion.
- Academic writing.
- Personal decision-making.
- Evaluating advertisements.
- Online discussions.

The purpose of logic is not simply to win arguments but to improve the quality of reasoning.

## 8. Summary

- Arguments contain premises and conclusions.
- Deductive reasoning aims at necessary conclusions.
- Inductive reasoning supports probable conclusions.
- Formal logic studies argument structures.
- Modus ponens and modus tollens are valid inference forms.
- Logical fallacies weaken arguments.
- Critical thinking requires examining both evidence and reasoning.
