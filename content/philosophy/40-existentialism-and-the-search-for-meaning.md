# Lesson 40: Existentialism and the Search for Meaning

Existentialism is a philosophical tradition concerned with human existence, freedom, responsibility, authenticity and the search for meaning. It examines what it means to live as an individual in a world where certainty, predetermined purpose and universal answers may be questioned. Existentialist thinkers explore how individuals create, discover or confront meaning through their choices and actions.

## 1. Meaning of Existentialism

Existentialism focuses on the lived experience of individual human beings.

Its central questions include:

- What does it mean to exist as a human being?
- Is life inherently meaningful?
- Are individuals genuinely free?
- How should people respond to uncertainty?
- What does it mean to live authentically?
- Can meaning be created through personal choices?

Existentialist philosophy is not a single unified doctrine. Different thinkers offer distinct accounts of freedom, existence, religion and meaning.

## 2. Existence and Essence

One of the most influential existentialist ideas is the distinction between existence and essence.

Jean-Paul Sartre expressed this through the principle:

"Existence precedes essence."

The principle is particularly associated with his account of human freedom.

It suggests that human beings do not arrive with a completely predetermined purpose or fixed identity.

Instead, individuals shape their identities through choices, actions and commitments.

For example, a person is not necessarily defined by a predetermined social role. Their identity develops through how they choose to live.

## 3. Søren Kierkegaard and Individual Existence

Søren Kierkegaard is an important precursor to existentialism.

He emphasised:

- Individual subjectivity.
- Personal commitment.
- Anxiety.
- Faith.
- The difficulty of making meaningful choices.

Kierkegaard criticised systems of thought that attempted to explain human existence entirely through abstract reasoning.

He argued that becoming an individual requires personal commitment rather than merely accepting established ideas.

### 3.1 The Stages of Life

Kierkegaard described three influential modes or stages of existence.

**Aesthetic stage:** Life is organised around immediate enjoyment, pleasure and interesting experiences.

**Ethical stage:** Life becomes organised around responsibility, commitment and moral obligations.

**Religious stage:** The individual develops a personal relationship with faith and religious commitment.

These stages represent different ways of relating to existence rather than simple chronological periods.

## 4. Friedrich Nietzsche and the Revaluation of Values

Friedrich Nietzsche challenged traditional moral and religious assumptions.

His philosophy examines the origins of values and the possibility of creating new ways of evaluating life.

### 4.1 "God Is Dead"

Nietzsche's statement "God is dead" is a philosophical diagnosis of the decline of traditional religious authority in modern European culture.

It does not simply describe a physical event.

Nietzsche was concerned that established religious and metaphysical frameworks were losing their ability to provide universally accepted meaning.

### 4.2 Nihilism

Nihilism refers broadly to the rejection or loss of belief in established meanings, values or foundations.

Nietzsche examined the danger of nihilism when traditional values lose their authority without new values being developed.

### 4.3 Will to Power

Nietzsche used the concept of will to power to describe a fundamental tendency towards expansion, interpretation, overcoming and the expression of strength.

Its precise meaning is debated and should not be reduced to a simple desire for political control.

### 4.4 Übermensch

The Übermensch, often translated as overman or superman, is an ideal associated with the creation and transformation of values.

It represents the possibility of overcoming inherited assumptions and affirming life through self-transformation.

## 5. Martin Heidegger and Being

Martin Heidegger investigated the meaning of Being in his work *Being and Time*.

He introduced the concept of *Dasein*, referring to the distinctive mode of existence of human beings as beings who understand and question their own existence.

### 5.1 Being-in-the-World

Heidegger argued that human beings should not be understood as isolated minds observing an external world.

We are already involved in practical relationships with people, objects, activities and environments.

This is described as being-in-the-world.

### 5.2 Being-Towards-Death

Heidegger examined how awareness of mortality influences human existence.

Recognising that life is finite can encourage individuals to confront their possibilities and take responsibility for their own lives.

## 6. Jean-Paul Sartre and Radical Freedom

Jean-Paul Sartre developed an influential secular existentialist philosophy.

He argued that human beings are fundamentally free in the sense that they must continually choose how to act within their circumstances.

Freedom is connected with responsibility because individuals cannot entirely escape the significance of their choices.

### 6.1 Bad Faith

Bad faith occurs when individuals deceive themselves about their freedom and responsibility.

For example, someone may claim that they have no choice in a situation when they are actually avoiding responsibility for the choices available to them.

Sartre used bad faith to examine how people hide from the uncertainty and responsibility associated with freedom.

### 6.2 Responsibility

For Sartre, choosing is not merely selecting between available options.

Our actions also express values and contribute to the kind of person we become.

This gives human freedom a serious ethical dimension.

## 7. Albert Camus and the Absurd

Albert Camus is frequently associated with existentialist themes, although he rejected the existentialist label.

His philosophy of the absurd examines the conflict between the human desire for meaning and the apparent indifference or silence of the universe.

### 7.1 The Absurd

The absurd arises from the relationship between:

- Human beings seeking meaning, order and explanation.
- A universe that does not provide an obvious ultimate answer.

The absurd is not simply a claim that life is meaningless.

It describes the tension between the human search for meaning and the absence of a guaranteed cosmic explanation.

### 7.2 Revolt

Camus argued that individuals should confront the absurd without escaping into false certainty or surrendering to despair.

He emphasised revolt, freedom and passionate engagement with life.

### 7.3 The Myth of Sisyphus

Camus used the Greek myth of Sisyphus to explore the absurd.

Sisyphus is condemned to repeatedly push a boulder up a mountain, only for it to roll back down.

The repetitive and apparently pointless task represents the human confrontation with an indifferent universe.

Camus concludes that Sisyphus must be imagined as capable of affirming his existence despite the absurdity of his situation.

## 8. Anxiety, Anguish and Authenticity

Existentialist philosophy examines several important aspects of human experience.

### 8.1 Anxiety

Anxiety can arise from uncertainty, freedom and awareness of possible futures.

Unlike fear, which generally concerns a particular object or threat, existential anxiety may concern existence itself.

### 8.2 Anguish

Anguish is associated with the recognition that individuals are responsible for their choices.

It can arise from awareness that choices have consequences and cannot always be justified through external authority.

### 8.3 Authenticity

Authenticity involves confronting one's existence honestly rather than simply adopting social expectations or avoiding responsibility.

It does not necessarily mean rejecting society or refusing to participate in ordinary life.

## 9. Existentialism and Religion

Existentialist thinkers differ in their approaches to religion.

**Religious existentialism** emphasises personal faith, commitment and the individual's relationship with the divine.

**Atheistic existentialism** explores freedom and meaning without relying on the existence of God.

Kierkegaard is associated with religious existentialist themes, while Sartre developed an influential atheistic existentialism.

## 10. Existentialism and Nihilism

Existentialism and nihilism are related but distinct.

Nihilism may involve rejecting the existence of objective or predetermined meaning.

Existentialism examines how individuals respond to the absence or uncertainty of predetermined meaning through freedom, commitment and action.

Some existentialist thinkers reject nihilistic resignation and emphasise the possibility of meaningful human engagement.

## 11. Applications of Existentialist Philosophy

Existentialist ideas influence:

- Literature and cinema.
- Psychology and psychotherapy.
- Personal identity.
- Moral responsibility.
- Political freedom.
- Art and creativity.
- Discussions about meaning and mortality.

Existentialist philosophy encourages individuals to examine their values and take responsibility for how they live.

## 12. Summary

Existentialism investigates freedom, existence, authenticity, responsibility and meaning.

Kierkegaard emphasised individual commitment and faith. Nietzsche examined the decline of traditional values and the creation of new ones. Heidegger investigated Being and mortality. Sartre developed radical freedom and bad faith, while Camus examined the absurd and the possibility of revolt.

Existentialism presents human life as an ongoing process of confronting uncertainty and taking responsibility for one's existence.
