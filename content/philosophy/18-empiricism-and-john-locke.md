# Lesson 18: Empiricism and John Locke

Empiricism is a major philosophical tradition that emphasizes experience as the foundation of knowledge.

Empiricists investigate how sensory perception, observation and experience contribute to the formation of ideas.

John Locke was one of the most influential empiricist philosophers. His work challenged rationalist accounts of innate ideas and helped shape modern epistemology, political philosophy and theories of personal identity.

## 1. Meaning of Empiricism

Empiricism is the philosophical position that knowledge is fundamentally connected to experience.

It emphasizes sources such as:

- Sensory perception.
- Observation.
- Experimentation.
- Reflection on mental experience.

Empiricists generally reject the idea that human beings possess extensive innate knowledge independently of experience.

However, different empiricists disagree about the exact role of reason and the reliability of sensory knowledge.

## 2. Historical Background

Modern empiricism developed during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

Its major representatives included:

- John Locke.
- George Berkeley.
- David Hume.

Their work developed important criticisms of rationalism and explored the limits of human knowledge.

## 3. John Locke: Life and Background

John Locke was born in England in 1632 and died in 1704.

He was a philosopher and political thinker whose work influenced modern epistemology and liberal political philosophy.

His major works include:

- *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*.
- *Two Treatises of Government*.
- *A Letter Concerning Toleration*.

Locke investigated how the human mind acquires ideas and how political authority can be justified.

## 4. The Mind as a Tabula Rasa

Locke rejected the idea that the mind contains fully formed innate ideas from birth.

He compared the mind at the beginning of life to a blank slate.

The Latin expression is *tabula rasa*.

This does not mean that human beings are born without any biological capacities.

It means that the content of knowledge develops through experience rather than being present as an established collection of innate ideas.

## 5. Two Sources of Ideas

Locke identified two major sources of ideas:

### 5.1 Sensation

Sensation occurs when external objects affect the senses.

Examples include:

- Seeing colours.
- Hearing sounds.
- Feeling temperature.
- Tasting food.
- Smelling substances.

These experiences provide ideas about the external world.

### 5.2 Reflection

Reflection occurs when the mind observes its own operations.

Examples include:

- Thinking.
- Doubting.
- Remembering.
- Comparing.
- Believing.
- Willing.

Reflection provides ideas about mental activities.

Together, sensation and reflection form the foundations of Locke's account of knowledge.

## 6. Simple and Complex Ideas

Locke distinguished between simple and complex ideas.

### 6.1 Simple Ideas

Simple ideas are basic mental contents received through sensation or reflection.

Examples:

- Redness.
- Warmth.
- Sweetness.
- Motion.
- Thinking.

The mind receives simple ideas and cannot arbitrarily create entirely new simple ideas independently of experience.

### 6.2 Complex Ideas

Complex ideas are formed when the mind combines, compares or abstracts simple ideas.

For example, the idea of an apple may involve combining experiences of colour, shape, taste and texture.

Locke identified three important operations:

1. Combination.
2. Comparison.
3. Abstraction.

## 7. Primary and Secondary Qualities

Locke distinguished between primary and secondary qualities.

### 7.1 Primary Qualities

Primary qualities are properties that Locke considered inherent in physical objects.

Examples include:

- Shape.
- Extension.
- Motion.
- Number.
- Solidity.

### 7.2 Secondary Qualities

Secondary qualities are powers in objects to produce certain sensations in observers.

Examples include:

- Colour.
- Taste.
- Sound.
- Smell.

For example, an object may possess physical properties that cause an observer to experience a particular colour.

The distinction attempts to explain how the physical properties of objects relate to subjective sensory experiences.

## 8. Locke's Theory of Personal Identity

Locke made an influential contribution to the philosophy of personal identity.

He distinguished between the identity of a substance and the identity of a person.

According to Locke, personal identity is closely connected with consciousness, particularly the ability to recognize past experiences as one's own.

### 8.1 Memory and Consciousness

A person remains the same person across time insofar as consciousness extends backward to past actions and experiences.

This makes psychological continuity important to personal identity.

### 8.2 The Prince and the Cobbler

Locke discusses a hypothetical case involving the consciousness of a prince entering the body of a cobbler.

If the prince's memories and consciousness were transferred into the cobbler's body, which individual would the resulting person be?

Locke's account suggests that personal identity follows consciousness rather than merely the physical body.

This thought experiment raises important questions about memory, identity and responsibility.

## 9. Locke's Political Philosophy

Locke's political philosophy influenced modern liberalism and constitutional government.

His ideas are presented particularly in *Two Treatises of Government*.

### 9.1 Natural Rights

Locke argued that individuals possess natural rights.

Important rights include:

- Life.
- Liberty.
- Property.

These rights are not simply gifts from political rulers.

### 9.2 State of Nature

Locke imagined a state of nature in which individuals exist without an established political government.

He argued that people are naturally free and equal, while also being bound by natural law.

### 9.3 Social Contract

Locke argued that individuals establish political society through consent.

Government exists to protect people's rights and maintain social order.

### 9.4 Right of Resistance

If a government systematically violates the rights it is supposed to protect, Locke argued that people may have a right to resist or replace it.

These ideas influenced later constitutional and democratic thought.

## 10. Locke Compared with Rationalism

Locke rejected the rationalist claim that substantial innate knowledge is necessary to explain human understanding.

However, he did not reject reason.

Instead, he argued that reason operates on ideas acquired through experience.

| Question | Rationalist emphasis | Locke's empiricist emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| Source of ideas | Reason and possibly innate ideas | Sensation and reflection |
| Innate knowledge | Often accepted in some form | Rejected as an established source of ideas |
| Role of reason | Fundamental source of knowledge | Organizes and examines acquired ideas |
| Knowledge | Includes necessary rational truths | Grounded in experience |

## 11. Criticisms of Locke

Locke's philosophy has faced several objections.

### 11.1 The Blank Slate Problem

Critics argue that humans possess biological and cognitive structures that influence how experience is processed.

### 11.2 Limits of Memory

If personal identity depends on memory, what happens when memories are lost or distorted?

### 11.3 Primary and Secondary Qualities

Critics question whether the distinction between primary and secondary qualities can be maintained as sharply as Locke proposed.

### 11.4 The Origins of Abstract Ideas

The process by which complex abstract ideas emerge from simple sensory experiences requires further explanation.

## 12. Contemporary Relevance

Locke's empiricism remains relevant to:

- Scientific investigation.
- Educational theory.
- Cognitive development.
- Political rights.
- Personal identity.
- The philosophy of perception.

His emphasis on experience also influenced later approaches to psychology and the scientific study of learning.

## 13. Summary

- Empiricism emphasizes experience as the foundation of knowledge.
- John Locke rejected the theory of innate ideas.
- Tabula rasa describes the mind as initially lacking established ideas.
- Sensation and reflection are the two major sources of ideas.
- Simple ideas are received through experience, while complex ideas are constructed by the mind.
- Locke distinguished primary and secondary qualities.
- His theory of personal identity emphasizes consciousness and memory.
- His political philosophy emphasizes natural rights, consent and limited government.
