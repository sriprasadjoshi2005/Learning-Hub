# Lesson 37: Justice, Equality and Distributive Ethics

Justice is a central concept in political and moral philosophy. It concerns fairness, rights, responsibilities and the distribution of social benefits and burdens. Philosophers have developed different theories to explain what a just society should look like and how resources, opportunities and freedoms should be distributed.

## 1. Meaning of Justice

Justice concerns the fair treatment of individuals and the proper organisation of social relationships.

It raises questions such as:

- What does each individual deserve?
- Should everyone receive equal resources?
- Should rewards depend on effort or contribution?
- How should society treat disadvantaged groups?
- What makes laws and institutions fair?

Justice involves both individual conduct and the structure of social institutions.

## 2. Major Forms of Justice

### 2.1 Distributive Justice

Distributive justice concerns how benefits, opportunities, resources and burdens should be allocated within society.

Examples include:

- Distribution of healthcare.
- Access to education.
- Taxation.
- Public resources.
- Employment opportunities.

### 2.2 Retributive Justice

Retributive justice concerns appropriate punishment for wrongdoing.

It asks whether punishment is deserved and how its severity should relate to the offence.

### 2.3 Restorative Justice

Restorative justice focuses on repairing harm and rebuilding relationships between affected individuals and communities.

It may involve:

- Acknowledgement of wrongdoing.
- Dialogue.
- Compensation.
- Reconciliation.
- Rehabilitation.

### 2.4 Procedural Justice

Procedural justice concerns the fairness of decision-making processes.

A fair outcome requires not only appropriate results but also fair procedures.

Examples include impartial hearings, consistent rules and opportunities to present evidence.

## 3. Equality

Equality concerns how individuals should be treated in relation to rights, opportunities, resources and social status.

Different forms include:

### 3.1 Formal Equality

Formal equality requires applying the same rules and standards to individuals in comparable circumstances.

### 3.2 Equality of Opportunity

Equality of opportunity aims to ensure that individuals have fair access to important opportunities regardless of irrelevant background factors.

### 3.3 Equality of Outcome

Equality of outcome focuses on reducing differences in the final distribution of resources, benefits or social conditions.

### 3.4 Substantive Equality

Substantive equality recognises that identical treatment may not always produce genuinely fair opportunities.

It considers structural disadvantages and the different circumstances of individuals.

## 4. John Rawls and Justice as Fairness

John Rawls developed one of the most influential theories of distributive justice in his work *A Theory of Justice*.

He proposed that principles of justice should be selected through a hypothetical decision-making situation called the original position.

### 4.1 The Original Position

The original position is a hypothetical situation in which individuals choose the basic principles governing society.

They are assumed to be rational and concerned with securing fair conditions for themselves.

### 4.2 The Veil of Ignorance

Rawls introduced the veil of ignorance to ensure impartiality.

Individuals choosing principles of justice do not know their:

- Social class.
- Wealth.
- Gender.
- Natural talents.
- Ethnic background.
- Religious beliefs.
- Position in society.

Because nobody knows which position they will occupy, they are encouraged to choose principles that protect individuals fairly.

## 5. Rawls's Principles of Justice

Rawls proposed two major principles.

### 5.1 Equal Basic Liberties

Each person should possess an equal claim to a fully adequate scheme of basic liberties compatible with the same scheme for everyone.

Examples include:

- Freedom of conscience.
- Freedom of thought.
- Political liberty.
- Freedom of association.
- Protection under the rule of law.

### 5.2 The Difference Principle and Fair Equality of Opportunity

Rawls argued that social and economic inequalities must satisfy demanding conditions.

They must:

1. Be attached to positions open under conditions of fair equality of opportunity.
2. Work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged members of society.

The difference principle does not require every individual to receive exactly the same amount.

It permits inequalities when they improve the position of those who are least advantaged, subject to Rawls's broader principles of justice.

## 6. Utilitarianism and Justice

Utilitarianism evaluates social arrangements according to their overall contribution to well-being.

This may support policies that produce substantial benefits for a large population.

However, critics argue that maximising total welfare may sometimes permit unfair treatment of minorities or individuals.

Rawls therefore rejected the idea that basic rights should simply be sacrificed whenever doing so increases aggregate social welfare.

## 7. Libertarian Theories of Justice

Libertarian theories emphasise individual liberty, property rights and voluntary exchange.

Robert Nozick developed an influential entitlement theory of justice.

According to Nozick, the justice of a distribution depends on:

- Just acquisition.
- Just transfer.
- Rectification of past injustice.

A distribution does not automatically become unjust merely because individuals possess unequal amounts of wealth.

The historical process through which possessions were acquired and transferred is central.

## 8. Amartya Sen and the Capability Approach

Amartya Sen developed an influential approach that evaluates justice through people's real opportunities to achieve valuable ways of living.

The capability approach distinguishes between:

- Resources: What individuals possess.
- Functionings: What individuals actually manage to be or do.
- Capabilities: The genuine opportunities available to them.

For example, two individuals may receive identical educational resources but have different abilities to benefit from them because of health, accessibility or social circumstances.

The capability approach therefore focuses on real freedom rather than resources alone.

## 9. Equality, Equity and Fairness

Equality and equity are related but distinct ideas.

**Equality** generally concerns providing equal treatment, rights or resources.

**Equity** concerns adjusting treatment or support to account for relevant differences in circumstances.

For example, providing additional accessibility support to an individual with a disability may be necessary to create a genuinely fair opportunity.

## 10. Applications of Distributive Justice

Theories of justice influence debates concerning:

- Taxation.
- Public healthcare.
- Education.
- Social welfare.
- Economic inequality.
- Employment.
- Environmental resources.
- Digital access.
- Political representation.

## 11. Summary

Justice concerns fairness in individual relationships and social institutions.

Major forms include distributive, retributive, restorative and procedural justice.

Rawls developed justice as fairness through the original position, veil of ignorance and two principles of justice. Nozick defended an entitlement-based approach, while Amartya Sen emphasised capabilities and real opportunities.
