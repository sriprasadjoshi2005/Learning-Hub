# Lesson 12: Socrates and the Socratic Method

Socrates was one of the most influential philosophers of ancient Greece. His philosophy focused on ethical questions, self-examination, knowledge and the meaning of a good life.

Rather than presenting a large collection of written doctrines, Socrates became known for questioning people, examining their beliefs and exposing contradictions in their reasoning.

His approach transformed philosophical inquiry into a disciplined process of dialogue and critical examination.

## 1. Life and Historical Background

Socrates was born in Athens around 470 BCE and died in 399 BCE.

He lived during a period of significant political, cultural and intellectual activity in ancient Greece.

Athens was an important centre of philosophy, literature, politics and public debate.

Socrates regularly engaged citizens in discussions about:

- Justice.
- Courage.
- Virtue.
- Knowledge.
- Piety.
- Wisdom.
- The good life.

He did not establish a formal philosophical school in the same manner as Plato or Aristotle.

## 2. Socrates and Written Philosophy

Socrates did not leave behind writings of his own.

Most knowledge about his life and philosophy comes from other ancient sources, particularly:

- Plato.
- Xenophon.
- Aristophanes.

These sources do not always portray him in exactly the same way.

Plato's dialogues are especially important, but historians distinguish between the historical Socrates and the philosophical character presented in Plato's works.

This creates what is known as the Socratic problem: determining which philosophical ideas can confidently be attributed to the historical Socrates.

## 3. The Socratic Method

The Socratic method is a form of philosophical dialogue in which questions are used to examine beliefs and clarify concepts.

Instead of simply providing definitions, Socrates encouraged individuals to defend their answers through reasoning.

A typical discussion involves:

1. Asking a fundamental question.
2. Receiving an initial definition.
3. Examining the definition through further questions.
4. Identifying contradictions or weaknesses.
5. Revising the original definition.
6. Continuing the investigation.

The purpose is not merely to win an argument. It is to improve understanding.

## 4. Elenchus: Socratic Examination

The term *elenchus* refers to the process of examining or refuting a person's position through questioning.

Consider the question:

"What is courage?"

A person might answer:

"Courage means never feeling afraid."

Socrates could ask:

- Can someone feel fear and still act courageously?
- Is a person who takes unnecessary risks courageous?
- Does courage require understanding the danger?
- Can someone act without fear because they do not understand the risk?

These questions reveal that the original definition may be inadequate.

A more refined understanding might be that courage involves responding appropriately to fear and danger.

## 5. Socratic Ignorance

Socrates is associated with the idea that recognizing one's ignorance is an important step toward wisdom.

This does not necessarily mean that he believed he knew absolutely nothing.

Instead, he emphasized the importance of recognizing the limitations of one's knowledge.

A person who assumes they already possess complete knowledge may stop questioning and learning.

A person who recognizes uncertainty remains open to investigation.

This idea is closely connected with intellectual humility.

## 6. The Examined Life

Socrates considered self-examination essential to a meaningful human life.

He believed individuals should investigate their beliefs, values and actions rather than living without reflection.

The examined life involves questions such as:

- Why do I believe what I believe?
- Are my actions consistent with my values?
- What does it mean to live well?
- Am I pursuing things that genuinely matter?
- What makes an action morally good?

For Socrates, philosophical reflection was not simply an academic activity. It was a way of living.

## 7. Socrates and Virtue

Socrates gave central importance to virtue.

Virtue refers to qualities that contribute to moral excellence and good character.

Important virtues included:

- Wisdom.
- Courage.
- Justice.
- Moderation.

### 7.1 Virtue and Knowledge

Socrates is associated with the idea that virtue is closely connected with knowledge.

He suggested that wrongdoing is often connected with ignorance or misunderstanding of what is genuinely good.

If individuals truly understood what was good for them, they would be more likely to act accordingly.

This position is sometimes described as Socratic intellectualism.

### 7.2 Can Knowledge Guarantee Good Behaviour?

The relationship between knowledge and moral behaviour remains philosophically controversial.

People sometimes recognize what is right but still act against their own judgment.

This issue is later explored in discussions of akrasia, or weakness of will.

## 8. Socrates and the Trial of Athens

In 399 BCE, Socrates was tried in Athens.

He faced accusations involving:

- Impiety.
- Introducing unfamiliar spiritual influences.
- Corrupting the youth.

He was found guilty and sentenced to death.

According to Plato's account in the *Phaedo*, Socrates accepted the sentence and died after drinking a preparation containing hemlock.

His trial raises important questions concerning:

- Freedom of thought.
- The authority of the state.
- Individual conscience.
- Religious and political conformity.
- The relationship between law and justice.

## 9. Important Socratic Dialogues

Several dialogues associated with Plato portray Socrates examining philosophical questions.

| Dialogue | Major concern |
|---|---|
| Euthyphro | Piety and moral definition |
| Apology | Socrates' defence and philosophical mission |
| Crito | Justice and obedience to law |
| Meno | Virtue and whether it can be taught |
| Phaedo | The soul and death |
| Republic | Justice and the ideal state |

These works are important sources for understanding Socratic philosophy and its development in Plato's thought.

## 10. Contemporary Relevance

The Socratic method remains useful in education, research and everyday reasoning.

It encourages individuals to:

- Ask precise questions.
- Examine assumptions.
- Recognize contradictions.
- Distinguish knowledge from opinion.
- Develop independent reasoning.
- Avoid accepting claims without justification.

It is particularly useful when discussing complicated or controversial issues.

## 11. Summary

- Socrates was an influential ancient Greek philosopher.
- He did not leave behind written philosophical works.
- His ideas are known through accounts by other ancient writers.
- The Socratic method uses questioning to examine beliefs.
- Elenchus involves testing claims and exposing contradictions.
- Socratic ignorance emphasizes recognizing the limits of knowledge.
- Socrates connected virtue with knowledge.
- His trial and death raised important questions about justice and freedom of thought.
