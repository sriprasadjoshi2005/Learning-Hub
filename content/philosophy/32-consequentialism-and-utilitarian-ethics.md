# Lesson 32: Consequentialism and Utilitarian Ethics

Consequentialism is a family of ethical theories that evaluates actions primarily through their consequences. Utilitarianism is one of its most influential forms, maintaining that actions should be assessed according to their contribution to overall well-being. These theories provide systematic approaches to moral decision-making, particularly when actions affect multiple individuals.

## 1. Meaning of Consequentialism

Consequentialism maintains that the moral status of an action depends on the consequences it produces.

An action is generally considered morally right when it produces better overall outcomes than the available alternatives.

For example, a public health policy may be evaluated by examining its effects on:

- Human health.
- Individual freedom.
- Economic resources.
- Social well-being.
- Long-term consequences.

Consequentialist reasoning focuses on outcomes rather than treating rules or intentions as the only determinants of morality.

## 2. Utilitarianism

Utilitarianism is a consequentialist ethical theory that evaluates actions according to their contribution to overall happiness or well-being.

Its central principle is commonly expressed as:

\[
\text{Greatest happiness for the greatest number}
\]

An action is morally preferable when it produces greater overall well-being than the alternatives.

Classical utilitarianism is particularly associated with Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill.

## 3. Jeremy Bentham and Quantitative Utilitarianism

Jeremy Bentham developed an influential form of classical utilitarianism.

He argued that pleasure and pain are central to evaluating human actions.

### 3.1 The Hedonic Calculus

Bentham proposed a method for evaluating pleasures and pains through several dimensions.

These include:

1. Intensity: How strong is the pleasure or pain?
2. Duration: How long does it last?
3. Certainty: How likely is it to occur?
4. Propinquity: How soon will it occur?
5. Fecundity: How likely is it to produce further pleasures?
6. Purity: How free is it from accompanying pain?
7. Extent: How many individuals are affected?

The hedonic calculus was intended to provide a systematic framework for comparing consequences.

### 3.2 Example

Suppose a government must choose between two public programmes.

Programme A provides a small benefit to a very large population.

Programme B provides a substantial benefit to a smaller population.

A Benthamite analysis would attempt to compare the total expected pleasure and pain associated with each programme.

The morally preferable choice would be the one producing the greatest overall balance of pleasure over pain.

## 4. John Stuart Mill and Qualitative Utilitarianism

John Stuart Mill developed utilitarianism further by distinguishing between higher and lower pleasures.

**Higher pleasures** involve intellectual, emotional and reflective activities.

Examples include:

- Learning.
- Artistic appreciation.
- Philosophical inquiry.
- Meaningful relationships.

**Lower pleasures** are more closely associated with immediate physical satisfaction.

Mill argued that the quality of pleasure matters, not merely its quantity.

His approach therefore differs from a purely quantitative calculation of happiness.

## 5. Act and Rule Utilitarianism

### 5.1 Act Utilitarianism

Act utilitarianism evaluates each individual action according to its expected consequences.

A particular action is morally right if it produces at least as much overall well-being as the available alternatives.

For example, an act utilitarian may support breaking a minor promise if doing so prevents serious harm and produces a substantially better outcome.

### 5.2 Rule Utilitarianism

Rule utilitarianism evaluates moral rules according to the consequences of generally following them.

A rule is morally justified when its widespread acceptance tends to produce greater overall well-being.

For example, a general rule against dishonesty may create trust and cooperation, even when an isolated lie could produce a beneficial outcome.

| Act utilitarianism | Rule utilitarianism |
|---|---|
| Evaluates individual actions. | Evaluates general rules. |
| Focuses on the consequences of a particular decision. | Focuses on the consequences of following a rule. |
| Can permit exceptions whenever they improve outcomes. | Gives importance to stable rules and social expectations. |

## 6. Preference Utilitarianism

Preference utilitarianism evaluates outcomes according to the satisfaction of informed preferences rather than pleasure alone.

It asks whether an action fulfils the interests and preferences of those affected.

This approach is associated with contemporary utilitarian discussions, including the work of Peter Singer.

It raises questions about:

- Conflicting preferences.
- Irrational or uninformed desires.
- The interests of animals.
- The distinction between preference satisfaction and well-being.

## 7. Strengths of Utilitarianism

Utilitarianism offers several important advantages.

- It considers the welfare of everyone affected.
- It provides a systematic approach to difficult decisions.
- It can support policies that reduce suffering.
- It does not automatically privilege one person's interests over another's.
- It encourages consideration of long-term consequences.

## 8. Criticisms of Utilitarianism

### 8.1 The Problem of Justice

A decision that maximises total happiness may violate the rights of an innocent individual.

For example, punishing an innocent person could theoretically satisfy an angry population, but doing so would violate fundamental principles of justice.

### 8.2 The Problem of Measurement

Happiness, suffering and preferences are difficult to measure objectively.

Different individuals may experience the same situation very differently.

### 8.3 The Problem of Prediction

Consequences are not always predictable.

A decision that appears beneficial in the short term may produce serious long-term harm.

### 8.4 Excessive Demands

Utilitarianism may appear to require individuals to sacrifice a large proportion of their personal resources whenever doing so would improve overall welfare.

Critics argue that this can place unrealistic demands on ordinary moral agents.

## 9. Applications of Utilitarian Reasoning

Utilitarian approaches are used in discussions concerning:

- Public health policy.
- Environmental protection.
- Resource allocation.
- Animal welfare.
- Economic decisions.
- Technology regulation.
- Disaster management.

Cost-benefit analysis shares some similarities with consequentialist reasoning, although economic calculations and moral utilitarianism are not identical.

## 10. Summary

Consequentialism evaluates actions according to their outcomes. Utilitarianism is a major consequentialist theory that seeks to maximise overall happiness or well-being.

Bentham developed quantitative approaches to pleasure and pain, while Mill distinguished higher and lower pleasures. Act, rule and preference utilitarianism offer different methods for evaluating consequences.

Despite its practical strengths, utilitarianism faces challenges involving justice, measurement, prediction and individual rights.
