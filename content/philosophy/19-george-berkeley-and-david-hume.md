# Lesson 19: George Berkeley and David Hume

George Berkeley and David Hume were major philosophers of British empiricism.

Both developed important criticisms of assumptions about reality and knowledge.

Berkeley questioned the existence of matter independently of perception, while Hume investigated the limits of causation, induction, personal identity and human understanding.

Their work helped transform modern epistemology and metaphysics.

## 1. George Berkeley

George Berkeley was born in Ireland in 1685 and died in 1753.

He was a philosopher and bishop who developed a philosophical position known as immaterialism or subjective idealism.

His major philosophical works include:

- *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge*.
- *Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous*.

Berkeley questioned whether material objects exist independently of being perceived.

## 2. Berkeley's Immaterialism

Berkeley rejected the idea that matter exists as an independently existing substance outside all perception.

He argued that what we call physical objects are collections of ideas perceived by minds.

His philosophy is commonly summarized through the Latin expression:

\[
\text{Esse est percipi}
\]

Meaning:

"To be is to be perceived."

The expression applies to sensible objects, not to perceiving minds.

## 3. Ideas and Spirits

Berkeley distinguished between two fundamental categories.

### 3.1 Ideas

Ideas are passive contents of perception.

Examples include:

- Colours.
- Sounds.
- Shapes.
- Textures.
- Tastes.

Ideas are experienced rather than actively producing themselves.

### 3.2 Spirits

Spirits are active perceiving and thinking beings.

They possess the capacity to:

- Perceive.
- Think.
- Will.
- Remember.
- Imagine.

Human minds and God are understood as spirits in Berkeley's philosophical system.

## 4. The Problem of Material Substance

Berkeley criticized the concept of matter as an underlying substance existing independently of perception.

He argued that we never directly experience such an invisible material substratum.

We experience colours, shapes, textures and other sensory ideas.

Therefore, he questioned whether it is justified to assume an additional material substance behind those ideas.

## 5. The Role of God

If objects exist only when perceived, what happens when no human being is observing them?

Berkeley answered by arguing that God continuously perceives everything.

This provides an explanation for the continuity and stability of the world even when individual humans are not observing particular objects.

For Berkeley, the regularity of nature is connected with divine activity.

## 6. Berkeley and Primary and Secondary Qualities

Berkeley rejected Locke's distinction between primary and secondary qualities.

He argued that both depend on perception.

For example:

- Colour changes depending on lighting and visual conditions.
- Apparent size changes with distance.
- Shape may appear different from different viewpoints.

Berkeley used such considerations to question the claim that primary qualities exist independently of perception in the manner proposed by Locke.

## 7. Criticisms of Berkeley

Berkeley's immaterialism faces several objections.

### 7.1 Dependence on Divine Perception

His account relies on the existence of God as the continuous perceiver of objects.

Philosophers who reject this theological assumption may not accept the explanation.

### 7.2 The External World

Critics argue that the regularity and independence of the physical world suggest that objects exist independently of individual perception.

### 7.3 Scientific Explanation

Some critics argue that scientific theories require a stable account of physical structures beyond immediate sensory experiences.

Berkeley's philosophy attempts to reinterpret these structures rather than accepting independent material substance.

## 8. David Hume

David Hume was born in Scotland in 1711 and died in 1776.

He was a major empiricist philosopher whose work influenced epistemology, metaphysics, ethics and the philosophy of science.

His major works include:

- *A Treatise of Human Nature*.
- *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*.
- *An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*.

Hume investigated the limits of human knowledge and the origins of human beliefs.

## 9. Impressions and Ideas

Hume distinguished between impressions and ideas.

### 9.1 Impressions

Impressions are vivid and immediate experiences.

Examples include:

- Feeling pain.
- Seeing a bright light.
- Hearing a loud sound.
- Experiencing fear.
- Feeling happiness.

### 9.2 Ideas

Ideas are less vivid representations of impressions.

Examples include:

- Remembering a painful event.
- Imagining a bright light.
- Thinking about a sound heard earlier.

Hume argued that simple ideas are generally derived from corresponding impressions.

This is associated with his copy principle.

## 10. Hume's Association of Ideas

Hume proposed that ideas are connected through recurring patterns.

Three important principles of association are:

### 10.1 Resemblance

One idea reminds us of another because they are similar.

Example: A photograph reminds us of the person depicted.

### 10.2 Contiguity

Ideas become associated because their objects occur near one another in time or space.

Example: Thinking of a classroom may remind someone of a particular teacher.

### 10.3 Cause and Effect

Ideas become associated through experiences of events that regularly occur together.

Example: Seeing smoke may lead someone to think of fire.

These principles help explain how thoughts move from one idea to another.

## 11. Hume's Problem of Causation

Hume questioned whether we directly perceive necessary causal connections.

Consider two events:

- A billiard ball strikes another ball.
- The second ball moves.

We observe the first event followed by the second.

However, Hume argued that we do not directly perceive an invisible necessary connection between them.

Instead, repeated experiences create an expectation that similar events will follow similar patterns.

### 11.1 Constant Conjunction

Constant conjunction refers to the repeated observation of two types of events occurring together in a regular sequence.

Through repeated experience, the mind develops an expectation of one event following another.

### 11.2 Habit and Custom

Hume argued that belief in causation is strongly connected with habit or custom.

When events repeatedly occur together, we become accustomed to expecting one after the other.

This expectation is psychologically powerful, but it does not establish a logically necessary connection through observation alone.

## 12. The Problem of Induction

Hume raised a major problem concerning the justification of inductive reasoning.

Induction assumes that observed patterns will continue under similar circumstances.

For example:

- The Sun has risen every previous day.
- Therefore, the Sun will rise tomorrow.

The conclusion is reasonable and practically useful.

However, it is not logically guaranteed by the past observations.

Hume argued that we cannot justify induction simply by assuming that nature will remain uniform, because that assumption itself depends on inductive reasoning.

This creates a philosophical difficulty.

## 13. Hume's Bundle Theory of the Self

Hume questioned the idea of a permanent and unchanging self.

When examining inner experience, he found particular perceptions such as:

- Thoughts.
- Emotions.
- Memories.
- Sensations.
- Desires.

He argued that we do not directly experience a separate permanent self existing independently of these perceptions.

This position is commonly called the bundle theory of the self.

The self is understood as a collection or bundle of changing perceptions connected through memory and imagination.

## 14. Hume's Skepticism

Hume questioned whether human reason can establish certainty in many areas.

He examined:

- Causation.
- Induction.
- External reality.
- Personal identity.
- Religious arguments.

However, his skepticism was not necessarily a recommendation to abandon everyday reasoning.

He recognized that human beings naturally rely on experience, habit and practical expectations.

His philosophy examines the limits of these beliefs rather than claiming that ordinary life is impossible.

## 15. Berkeley and Hume Compared

| Feature | Berkeley | Hume |
|---|---|---|
| Main concern | Perception and material substance | Knowledge, causation and human understanding |
| View of ideas | Passive contents of perception | Representations derived from impressions |
| External reality | Objects are collections of perceived ideas | The justification of beliefs about external reality is questioned |
| Role of God | Central to explaining continuous perception | Examines religious arguments critically |
| Major contribution | Immaterialism | Problem of induction and bundle theory |

## 16. Contemporary Relevance

Berkeley and Hume remain relevant to:

- Philosophy of perception.
- Artificial intelligence.
- Scientific explanation.
- Causal inference.
- Personal identity.
- Skepticism.
- The philosophy of science.

Their work encourages us to distinguish what we directly experience from what we infer or assume.

## 17. Summary

- Berkeley developed immaterialism, questioning matter as an independently existing substance.
- His philosophy distinguishes passive ideas from active spirits.
- Berkeley argued that God continuously perceives objects.
- Hume distinguished vivid impressions from less vivid ideas.
- Hume explained associations through resemblance, contiguity and cause and effect.
- His analysis of causation emphasized constant conjunction and habit.
- The problem of induction questions how past observations justify future predictions.
- Hume's bundle theory describes the self as a collection of changing perceptions.
