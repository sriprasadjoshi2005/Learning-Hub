# Lesson 39: Philosophy of Religion and Arguments for God's Existence

Philosophy of religion investigates the nature, rationality and implications of religious beliefs. It examines arguments concerning the existence of God, the problem of evil, religious experience, faith, miracles and the relationship between religion and reason. Its purpose is philosophical analysis rather than the promotion or rejection of any particular religious tradition.

## 1. Meaning of Philosophy of Religion

Philosophy of religion critically examines religious concepts and claims through logical reasoning and philosophical argument.

Its central questions include:

- Does God exist?
- Can God's existence be demonstrated through reason?
- Why does suffering exist?
- What is the relationship between faith and evidence?
- Can religious experiences provide knowledge?
- Are miracles possible?
- What is the nature of divine attributes?

Philosophy of religion can examine theistic, atheistic, agnostic and non-theistic positions.

## 2. Theism, Atheism and Agnosticism

### 2.1 Theism

Theism is the belief in the existence of at least one deity.

Classical theism commonly describes God as omnipotent, omniscient and perfectly good.

### 2.2 Atheism

Atheism generally refers to the absence of belief in God or the belief that no God exists.

Different forms of atheism make different claims about the strength of available arguments and evidence.

### 2.3 Agnosticism

Agnosticism concerns the limits of knowledge regarding divine existence.

An agnostic position may maintain that God's existence is unknown or cannot currently be established.

These positions are not always mutually exclusive in every philosophical classification because belief and knowledge are distinct concepts.

## 3. The Cosmological Argument

The cosmological argument attempts to establish the existence of a necessary or first cause by reasoning from the existence of the universe.

A simplified version proceeds as follows:

1. Things that begin to exist have causes.
2. The universe began to exist.
3. Therefore, the universe has a cause.

Some versions argue that the ultimate explanation must be a necessary being rather than another contingent entity.

### 3.1 Thomas Aquinas

Thomas Aquinas developed influential cosmological arguments in his *Five Ways*.

His arguments include reasoning from:

- Motion or change.
- Efficient causation.
- Contingency and necessity.
- Degrees of perfection.
- The apparent direction or order of natural things.

Aquinas argued that the chain of dependent explanations ultimately requires a first principle.

### 3.2 Criticisms

Critics raise questions such as:

- Must every event have a cause?
- Can the universe itself be necessary?
- Is an infinite chain of causes impossible?
- Does identifying a first cause establish the existence of a personal God?

## 4. The Ontological Argument

The ontological argument attempts to establish God's existence through conceptual or logical reasoning rather than direct empirical observation.

### 4.1 Anselm's Argument

Anselm of Canterbury described God as the greatest conceivable being.

In a simplified presentation:

1. God is understood as the greatest conceivable being.
2. Existing in reality is argued to be greater than existing only in the understanding.
3. Therefore, the greatest conceivable being must exist in reality.

The argument attempts to show that denying God's existence creates a contradiction within the concept of God.

### 4.2 Criticisms

Gaunilo criticised the reasoning by presenting a parody involving a perfect island.

Immanuel Kant argued that existence is not a real predicate that simply adds a property to a concept.

These criticisms challenge whether existence can be derived from a definition.

## 5. The Teleological Argument

The teleological argument reasons from apparent order, complexity or purpose in the universe towards an intelligent designer.

The term teleology concerns purpose or goal-directed explanation.

### 5.1 William Paley and the Watchmaker Analogy

William Paley compared the complexity of living organisms to the complexity of a watch.

He argued that just as a watch's organised parts suggest a designer, the apparent organisation of living systems suggests an intelligent creator.

### 5.2 Criticisms

Important criticisms include:

- Natural selection provides an explanation for biological adaptation.
- Apparent design does not necessarily establish an intelligent designer.
- The existence of imperfections and suffering raises further questions.
- Even evidence of design would not automatically establish the attributes of a particular deity.

## 6. The Problem of Evil

The problem of evil is one of the most influential challenges to classical theism.

It asks how the existence of suffering and evil can be reconciled with a God who is omnipotent, omniscient and perfectly good.

Two broad forms are important.

### 6.1 Logical Problem of Evil

The logical problem argues that the existence of evil appears logically incompatible with the existence of an all-powerful, all-knowing and perfectly good God.

### 6.2 Evidential Problem of Evil

The evidential problem argues that the amount, distribution or apparently pointless character of suffering provides evidence against the existence of such a God.

### 6.3 Responses to the Problem

#### Free Will Defence

The free will defence argues that genuine moral freedom may make wrongdoing possible.

A world containing free agents may be considered valuable even though those agents can choose harmful actions.

#### Soul-Making Theodicy

John Hick developed an influential soul-making approach.

It argues that certain forms of difficulty and suffering may provide conditions for developing moral and spiritual qualities.

#### Natural Law Responses

Some responses argue that a stable natural order requires regular physical processes, which can sometimes produce harmful outcomes.

These responses remain philosophically debated.

## 7. Religious Experience

Religious experience refers to experiences interpreted as encounters with or awareness of a divine or transcendent reality.

Examples include:

- Mystical experiences.
- Experiences of divine presence.
- Feelings of spiritual unity.
- Experiences interpreted as revelation.

William James examined religious experiences in *The Varieties of Religious Experience*.

Philosophers debate whether such experiences provide genuine evidence or can be explained through psychological, neurological and cultural processes.

## 8. Faith and Reason

The relationship between faith and reason is a major topic in philosophy of religion.

Possible positions include:

- Faith and reason are compatible.
- Religious beliefs require independent rational justification.
- Some religious truths are accessible through faith rather than reason.
- Faith may involve commitment beyond available evidence.

Philosophers disagree about whether faith can constitute a rational basis for belief.

## 9. Divine Attributes and Their Problems

Classical theism commonly attributes several characteristics to God.

### 9.1 Omnipotence

Omnipotence refers to unlimited power.

A philosophical difficulty concerns whether an all-powerful being can perform logically contradictory tasks.

### 9.2 Omniscience

Omniscience refers to complete knowledge.

It raises questions about whether divine foreknowledge is compatible with human free will.

### 9.3 Perfect Goodness

Perfect goodness refers to complete moral perfection.

The problem of evil challenges how this attribute can be reconciled with widespread suffering.

## 10. Summary

Philosophy of religion examines arguments about divine existence, religious experience, faith, evil and divine attributes.

Major arguments include the cosmological, ontological and teleological arguments. Important objections include the problem of evil, criticism of conceptual arguments and naturalistic explanations of religious experiences.
