# Lesson 6: Propositions and Arguments

Philosophical reasoning depends on the ability to identify statements, organize premises and evaluate conclusions. Propositions express claims that can be true or false, while arguments use propositions to support conclusions.

Understanding their structure is essential for studying formal logic, critical thinking and philosophical debate.

## 1. What Is a Proposition?

A proposition is the meaning expressed by a declarative statement that can be evaluated as true or false.

Examples:

- The Earth is a planet.
- All humans are mortal.
- Some birds cannot fly.
- No square is a circle.

A proposition is not identical to the sentence expressing it.

For example:

- "The Earth revolves around the Sun."
- "La Tierra gira alrededor del Sol."

These sentences use different languages but express the same proposition.

## 2. Sentences That Are Not Propositions

Not every sentence expresses a proposition.

### 2.1 Questions

"Where are you going?"

This asks for information rather than asserting something.

### 2.2 Commands

"Open the window."

This gives an instruction rather than expressing a truth-evaluable claim.

### 2.3 Exclamations

"What a beautiful painting!"

This primarily expresses an emotional or evaluative reaction.

However, a declarative sentence expressing an aesthetic judgment can function as a proposition.

Example: "This painting is beautiful."

## 3. Types of Propositions

### 3.1 Simple Propositions

A simple proposition expresses one basic claim.

Example:

"Birds have feathers."

### 3.2 Compound Propositions

A compound proposition combines two or more propositions using logical connectives.

Example:

"It is raining and the road is wet."

Let:

- P: It is raining.
- Q: The road is wet.

The compound proposition is:

\[
P \land Q
\]

## 4. Categorical Propositions

Traditional categorical logic examines relationships between classes or categories.

Four standard forms are commonly represented using A, E, I and O.

### 4.1 Universal Affirmative: A

Form:

"All S are P."

Example:

"All humans are mortal."

It asserts that every member of the subject class belongs to the predicate class.

### 4.2 Universal Negative: E

Form:

"No S are P."

Example:

"No squares are circles."

It denies that any member of the subject class belongs to the predicate class.

### 4.3 Particular Affirmative: I

Form:

"Some S are P."

Example:

"Some students are musicians."

It asserts that at least one member of the subject class belongs to the predicate class.

### 4.4 Particular Negative: O

Form:

"Some S are not P."

Example:

"Some birds are not capable of flight."

It asserts that at least one member of the subject class does not belong to the predicate class.

## 5. Arguments and Their Structure

An argument is a group of propositions in which premises are intended to support a conclusion.

Example:

1. All mammals are warm-blooded.
2. Dolphins are mammals.
3. Therefore, dolphins are warm-blooded.

The structure is:

\[
P_1,\ P_2 \therefore C
\]

Where:

- \(P_1\) = First premise.
- \(P_2\) = Second premise.
- \(C\) = Conclusion.
- \(\therefore\) = Therefore.

## 6. Explicit and Implicit Premises

Some arguments state every premise explicitly.

Others rely on assumptions that are left unstated.

Example:

"She is a doctor, so she understands human anatomy."

Possible explicit premise:

- She is a doctor.

Possible implicit premise:

- Doctors generally receive training in human anatomy.

Conclusion:

- She understands human anatomy.

Implicit premises must be examined because they may contain unjustified assumptions.

## 7. Standardizing Arguments

Standardization means rewriting an argument so its logical structure becomes clear.

Original statement:

"Since all humans are mortal and Socrates was human, he must have been mortal."

Standardized form:

- Premise 1: All humans are mortal.
- Premise 2: Socrates was human.
- Conclusion: Socrates was mortal.

Standardization helps identify missing premises, ambiguous expressions and reasoning errors.

## 8. Evaluating Arguments

Arguments can be evaluated using several criteria.

### 8.1 Relevance

Do the premises actually relate to the conclusion?

### 8.2 Acceptability

Are the premises supported or reasonably acceptable?

### 8.3 Sufficiency

Do the premises provide enough support for the conclusion?

### 8.4 Logical Structure

Does the conclusion follow from the premises?

An argument may contain relevant information but still fail to establish its conclusion.

## 9. Argument Diagrams

Argument diagrams represent the relationship between premises and conclusions.

Consider:

- Regular exercise improves cardiovascular fitness.
- Walking is a form of exercise.
- Therefore, walking can improve cardiovascular fitness.

The premises work together to support the conclusion.

A simplified diagram is:

\[
P_1 + P_2 \rightarrow C
\]

In other arguments, individual premises may provide separate support for the same conclusion.

Diagramming helps reveal whether premises are independent or jointly necessary.

## 10. Practical Example: Analyzing an Argument

Consider:

"All forms of entertainment are harmful because some people become addicted to video games."

Analysis:

**Premise:** Some people become addicted to video games.

**Conclusion:** All forms of entertainment are harmful.

The argument is weak because the premise concerns only some people and one form of entertainment, while the conclusion makes a universal claim.

The conclusion is broader than the evidence supports.

A more reasonable conclusion would be:

"Some forms of entertainment can become harmful when used excessively."

## 11. Summary

- Propositions express claims that can be true or false.
- Questions and commands do not ordinarily express propositions.
- Propositions can be simple or compound.
- Categorical propositions have four traditional forms: A, E, I and O.
- Arguments contain premises and conclusions.
- Some arguments contain implicit premises.
- Standardization makes argument structures easier to evaluate.
- Strong arguments require relevant, acceptable and sufficient premises.
