# Lesson 29: Philosophy of Science and Scientific Reasoning

Philosophy of science investigates the foundations, methods, assumptions and limits of scientific knowledge. It examines what makes an explanation scientific, how scientific theories are evaluated, and how scientific knowledge develops. It also explores the relationship between observation, experimentation, logic and theory.

## 1. Meaning of Philosophy of Science

Philosophy of science examines the nature and development of scientific knowledge.

Its central questions include:

- What distinguishes science from non-science?
- How are scientific theories justified?
- Can scientific knowledge be completely objective?
- What is the role of observation and experimentation?
- How do scientific theories change?
- Can science discover the ultimate nature of reality?

It does not replace scientific investigation. Instead, it critically examines the reasoning and assumptions underlying scientific practice.

## 2. The Scientific Method

The scientific method refers broadly to systematic procedures used to investigate natural phenomena.

A simplified scientific investigation may involve:

1. Observation of a phenomenon.
2. Identification of a research question.
3. Development of a hypothesis.
4. Design of an experiment.
5. Collection of data.
6. Analysis of results.
7. Evaluation of the hypothesis.
8. Communication and replication of findings.

Scientific investigation is not always strictly linear. Researchers may revise hypotheses, repeat experiments or develop new questions during the process.

## 3. Hypotheses, Theories and Laws

### 3.1 Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a proposed explanation or prediction that can be investigated.

For example:

"Increasing the temperature of a reaction will increase its reaction rate under specified conditions."

A useful scientific hypothesis should be sufficiently clear to allow meaningful investigation.

### 3.2 Scientific Theory

A scientific theory is a well-supported explanatory framework that integrates evidence and provides explanations of phenomena.

Examples include:

- Theory of evolution.
- Germ theory of disease.
- Atomic theory.
- Theory of plate tectonics.

A scientific theory is not merely an unsupported guess.

### 3.3 Scientific Law

A scientific law describes a regular relationship or pattern observed in nature.

For example, Newton's second law is commonly expressed as:

\[
F=ma
\]

A scientific law generally describes a relationship, while a theory provides a broader explanatory framework.

A theory does not simply become a law after receiving enough evidence.

## 4. Induction and Deduction in Science

Scientific reasoning frequently involves both inductive and deductive reasoning.

### 4.1 Inductive Reasoning

Induction moves from particular observations towards general conclusions.

Example:

- Several observed metals expand when heated.
- Further experiments show similar behaviour.
- A general conclusion is proposed about thermal expansion.

Inductive conclusions are generally probable rather than logically guaranteed.

### 4.2 Deductive Reasoning

Deduction moves from general premises to conclusions that necessarily follow if the premises are true and the reasoning is valid.

Example:

1. All objects with property A satisfy condition B.
2. Object X has property A.
3. Therefore, object X satisfies condition B.

Scientific theories generate predictions through deductive reasoning, which can then be tested through observation.

## 5. Karl Popper and Falsifiability

Karl Popper argued that falsifiability is a central criterion for distinguishing scientific theories from theories that cannot be empirically tested.

A theory is falsifiable when it makes claims that could, in principle, be shown to be false through relevant observations or experiments.

For example:

"The temperature of this substance will increase when a specified amount of energy is supplied under controlled conditions" is testable.

A claim that explains every possible outcome without allowing any possible counterexample is difficult to test scientifically.

### 5.1 Falsification

Falsification occurs when evidence conflicts with a theory or one of its predictions.

Scientific theories should remain open to testing and possible revision.

However, a failed prediction does not always immediately disprove an entire theory because experimental assumptions, instruments and background conditions may also be responsible.

## 6. Thomas Kuhn and Scientific Revolutions

Thomas Kuhn argued that scientific development involves periods of normal science interrupted by major conceptual changes.

### 6.1 Paradigm

A paradigm is a shared framework of theories, methods, assumptions and standards that guides scientific research.

Scientists working within a paradigm generally accept its fundamental assumptions while investigating particular problems.

### 6.2 Normal Science

Normal science involves solving problems within an established paradigm.

Researchers refine measurements, extend theories and address unanswered questions.

### 6.3 Anomalies and Crisis

An anomaly occurs when observations or experimental results do not fit comfortably within the accepted framework.

When serious anomalies accumulate, confidence in the paradigm may weaken.

### 6.4 Scientific Revolution

A scientific revolution occurs when an established paradigm is replaced or substantially transformed by a competing framework.

Kuhn used historical developments in astronomy and physics to illustrate this process.

## 7. The Problem of Demarcation

The demarcation problem asks how to distinguish science from non-scientific or pseudoscientific claims.

Possible criteria include:

- Testability.
- Falsifiability.
- Empirical support.
- Reproducibility.
- Logical consistency.
- Predictive success.
- Openness to criticism.

No single simple criterion captures every feature of scientific practice.

## 8. Objectivity and Scientific Values

Science aims to produce objective knowledge, but scientific research is conducted by human beings within social and institutional environments.

Potential influences include:

- Personal assumptions.
- Funding priorities.
- Research incentives.
- Cultural expectations.
- Measurement limitations.
- Publication bias.

Scientific objectivity is strengthened through:

- Transparent methods.
- Peer review.
- Independent replication.
- Statistical scrutiny.
- Open criticism.
- Disclosure of limitations.

## 9. Scientific Realism and Anti-Realism

### 9.1 Scientific Realism

Scientific realism maintains that successful scientific theories often provide approximately true descriptions of both observable and unobservable aspects of reality.

For example, scientific realists generally accept that entities such as electrons exist, even though they are not directly visible to ordinary human perception.

### 9.2 Scientific Anti-Realism

Scientific anti-realism questions whether successful scientific theories should necessarily be interpreted as literally true descriptions of an underlying reality.

Some anti-realist approaches emphasise the predictive and explanatory usefulness of theories without requiring commitment to every theoretical entity.

## 10. Applications and Importance

Philosophy of science is relevant to:

- Scientific research.
- Medical evidence.
- Climate science.
- Artificial intelligence.
- Technology development.
- Public policy.
- Evaluating pseudoscientific claims.

It encourages careful distinction between evidence, explanation, prediction and assumption.

## 11. Summary

Philosophy of science investigates the foundations and methods of scientific knowledge.

Important topics include the scientific method, hypotheses, theories, laws, induction, deduction, Popper's falsifiability, Kuhn's paradigms, the demarcation problem, scientific objectivity and scientific realism.
