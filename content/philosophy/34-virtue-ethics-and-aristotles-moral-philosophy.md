# Lesson 34: Virtue Ethics and Aristotle's Moral Philosophy

Virtue ethics is a major approach to moral philosophy that focuses on character, moral development and the qualities that make a person good. Instead of concentrating exclusively on rules or consequences, it asks what kind of person one should become. Aristotle's account of virtue, practical wisdom and human flourishing remains one of its most influential foundations.

## 1. Meaning of Virtue Ethics

Virtue ethics evaluates morality primarily through the character and dispositions of individuals.

It investigates qualities such as:

- Courage.
- Honesty.
- Compassion.
- Generosity.
- Justice.
- Patience.
- Wisdom.
- Self-control.

A virtuous person does not merely perform isolated good actions. They develop stable character traits that guide their behaviour across different situations.

## 2. Aristotle and Ancient Greek Ethics

Aristotle developed a systematic account of ethics in his work *Nicomachean Ethics*.

He argued that human actions are generally directed towards goals and that the highest human good is *eudaimonia*.

Eudaimonia is commonly translated as flourishing, well-being or living a fulfilled human life.

It is not simply temporary pleasure or material success.

## 3. Eudaimonia and the Human Good

Aristotle argued that the highest human good should be:

- Desirable for its own sake.
- Sufficiently complete.
- Associated with a fulfilled human life.

Eudaimonia involves living and acting well over a complete life.

Aristotle connected human flourishing with the distinctive capacities of human beings, particularly rational activity.

A simplified expression is:

\[
\text{Human flourishing} \approx \text{Rational activity in accordance with virtue}
\]

This is a conceptual summary rather than a mathematical equation.

## 4. The Function Argument

Aristotle used the function argument to investigate what constitutes a good human life.

The reasoning can be summarised as follows:

1. Different things have characteristic functions.
2. A good example of something performs its characteristic function well.
3. Human beings possess distinctive capacities for rational activity.
4. A good human life therefore involves the excellent exercise of rational capacities.

For Aristotle, human flourishing requires the development and exercise of virtue.

## 5. Moral and Intellectual Virtues

Aristotle distinguished between moral virtues and intellectual virtues.

### 5.1 Moral Virtues

Moral virtues involve character and habits governing behaviour.

Examples include:

- Courage.
- Temperance.
- Generosity.
- Truthfulness.
- Friendliness.
- Justice.

They develop through practice and habituation.

### 5.2 Intellectual Virtues

Intellectual virtues involve excellence in thinking and understanding.

Examples include:

- Wisdom.
- Understanding.
- Scientific knowledge.
- Practical wisdom.
- Art or technical skill.

They develop through learning, experience and intellectual training.

## 6. The Doctrine of the Mean

Aristotle's doctrine of the mean proposes that many moral virtues involve an appropriate balance between excessive and deficient responses.

The mean is not necessarily an arithmetic midpoint.

It is the appropriate response relative to the individual and circumstances.

Examples include:

| Deficiency | Virtue | Excess |
|---|---|---|
| Cowardice | Courage | Rashness |
| Insensibility | Temperance | Self-indulgence |
| Stinginess | Generosity | Wastefulness |
| Lack of confidence | Proper confidence | Excessive vanity |

For example, courage is not simply the absence of fear.

A courageous person recognises danger and responds appropriately rather than allowing fear or recklessness to dominate.

## 7. Habituation and Moral Development

Aristotle maintained that virtues develop through repeated practice.

A person becomes courageous by repeatedly performing courageous actions under appropriate circumstances.

Similarly:

- Honesty develops through truthful conduct.
- Generosity develops through appropriate giving.
- Self-control develops through disciplined behaviour.
- Justice develops through fair treatment.

Virtue is therefore connected with habit formation and character development.

However, merely repeating an action is not sufficient. A virtuous person should also understand the action and perform it for appropriate reasons.

## 8. Practical Wisdom

Practical wisdom, known as *phronesis*, is the intellectual capacity to determine how to act appropriately in particular situations.

General moral rules cannot always determine the correct response to every circumstance.

Practical wisdom helps individuals:

- Recognise relevant moral features.
- Understand competing values.
- Choose appropriate means.
- Balance emotions and reason.
- Apply virtues to specific situations.

A courageous response in one situation may be inappropriate in another.

Practical wisdom helps determine the difference.

## 9. Virtue Ethics Compared with Other Theories

| Virtue ethics | Consequentialism | Deontology |
|---|---|---|
| Focuses on character. | Focuses on consequences. | Focuses on duties and principles. |
| Asks what kind of person one should become. | Asks which action produces the best outcome. | Asks what moral obligations apply. |
| Emphasises virtues and practical wisdom. | Emphasises overall outcomes. | Emphasises moral rules and duties. |

These approaches are not necessarily incompatible in every practical decision.

## 10. Strengths of Virtue Ethics

- Emphasises long-term moral development.
- Recognises the importance of motivation and character.
- Connects ethics with ordinary human experience.
- Gives importance to practical judgment.
- Avoids reducing morality to mechanical rule-following.

## 11. Criticisms of Virtue Ethics

### 11.1 Lack of Precise Rules

Virtue ethics may not always provide a clear answer when an immediate decision must be made.

### 11.2 Cultural Differences

Different societies may disagree about which character traits should be encouraged.

### 11.3 The Problem of Moral Conflict

Virtues may conflict in particular situations.

For example, honesty and compassion may point towards different responses.

Practical wisdom is required, but its application may remain difficult to evaluate.

## 12. Summary

Virtue ethics focuses on character and the development of moral excellence.

Aristotle's account emphasises eudaimonia, the function argument, moral and intellectual virtues, habituation, the doctrine of the mean and practical wisdom.

It presents morality as the cultivation of a character capable of living and acting well.
