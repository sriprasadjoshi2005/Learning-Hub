# Lesson 36: Political Philosophy and the Nature of the State

Political philosophy investigates the foundations of political authority, the legitimacy of governments, individual rights, justice and the organisation of society. It asks why governments should exist, what powers they should possess, and how political institutions can be justified. This lesson introduces major concepts and philosophical approaches to political life.

## 1. Meaning of Political Philosophy

Political philosophy examines the principles underlying political institutions and social organisation.

Its central questions include:

- Why do societies need governments?
- What makes political authority legitimate?
- What rights should individuals possess?
- What is the relationship between freedom and equality?
- What makes a political system just?
- When is resistance to government justified?

Political philosophy evaluates political arrangements rather than merely describing how they operate.

## 2. The State

A state is a political organisation that exercises authority over a population within a defined territory.

A state commonly includes:

- A population.
- A defined territory.
- A government.
- Institutions of law.
- Sovereign political authority.

The state differs from the government.

The government refers to the institutions and officials currently exercising political power, while the state is the broader political organisation that persists across changes in government.

## 3. Political Authority and Legitimacy

Political authority refers to the recognised right to make and enforce decisions within a political community.

Legitimacy concerns whether that authority is justified.

A government may possess the practical ability to control a population without necessarily possessing legitimate moral authority.

Important foundations proposed for political legitimacy include:

- Consent.
- Democratic representation.
- Constitutional rules.
- Protection of rights.
- Public welfare.
- Justice.

## 4. The Social Contract Tradition

Social contract theories attempt to explain political authority through an agreement, either actual or hypothetical, among individuals.

Three influential philosophers developed different versions of this tradition.

### 4.1 Thomas Hobbes

In *Leviathan*, Thomas Hobbes described a hypothetical state of nature without a common political authority.

He argued that such a condition would involve insecurity because individuals could threaten one another without a reliable system of enforcement.

To escape this condition, individuals authorise a sovereign power capable of maintaining peace and security.

Hobbes therefore defended strong political authority as necessary for social order.

### 4.2 John Locke

John Locke developed a different account of the state of nature.

He argued that individuals possess natural rights, including:

- Life.
- Liberty.
- Property.

Governments are established to protect these rights.

Political authority is legitimate only when it operates within appropriate limits and serves the people.

Locke also defended the right of resistance when a government systematically violates its responsibilities.

### 4.3 Jean-Jacques Rousseau

Rousseau argued that legitimate political authority should be based on the collective self-government of citizens.

He introduced the concept of the general will.

The general will concerns the common interest of the political community rather than merely the sum of private preferences.

Rousseau's political philosophy emphasises freedom, civic participation and popular sovereignty.

## 5. Sovereignty

Sovereignty refers to the highest recognised political authority within a territory.

### 5.1 Internal Sovereignty

Internal sovereignty concerns authority over institutions and individuals within the state.

### 5.2 External Sovereignty

External sovereignty concerns the independence of a state from the authority of other states.

In contemporary international relations, sovereignty is influenced by treaties, international law and global institutions.

## 6. Freedom and Political Liberty

Political philosophy distinguishes several forms of freedom.

### 6.1 Negative Liberty

Negative liberty refers to freedom from external interference.

An individual enjoys negative liberty when others do not unnecessarily prevent them from acting.

### 6.2 Positive Liberty

Positive liberty concerns the capacity for self-direction and meaningful control over one's life.

It may involve access to education, resources and conditions necessary for genuine agency.

The distinction is strongly associated with Isaiah Berlin.

## 7. Rights and Duties

Rights establish justified claims or protections that individuals possess.

Examples include:

- Freedom of expression.
- Freedom of conscience.
- Equality before the law.
- Protection from arbitrary punishment.
- Political participation.

Rights are often associated with corresponding duties.

For example, respecting another person's freedom of expression requires avoiding unjustified interference with their ability to communicate.

## 8. Democracy and Political Participation

Democracy is a political system in which citizens participate in the exercise of political authority.

Important forms include:

- Direct democracy.
- Representative democracy.
- Constitutional democracy.
- Participatory democracy.

Democratic systems generally rely on principles such as political equality, public accountability and meaningful participation.

However, democracy alone does not automatically guarantee justice or protect every minority interest.

## 9. The Problem of Political Obligation

Political obligation concerns whether individuals have a moral duty to obey the laws of their state.

Possible justifications include:

- Explicit or implicit consent.
- Fairness and reciprocity.
- The benefits of social cooperation.
- The need to maintain public order.
- Respect for legitimate institutions.

A major difficulty is explaining why individuals should be considered to have consented to political authority simply because they live within a particular state.

## 10. Civil Disobedience

Civil disobedience involves a deliberate and usually public violation of a law to protest serious injustice.

It is generally distinguished from ordinary criminal behaviour by its political and moral purpose.

Civil disobedience raises questions about:

- The legitimacy of unjust laws.
- The limits of obedience.
- Democratic responsibility.
- The role of public protest.
- The relationship between law and morality.

## 11. Summary

Political philosophy investigates the state, authority, legitimacy, sovereignty, freedom, rights, democracy and political obligation.

Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau developed influential social contract theories that offer different explanations of political authority.

Their ideas continue to influence debates concerning government, individual liberty, justice and democratic participation.
