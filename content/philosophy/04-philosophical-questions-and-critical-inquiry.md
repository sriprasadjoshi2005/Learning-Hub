# Lesson 4: Philosophical Questions and Critical Inquiry

Philosophy begins with questions. However, not every question is philosophical. Questions about the temperature of water or the distance between two cities can usually be answered through measurement.

Philosophical questions often investigate deeper issues concerning meaning, truth, existence, knowledge and value.

Critical inquiry is the disciplined process of examining such questions through reasoning, evidence, assumptions and arguments.

## 1. What Makes a Question Philosophical?

A philosophical question generally has one or more of the following characteristics:

1. It concerns fundamental concepts.
2. It requires clarification of meanings.
3. It involves assumptions that need examination.
4. It permits competing arguments.
5. It has implications beyond a single factual observation.

Examples:

- Does free will exist?
- What makes an action morally right?
- Can machines become conscious?
- Is beauty objective or subjective?
- What is the difference between appearance and reality?

## 2. Types of Philosophical Questions

### 2.1 Questions About Reality

These questions investigate existence and the nature of the universe.

Examples:

- Does reality exist independently of our minds?
- Is everything physical?
- Why does anything exist?

These questions belong primarily to metaphysics.

### 2.2 Questions About Knowledge

These questions investigate what we can know and how we can know it.

Examples:

- Can our senses deceive us?
- Is certainty possible?
- What makes evidence reliable?

These questions belong primarily to epistemology.

### 2.3 Questions About Morality

These questions investigate right and wrong.

Examples:

- Is it acceptable to lie to prevent harm?
- Do individuals have moral obligations toward strangers?
- Can an action be right if it produces harmful consequences?

These questions belong to ethics.

### 2.4 Questions About Human Existence

These questions concern identity, freedom, purpose and meaning.

Examples:

- What makes someone the same person over time?
- Are humans genuinely free?
- Does life have an objective purpose?

### 2.5 Questions About Society

These questions investigate justice, authority, equality and political responsibility.

Examples:

- What makes a government legitimate?
- Should everyone have equal opportunities?
- When is civil disobedience justified?

## 3. Facts, Opinions and Philosophical Claims

It is important to distinguish different types of statements.

### 3.1 Factual Statements

Factual statements can be investigated through observation, measurement or other suitable evidence.

Example:

"Water freezes at approximately 0°C under standard atmospheric pressure."

### 3.2 Opinions

Opinions express personal judgments or preferences.

Example:

"Classical music is more enjoyable than electronic music."

An opinion may be supported by reasons, but the preference itself is subjective.

### 3.3 Philosophical Claims

Philosophical claims propose positions about fundamental questions.

Example:

"Human beings possess free will."

Such a claim requires conceptual clarification and philosophical argument.

Some philosophical claims also have empirical implications and can be informed by scientific research.

## 4. The Structure of Critical Inquiry

Critical inquiry can be organized into a systematic process.

### Step 1: Identify the Question

Determine exactly what is being investigated.

Example:

"Is artificial intelligence capable of understanding?"

### Step 2: Define Important Concepts

Clarify the meanings of terms such as intelligence and understanding.

Without clear definitions, people may argue while using the same words differently.

### Step 3: Identify Assumptions

Determine what the question or proposed answer takes for granted.

For example, the claim that machines cannot understand may assume that understanding requires biological consciousness.

### Step 4: Examine Arguments

Identify the reasons offered in support of each position.

### Step 5: Consider Counterarguments

Search for objections, alternative explanations and counterexamples.

### Step 6: Evaluate Evidence

Determine whether the available evidence supports the claims being made.

### Step 7: Form a Reasoned Conclusion

Develop a conclusion that accurately reflects the strength of the arguments.

A good conclusion may acknowledge uncertainty.

## 5. Assumptions and Hidden Premises

An assumption is something accepted or taken for granted without necessarily being explicitly stated.

Consider:

"People who disagree with this policy do not care about society."

This statement assumes that disagreement must result from a lack of concern.

However, people may disagree because they have different ideas about what benefits society.

Identifying hidden assumptions prevents misleading conclusions.

## 6. Counterexamples and Their Importance

A counterexample is an example that challenges or disproves a general claim.

Consider:

"All successful people are happy."

A single genuinely successful but unhappy individual challenges the universal claim.

However, counterexamples must be relevant and accurately understood.

A carefully constructed philosophical argument should survive reasonable objections rather than ignoring inconvenient cases.

## 7. Thought Experiments in Critical Inquiry

Thought experiments help investigate questions by creating hypothetical situations.

### Example: The Experience Machine

Imagine a machine capable of providing experiences that feel completely real and permanently pleasurable.

A person could enter the machine and experience a lifetime of happiness without interacting with the external world.

Would you choose to enter?

This thought experiment raises questions about:

- Whether happiness is the only important value.
- Whether authentic experiences matter.
- Whether reality is more valuable than illusion.
- Whether meaningful relationships require genuine interaction.

It demonstrates how a hypothetical situation can reveal hidden assumptions about human values.

## 8. Open-Mindedness and Intellectual Humility

Critical inquiry requires a willingness to reconsider one's beliefs.

**Open-mindedness** means seriously considering alternative explanations.

**Intellectual humility** means recognizing that one's knowledge may be incomplete and one's conclusions may be mistaken.

These qualities do not require accepting every viewpoint.

Instead, they encourage fair evaluation before forming conclusions.

## 9. Practical Application: Evaluating a Claim

Consider the statement:

"Technology always improves human life."

A critical examination might identify the following:

**Supporting considerations:**
- Technology improves communication.
- Medical technology can save lives.
- Automation can increase productivity.

**Counterconsiderations:**
- Technology can create privacy risks.
- Excessive dependence can reduce certain skills.
- Some technologies may contribute to environmental damage.

**Reasoned conclusion:**

Technology can improve human life, but its effects depend on how it is designed, distributed and used.

The word "always" makes the original statement too broad.

## 10. Summary

- Philosophical questions investigate fundamental issues about reality, knowledge, morality and existence.
- Facts, opinions and philosophical claims are different kinds of statements.
- Critical inquiry involves identifying questions, clarifying concepts and examining assumptions.
- Counterexamples help test universal claims.
- Thought experiments explore the implications of philosophical positions.
- Open-mindedness and intellectual humility strengthen philosophical reasoning.
