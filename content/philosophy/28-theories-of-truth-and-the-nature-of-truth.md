# Lesson 28: Theories of Truth and the Nature of Truth

Truth is one of the most fundamental concepts in philosophy. It is central to knowledge, logic, science, communication and ethics. Philosophers have developed different theories to explain what makes a statement true and how truth relates to reality, consistency and practical consequences.

## 1. Meaning of Truth

Truth concerns whether a statement, belief or proposition accurately represents what is the case.

For example:

- "The Earth orbits the Sun" is true.
- "The Earth is the centre of the Solar System" is false.

However, defining truth becomes more difficult when dealing with abstract, mathematical, moral or philosophical statements.

Important questions include:

- What makes a statement true?
- Is truth independent of human beliefs?
- Does truth require agreement with reality?
- Can a statement be true because it is useful?
- Can contradictory statements both be true?

## 2. The Correspondence Theory of Truth

The correspondence theory maintains that a statement is true when it corresponds to reality or the facts.

For example:

The statement "Water boils at approximately 100°C at standard atmospheric pressure" is true because it corresponds to the relevant physical conditions.

A simplified representation is:

\[
P \text{ is true} \iff P \text{ corresponds to reality}
\]

The symbol \(\iff\) means "if and only if."

### 2.1 Strengths

- Provides an intuitive understanding of truth.
- Connects truth with objective reality.
- Fits naturally with many scientific statements.
- Distinguishes truth from personal opinion.

### 2.2 Criticisms

- It can be difficult to explain what correspondence means.
- It raises questions about the nature of facts.
- Abstract and mathematical truths may be difficult to explain through simple correspondence.
- It may not fully explain how language represents reality.

## 3. The Coherence Theory of Truth

The coherence theory associates truth with consistency and logical relationships within a broader system of beliefs.

A statement is considered true when it coheres with an organised and consistent system of propositions.

For example, a scientific explanation may be supported by:

- Established observations.
- Mathematical models.
- Other scientific theories.
- Independent experimental results.

### 3.1 Strengths

- Emphasises systematic understanding.
- Highlights relationships between beliefs.
- Is useful for evaluating complex systems of knowledge.

### 3.2 Criticisms

- A consistent system may still be disconnected from reality.
- Different systems may be internally coherent but incompatible with each other.
- Coherence alone may not establish objective truth.

## 4. The Pragmatic Theory of Truth

Pragmatist approaches connect truth with practical consequences, successful inquiry and the long-term results of holding or testing beliefs.

Important philosophers associated with pragmatism include:

- Charles Sanders Peirce.
- William James.
- John Dewey.

Pragmatism does not simply mean that any useful belief is true.

In Peirce's approach, truth is connected with the ideal outcome of sustained inquiry by a community of investigators.

### 4.1 Strengths

- Emphasises practical investigation.
- Connects belief with experience.
- Highlights the importance of testing ideas.
- Recognises that inquiry develops over time.

### 4.2 Criticisms

- A belief may be useful without being true.
- Practical success can depend on circumstances.
- Different individuals may consider different beliefs useful.

## 5. The Deflationary Theory of Truth

Deflationary theories argue that truth does not require a complicated metaphysical explanation.

The expression "It is true that snow is white" communicates essentially the same claim as "Snow is white."

The concept of truth allows us to endorse, generalise or refer to statements without necessarily adding a separate property to each proposition.

A familiar logical principle is the equivalence schema:

\[
\text{"P" is true} \iff P
\]

For example:

"'The Earth is round' is true" is equivalent to "The Earth is round."

## 6. The Semantic Theory of Truth

Alfred Tarski developed an influential semantic account of truth for formal languages.

His approach distinguishes between:

- An object language, which is the language being discussed.
- A metalanguage, which is used to describe that language.

A central principle is the T-schema:

\[
\text{"Snow is white" is true if and only if snow is white.}
\]

Tarski's work provides a formal framework for defining truth in certain languages while avoiding semantic paradoxes.

## 7. Truth and Logical Consistency

Truth and logical consistency are related but distinct.

A set of propositions is logically consistent when it does not contain a contradiction under the relevant logical system.

However, consistency does not automatically establish truth.

For example, a fictional world can have a perfectly consistent set of rules without corresponding to the actual world.

Likewise, an inconsistent set of beliefs may contain some individually true propositions.

## 8. Truth and Relativism

Relativism maintains that certain truths or standards of truth depend on a particular framework, culture, language or perspective.

Different forms include:

- Cultural relativism.
- Epistemic relativism.
- Moral relativism.
- Conceptual relativism.

The philosophical challenge is determining whether truth is genuinely relative or whether different people simply possess different beliefs about objective matters.

## 9. Truth and Misinformation

Understanding theories of truth is useful when evaluating information.

A responsible evaluation should consider:

1. Whether the claim is clearly stated.
2. Whether reliable evidence supports it.
3. Whether the evidence is relevant.
4. Whether alternative explanations exist.
5. Whether the claim is logically consistent.
6. Whether independent sources support the conclusion.

Agreement, popularity and emotional appeal do not independently establish truth.

## 10. Summary

Truth is central to knowledge and rational inquiry.

The correspondence theory connects truth with reality, the coherence theory emphasises consistency within a system, and pragmatism connects truth with practical inquiry and consequences. Deflationary and semantic theories examine the logical and linguistic functions of truth.

These approaches provide different ways of understanding what it means for a statement to be true.
