# Lesson 31: Ethics and the Foundations of Moral Philosophy

Ethics is the branch of philosophy concerned with morality, right and wrong, good and bad, and the principles that guide human conduct. It investigates how individuals should behave, what makes actions morally acceptable, and whether moral values are universal or dependent on social and cultural contexts.

## 1. Meaning and Scope of Ethics

Ethics examines the principles and values used to evaluate human actions.

Important questions include:

- What makes an action morally right or wrong?
- Is morality based on reason, emotion, religion or social agreement?
- Do moral obligations apply equally to everyone?
- Can an action be right even if it produces harmful consequences?
- Are moral values objective or subjective?

Ethics is not limited to identifying socially accepted behaviour. It critically examines whether accepted practices are actually justified.

## 2. Morality and Ethics

Although the terms are closely related, they can be distinguished.

**Morality** refers to the beliefs, principles, values and practices concerning right and wrong.

**Ethics** refers to the systematic philosophical examination of morality.

For example, a society may consider honesty an important moral value. Ethics investigates why honesty should be valued, whether it is always required, and how it should be balanced against other moral considerations.

## 3. Major Branches of Ethics

Ethics is commonly divided into three major branches.

### 3.1 Normative Ethics

Normative ethics investigates the standards and principles that determine how people ought to act.

It asks questions such as:

- What makes an action morally right?
- What duties do individuals have?
- What kind of person should someone become?

Major normative theories include consequentialism, deontology and virtue ethics.

### 3.2 Metaethics

Metaethics investigates the meaning, foundations and status of moral judgments.

It asks:

- What does "good" mean?
- Are moral facts objective?
- Can moral statements be true or false?
- Are moral judgments expressions of emotion or rational beliefs?

### 3.3 Applied Ethics

Applied ethics examines moral questions arising in particular situations and fields.

Examples include:

- Medical ethics.
- Environmental ethics.
- Business ethics.
- Technology ethics.
- Animal ethics.
- Bioethics.

## 4. Moral Judgments and Moral Reasoning

A moral judgment evaluates an action, decision or character trait according to ethical standards.

For example:

"Deliberately harming an innocent person is wrong."

Moral reasoning involves examining the reasons supporting such judgments.

A structured moral analysis may involve:

1. Identifying the action or decision.
2. Identifying the individuals affected.
3. Examining relevant moral principles.
4. Considering possible consequences.
5. Evaluating conflicting obligations.
6. Reaching a reasoned conclusion.

Moral reasoning requires more than personal preference because ethical conclusions should be supported by defensible arguments.

## 5. Moral Values and Principles

Moral values are qualities or ideals considered important in ethical life.

Examples include:

- Honesty.
- Justice.
- Compassion.
- Responsibility.
- Respect.
- Fairness.
- Freedom.
- Integrity.

Moral principles provide general standards for evaluating actions.

For example, the principle of fairness requires that similar cases be treated similarly unless a relevant difference justifies different treatment.

## 6. Moral Objectivism and Subjectivism

### 6.1 Moral Objectivism

Moral objectivism maintains that at least some moral judgments are true independently of individual preferences or opinions.

For example, an objectivist may argue that deliberately torturing an innocent person for entertainment is wrong regardless of whether a society approves of it.

### 6.2 Moral Subjectivism

Moral subjectivism maintains that moral judgments depend on the attitudes, feelings or perspectives of individuals.

According to this approach, moral evaluations are closely connected to subjective responses rather than entirely independent moral facts.

### 6.3 Moral Relativism

Moral relativism maintains that moral standards or judgments depend on particular frameworks, such as cultural or social contexts.

It highlights differences in moral practices across societies.

However, moral relativism raises difficult questions about whether harmful cultural practices can be criticised from outside their own framework.

## 7. Moral Dilemmas

A moral dilemma occurs when an individual faces competing moral obligations or values and cannot satisfy all of them simultaneously.

Example:

A person must choose between telling a painful truth and protecting someone from immediate emotional distress.

The conflict involves honesty and compassion.

Other examples include:

- Individual freedom versus public safety.
- Loyalty versus impartial justice.
- Personal obligations versus broader social responsibilities.
- Privacy versus the prevention of serious harm.

Moral dilemmas demonstrate that ethical decisions can require balancing several legitimate values.

## 8. Ethics and Law

Ethics and law are related but not identical.

| Ethics | Law |
|---|---|
| Examines moral right and wrong. | Establishes legally enforceable rules. |
| Can criticise existing laws. | Is created and enforced through legal institutions. |
| May involve personal and social obligations. | Defines legal rights and responsibilities. |
| Moral violations are not always illegal. | Legal violations may carry formal penalties. |

An action can be legal but morally questionable, or morally justified but legally prohibited under a particular legal system.

## 9. Importance of Ethics

Ethics is essential to:

- Personal decision-making.
- Public policy.
- Scientific research.
- Medical practice.
- Artificial intelligence.
- Environmental protection.
- Business conduct.
- Social justice.

It provides methods for evaluating decisions beyond convenience, popularity or personal advantage.

## 10. Summary

Ethics is the philosophical study of morality and human conduct.

Its major branches are normative ethics, metaethics and applied ethics. It examines moral values, principles, objectivism, subjectivism, relativism and moral dilemmas.

Ethical reasoning helps individuals evaluate actions through defensible principles and careful consideration of competing values.
