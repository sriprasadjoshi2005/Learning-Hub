# Lesson 43: Philosophy of Science and Scientific Method

The philosophy of science examines the foundations, methods, assumptions, and limits of scientific knowledge. It investigates what makes a theory scientific, how scientific explanations are developed, and whether scientific progress brings us closer to understanding objective reality.

## 1. What Is Philosophy of Science?

Philosophy of science examines questions such as:

- What distinguishes science from non-science?
- What counts as scientific evidence?
- Can scientific theories be proven?
- How do scientific theories change?
- Are scientific observations completely objective?

It connects philosophical reasoning with scientific investigation.

## 2. Induction and Deduction

### 2.1 Inductive Reasoning

Induction moves from particular observations toward general conclusions.

Example:

1. Copper expands when heated.
2. Iron expands when heated.
3. Aluminium expands when heated.
4. Therefore, metals generally expand when heated.

Inductive conclusions are usually probable rather than logically guaranteed.

### 2.2 Deductive Reasoning

Deduction moves from general premises toward specific conclusions.

Example:

Premise 1: All mammals are warm-blooded.

Premise 2: Whales are mammals.

Conclusion: Whales are warm-blooded.

A valid deductive argument guarantees its conclusion if its premises are true.

## 3. Karl Popper and Falsifiability

Karl Popper argued that scientific theories should be falsifiable.

A theory is falsifiable if some possible observation could demonstrate that it is incorrect.

For example:

"The temperature of water affects its boiling behavior under controlled pressure" can be investigated through experiments.

A statement that is compatible with every possible observation cannot be scientifically tested in the same way.

### 3.1 Falsification

Scientific theories should be exposed to serious attempts at testing.

A theory that survives repeated testing gains support, but it is not permanently proven.

### 3.2 Importance of Falsifiability

Falsifiability encourages:
- Testable predictions.
- Experimental investigation.
- Critical examination.
- Scientific correction.

## 4. Thomas Kuhn and Paradigm Shifts

Thomas Kuhn argued that scientific development does not always occur through gradual accumulation of knowledge.

He introduced the concept of a paradigm.

A paradigm provides:
- Accepted theories.
- Standard methods.
- Shared assumptions.
- Scientific research problems.

### 4.1 Stages of Scientific Development

Kuhn described a general pattern:

1. Normal science: Researchers work within an accepted paradigm.
2. Anomalies: Observations arise that the existing paradigm struggles to explain.
3. Crisis: Confidence in the paradigm weakens.
4. Scientific revolution: A competing framework replaces the previous paradigm.
5. New normal science: Research continues within the new framework.

The transition from Newtonian mechanics to Einstein's relativity is an important example of a major theoretical transformation.

## 5. Scientific Realism and Anti-Realism

### 5.1 Scientific Realism

Scientific realism argues that successful scientific theories provide genuine knowledge about a mind-independent world.

It generally accepts that unobservable entities, such as electrons, can be real.

### 5.2 Scientific Anti-Realism

Scientific anti-realism questions whether scientific theories should be interpreted as literally describing an unobservable reality.

Some anti-realist approaches emphasize the predictive success and practical usefulness of scientific theories.

## 6. The Problem of Demarcation

The demarcation problem asks how science can be distinguished from pseudoscience and other forms of inquiry.

Potential scientific characteristics include:
- Testability.
- Evidence-based reasoning.
- Transparency.
- Reproducibility.
- Openness to correction.

No single criterion resolves every difficult case.

## 7. Objectivity and Scientific Observation

Scientific objectivity involves attempts to reduce the influence of personal preferences and assumptions.

However, scientific investigation can be affected by:
- Researcher expectations.
- Instrument limitations.
- Measurement errors.
- Theoretical assumptions.
- Social and institutional influences.

Scientific procedures such as replication, peer review, and independent testing help reduce these problems.

## 8. Summary

- Philosophy of science examines scientific knowledge and methodology.
- Induction develops general conclusions from observations.
- Deduction derives conclusions from premises.
- Popper emphasized falsifiability.
- Kuhn explained scientific revolutions through paradigm shifts.
- Scientific realism and anti-realism offer different interpretations of scientific theories.
- Scientific objectivity requires critical testing and correction.
