# Lesson 27: Philosophical Skepticism and the Limits of Knowledge

Philosophical skepticism examines whether knowledge is possible and whether our beliefs can be justified with sufficient certainty. It challenges assumptions about perception, memory, reasoning and the external world. Skepticism is not simply disbelief; it is a method of critically examining the grounds on which knowledge claims are made.

## 1. Meaning of Philosophical Skepticism

Skepticism is the philosophical position or method that questions whether particular beliefs or claims can be established as knowledge.

Skeptical questions include:

- Can we trust our senses?
- Can memory provide reliable knowledge?
- Is certainty possible?
- Can we prove that the external world exists?
- How can we distinguish knowledge from convincing illusion?

Skepticism encourages careful examination of evidence and justification.

## 2. Types of Skepticism

### 2.1 Global Skepticism

Global skepticism questions whether human beings can possess knowledge at all, or whether knowledge is possible across a broad range of subjects.

It challenges the general reliability of human knowledge.

### 2.2 Local Skepticism

Local skepticism questions knowledge within a particular domain.

Examples include skepticism concerning:

- Religious claims.
- Moral knowledge.
- Historical evidence.
- Knowledge of other minds.
- Scientific theories.

### 2.3 Academic Skepticism

Academic skepticism is associated with the skeptical tradition of Plato's Academy, particularly its later development.

It emphasises the difficulty of attaining certainty and the need to examine competing arguments.

### 2.4 Pyrrhonian Skepticism

Pyrrhonian skepticism is associated with Pyrrho of Elis and the later writings of Sextus Empiricus.

It recommends suspending judgment when arguments appear balanced and certainty cannot be established.

This suspension of judgment is known as *epoché*.

The tradition associates suspension of judgment with *ataraxia*, or tranquillity.

## 3. Descartes and Methodical Doubt

René Descartes used systematic doubt as a method for identifying secure foundations of knowledge.

He considered whether various sources of belief could be doubted.

### 3.1 Doubt About the Senses

Our senses sometimes deceive us.

For example:

- Distant objects appear smaller than they are.
- Straight objects can appear distorted in water.
- Lighting conditions can alter colour perception.

Descartes argued that if the senses sometimes deceive us, they cannot provide absolute certainty in every situation.

### 3.2 The Dream Argument

Descartes asked how we can be completely certain that we are awake rather than dreaming.

Dreams can sometimes feel realistic while they are occurring.

If an experience could occur in a dream, how can we prove that the current experience is not also a dream?

### 3.3 The Evil Demon Hypothesis

Descartes imagined a powerful deceiver capable of producing false experiences and misleading beliefs.

The purpose was to test whether any belief could survive even the most extreme form of doubt.

### 3.4 The Cogito

Despite these doubts, Descartes argued that the act of doubting establishes the existence of a thinking subject.

\[
\text{Cogito, ergo sum}
\]

"I think, therefore I am."

Even if a deceiver exists, there must be something that is being deceived.

## 4. The Problem of the External World

The external world problem concerns whether we can establish that objects exist independently of our experiences.

We directly encounter experiences of objects, but how do we know that these experiences accurately represent an external reality?

For example, a person may experience a convincing visual environment in a dream or simulation.

The philosophical challenge is to establish what justifies belief in a mind-independent world.

## 5. The Problem of Other Minds

The problem of other minds asks how we can know that other individuals possess conscious experiences.

We can observe:

- Their behaviour.
- Their speech.
- Their physical reactions.
- Their neurological activity.

However, we do not directly experience another person's subjective consciousness.

The philosophical challenge is to explain how knowledge of other minds is justified.

One response is the argument from analogy: because other individuals behave similarly to ourselves and possess similar biological structures, it is reasonable to infer that they also experience mental states.

## 6. Hume's Problem of Induction

David Hume challenged the rational justification of inductive reasoning.

Induction involves drawing general conclusions from particular observations.

For example:

1. The Sun has risen every previous morning.
2. Therefore, the Sun will rise tomorrow.

The conclusion is highly reasonable in ordinary life, but it does not follow with deductive necessity.

Hume argued that assuming the future will resemble the past cannot be justified without relying on further inductive reasoning.

This creates the problem of induction.

## 7. Responses to Skepticism

### 7.1 Common-Sense Realism

Common-sense approaches maintain that ordinary beliefs about the external world are reasonable starting points.

Not every belief requires philosophical proof before it can be accepted.

### 7.2 Pragmatic Responses

Pragmatists emphasise the practical role of beliefs and their consequences in experience.

A belief may be rationally maintained when it consistently supports successful interaction with the world.

### 7.3 Transcendental Arguments

Transcendental arguments attempt to show that certain conditions must be true for experience, thought or knowledge to be possible.

For example, an argument may attempt to establish that the experience of an ordered world presupposes certain structures of understanding.

### 7.4 Fallibilism

Fallibilism maintains that knowledge does not require absolute certainty.

A person can possess knowledge even when the possibility of error cannot be completely eliminated.

This approach is particularly important in scientific reasoning.

## 8. Skepticism and Science

Science does not generally require absolute certainty.

Scientific knowledge develops through:

- Observation.
- Experimentation.
- Replication.
- Peer criticism.
- Statistical analysis.
- Revision of theories.

Scientific claims remain open to correction when new evidence becomes available.

This is not necessarily a weakness. It is an important feature of systematic knowledge acquisition.

## 9. Productive and Destructive Skepticism

Productive skepticism questions claims while remaining open to evidence.

Destructive skepticism rejects claims without appropriate consideration of evidence or standards of justification.

A balanced skeptical approach avoids both unquestioning acceptance and automatic rejection.

## 10. Summary

Philosophical skepticism questions the possibility and limits of knowledge.

Important forms include global and local skepticism, Academic skepticism and Pyrrhonian skepticism. Descartes' methodical doubt, Hume's problem of induction, the external world problem and the problem of other minds demonstrate major challenges to knowledge.

Responses include common-sense reasoning, pragmatic approaches, transcendental arguments and fallibilism.
