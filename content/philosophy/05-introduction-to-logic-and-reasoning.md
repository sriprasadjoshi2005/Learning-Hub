# Lesson 5: Introduction to Logic and Reasoning

Logic is the branch of philosophy that studies the principles of correct reasoning. It helps us determine whether conclusions follow from the information or assumptions presented.

Human beings constantly use reasoning when making decisions, solving problems and evaluating claims. However, not all reasoning is reliable. Logic provides methods for identifying errors and constructing stronger arguments.

## 1. Meaning of Logic

Logic is the systematic study of valid inference and the structure of arguments.

An inference is the process of moving from one or more statements to a conclusion.

Example:

- All humans are mortal.
- Socrates is human.
- Therefore, Socrates is mortal.

The first two statements provide the basis for the conclusion.

Logic examines whether the conclusion follows from the premises.

## 2. Arguments

An argument is a collection of statements in which some statements provide support for another statement.

Arguments contain two major components:

### 2.1 Premises

Premises are statements offered as reasons or evidence supporting a conclusion.

### 2.2 Conclusion

A conclusion is the statement that the argument attempts to establish.

Example:

- Premise 1: All planets orbit stars.
- Premise 2: Earth is a planet.
- Conclusion: Earth orbits a star.

## 3. Recognizing Premises and Conclusions

Certain words frequently indicate premises or conclusions.

**Common premise indicators:**

- Because
- Since
- Given that
- As
- Assuming that

**Common conclusion indicators:**

- Therefore
- Thus
- Hence
- Consequently
- It follows that

Example:

"Since all mammals are warm-blooded and whales are mammals, whales are therefore warm-blooded."

Premises:
- All mammals are warm-blooded.
- Whales are mammals.

Conclusion:
- Whales are warm-blooded.

These indicator words are useful but not essential. Some arguments contain no explicit indicator words.

## 4. Statements and Propositions

A proposition is a statement that expresses something capable of being true or false.

Examples:

- The Earth revolves around the Sun.
- All humans are mortal.
- Seven is an even number.

Questions, commands and emotional expressions do not ordinarily function as propositions.

Examples:

- "What time is it?"
- "Close the door."
- "Wonderful!"

These expressions do not directly assert something that can be classified as true or false.

## 5. Basic Logical Connectives

Logical reasoning often uses connectives to combine propositions.

Let:

- \(P\) represent one proposition.
- \(Q\) represent another proposition.

### 5.1 Negation

Negation reverses the truth value of a proposition.

Symbol:

\[
\neg P
\]

If P is true, not P is false.

Example:

P: It is raining.

\[
\neg P
\]

It is not raining.

### 5.2 Conjunction

Conjunction combines two propositions using "and."

Symbol:

\[
P \land Q
\]

It is true only when both propositions are true.

### 5.3 Disjunction

Disjunction represents "or."

Symbol:

\[
P \lor Q
\]

In standard inclusive logic, it is true when at least one proposition is true.

### 5.4 Conditional

A conditional expresses "if P, then Q."

Symbol:

\[
P \rightarrow Q
\]

It is false only when P is true and Q is false.

### 5.5 Biconditional

A biconditional expresses "P if and only if Q."

Symbol:

\[
P \leftrightarrow Q
\]

It is true when P and Q have the same truth value.

## 6. Truth Tables

Truth tables show the truth values of logical expressions under different possible conditions.

| P | Q | P AND Q | P OR Q | P → Q |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | T | F |
| F | T | F | T | T |
| F | F | F | F | T |

Important observations:

- Conjunction requires both propositions to be true.
- Inclusive disjunction requires at least one proposition to be true.
- A conditional is false only when its antecedent is true and its consequent is false.

## 7. Types of Reasoning

Two major forms of reasoning are deduction and induction.

### 7.1 Deductive Reasoning

Deductive reasoning attempts to establish a conclusion that follows necessarily from its premises.

Example:

- All squares have four sides.
- This shape is a square.
- Therefore, this shape has four sides.

If the premises are true and the reasoning is valid, the conclusion must be true.

### 7.2 Inductive Reasoning

Inductive reasoning moves from observations to a generalization or probable conclusion.

Example:

- The Sun has risen every observed morning.
- Therefore, the Sun will probably rise tomorrow.

The conclusion is supported by repeated observations but is not logically guaranteed by them.

## 8. Validity and Truth

Truth and validity are different concepts.

**Truth** concerns whether a proposition accurately represents what is the case.

**Validity** concerns whether an argument's conclusion follows logically from its premises.

Consider:

- All fish are mammals.
- All sharks are fish.
- Therefore, all sharks are mammals.

The first premise is false in reality, but the argument has a valid logical structure.

This illustrates that a valid argument can contain false premises.

A sound argument is both valid and based on true premises.

## 9. Why Logic Matters

Logic is useful in:

- Scientific reasoning.
- Mathematical proofs.
- Legal arguments.
- Programming and computer science.
- Everyday decision-making.
- Identifying misleading claims.
- Constructing philosophical arguments.

It helps distinguish a conclusion that is merely persuasive from one that is properly supported.

## 10. Summary

- Logic studies correct reasoning and valid inference.
- Arguments contain premises and conclusions.
- Propositions are statements capable of being true or false.
- Logical connectives include negation, conjunction, disjunction, conditional and biconditional.
- Truth tables represent the truth conditions of logical expressions.
- Deduction aims at necessary conclusions, while induction supports probable conclusions.
- Truth and validity are distinct.
- A sound argument is valid and has true premises.
