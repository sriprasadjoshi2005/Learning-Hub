# Lesson 35: Applied Ethics and Contemporary Moral Problems

Applied ethics uses philosophical principles and ethical theories to evaluate practical moral problems. It examines how concepts such as rights, duties, justice, welfare and responsibility apply to real situations. This lesson introduces major areas of applied ethics and develops a systematic method for analysing contemporary moral dilemmas.

## 1. Meaning of Applied Ethics

Applied ethics investigates specific moral questions arising in everyday life, professional activities, institutions and technological developments.

It applies ethical reasoning to problems such as:

- Medical treatment.
- Environmental protection.
- Animal welfare.
- Artificial intelligence.
- Business decisions.
- Scientific research.
- Privacy and surveillance.
- Social inequality.

Applied ethics does not simply apply one moral theory mechanically. It often compares several ethical approaches to identify competing values and possible solutions.

## 2. Ethical Analysis of a Practical Problem

A structured approach to applied ethics may involve the following steps:

1. Define the problem clearly.
2. Identify everyone affected.
3. Identify relevant rights and responsibilities.
4. Examine possible actions.
5. Consider short-term and long-term consequences.
6. Apply relevant ethical theories.
7. Identify conflicting values.
8. Justify the most defensible decision.

This approach reduces the influence of impulsive judgments and unsupported assumptions.

## 3. Medical Ethics

Medical ethics examines moral questions arising in healthcare and medical research.

Four widely used principles are:

### 3.1 Autonomy

Autonomy refers to the patient's right to make informed decisions about their own healthcare.

Respecting autonomy requires providing relevant information and avoiding unnecessary coercion.

### 3.2 Beneficence

Beneficence requires acting in ways that promote the well-being of patients.

Medical professionals should consider how treatment can benefit those receiving care.

### 3.3 Non-Maleficence

Non-maleficence means avoiding or minimising unnecessary harm.

It is often expressed through the principle:

"First, do no harm."

The principle does not mean that all medical interventions must be free from risk. Rather, risks should be carefully evaluated and justified.

### 3.4 Justice

Justice concerns fairness in the distribution of healthcare resources, opportunities and treatment.

For example, limited medical resources should be allocated according to defensible criteria rather than arbitrary discrimination.

## 4. Bioethics

Bioethics examines ethical questions involving biology, medicine and life sciences.

Major issues include:

- Genetic modification.
- Human experimentation.
- Organ transplantation.
- Reproductive technologies.
- Stem-cell research.
- End-of-life decisions.
- Genetic privacy.

Bioethical reasoning considers individual rights, social consequences, scientific freedom and possible risks.

## 5. Environmental Ethics

Environmental ethics investigates moral responsibilities towards the natural world.

It asks:

- Do animals have moral rights?
- Does nature possess value independently of human interests?
- What obligations do present generations have towards future generations?
- Is environmental destruction morally acceptable when economically beneficial?

### 5.1 Anthropocentrism

Anthropocentrism places human interests at the centre of moral consideration.

Nature is often valued because of its importance to human survival and well-being.

### 5.2 Biocentrism

Biocentrism maintains that living organisms have moral significance beyond their usefulness to human beings.

### 5.3 Ecocentrism

Ecocentrism gives moral importance to ecological systems, species, habitats and the wider natural environment.

It considers the value of ecological relationships rather than focusing exclusively on individual organisms.

## 6. Animal Ethics

Animal ethics examines whether and how non-human animals deserve moral consideration.

Important issues include:

- Animal experimentation.
- Industrial farming.
- Animal entertainment.
- Wildlife conservation.
- Habitat destruction.

Peter Singer's work is influential in discussions of animal suffering and equal consideration of interests.

A central argument is that the capacity to suffer provides an important reason to consider an individual's interests.

This approach challenges the assumption that human interests automatically outweigh animal interests in every situation.

## 7. Business Ethics

Business ethics investigates moral responsibilities in economic and organisational activities.

Important topics include:

- Fair wages.
- Consumer protection.
- Workplace safety.
- Honest advertising.
- Environmental responsibility.
- Corruption.
- Corporate accountability.

A company may be legally permitted to perform an action while still facing ethical criticism for its effects on workers, consumers or the environment.

## 8. Technology and Digital Ethics

Technological development creates new ethical problems.

### 8.1 Privacy

Digital systems collect and process personal information.

Ethical concerns include:

- Unauthorised data collection.
- Excessive surveillance.
- Data breaches.
- Lack of informed consent.
- Misuse of personal information.

### 8.2 Artificial Intelligence

AI ethics investigates the responsibilities associated with designing and deploying intelligent systems.

Important concerns include:

- Bias and discrimination.
- Transparency.
- Accountability.
- Safety.
- Human oversight.
- Employment effects.
- Misuse of automated decisions.

### 8.3 Digital Manipulation

Online platforms can influence behaviour through recommendation systems, targeted content and persuasive design.

Ethical questions arise when systems manipulate users without meaningful awareness or control.

## 9. Research Ethics

Research ethics establishes standards for responsible scientific investigation.

Important principles include:

- Informed consent.
- Voluntary participation.
- Privacy and confidentiality.
- Avoidance of unnecessary harm.
- Honest reporting of results.
- Proper acknowledgement of contributions.

Researchers have a responsibility to protect participants and maintain the integrity of scientific knowledge.

## 10. Ethical Pluralism

Ethical pluralism recognises that moral decisions may involve several important values that cannot always be reduced to a single principle.

For example, a medical decision may involve:

- Autonomy.
- Beneficence.
- Justice.
- Privacy.
- Safety.

These principles may conflict, requiring careful judgment.

Ethical pluralism encourages consideration of multiple legitimate moral perspectives.

## 11. Summary

Applied ethics examines practical moral problems in healthcare, biology, environmental protection, animal welfare, business, technology and scientific research.

It uses ethical theories and principles to analyse real situations, identify affected parties and justify decisions.

Its importance continues to grow as scientific and technological developments create increasingly complex moral challenges.
