# Lesson 15: Hellenistic Philosophy: Stoicism and Epicureanism

Hellenistic philosophy developed after the conquests of Alexander the Great and the expansion of Greek culture across a large part of the Mediterranean and Near Eastern world.

During this period, philosophers increasingly focused on practical questions concerning happiness, emotional stability, suffering, desire and the art of living.

Two influential philosophical traditions were Stoicism and Epicureanism.

Although both aimed to explain how human beings could live well, they proposed different approaches to happiness and the nature of a good life.

## 1. Historical Background

The Hellenistic period is generally associated with the period following Alexander the Great's death in 323 BCE.

Political and cultural changes created new philosophical environments.

Individuals increasingly encountered diverse cultures, political systems and religious traditions.

Philosophy became an important means of addressing personal uncertainty and developing a stable way of life.

Major Hellenistic schools included:

- Stoicism.
- Epicureanism.
- Skepticism.
- Cynicism.

## 2. Stoicism

Stoicism was founded by Zeno of Citium in the early third century BCE.

Its name comes from the Stoa Poikile, or Painted Porch, in Athens, where Zeno taught.

Stoicism developed into a comprehensive philosophy involving logic, physics and ethics.

Its central practical concern was how to live a virtuous and rational life.

## 3. Major Stoic Philosophers

Important Stoic thinkers included:

**Zeno of Citium:** Founder of Stoicism.

**Cleanthes:** An early leader of the Stoic school.

**Chrysippus:** Developed and systematized Stoic logic and philosophy.

**Seneca:** Roman Stoic philosopher who wrote about ethics, anger and the use of time.

**Epictetus:** Emphasized freedom through rational judgment and self-discipline.

**Marcus Aurelius:** Roman emperor whose *Meditations* contains reflections on virtue, duty and impermanence.

## 4. The Stoic View of Reality

Stoics believed that the universe is governed by a rational ordering principle called logos.

They viewed nature as an interconnected system governed by causal relationships.

Human beings participate in this rational order through their capacity for reason.

Living well therefore requires understanding nature and acting in accordance with reason.

## 5. The Dichotomy of Control

One of the most recognizable Stoic ideas concerns the distinction between what is within our control and what is not.

Epictetus emphasized that some things depend on us, while others do not.

### 5.1 Within Our Control

Examples include:

- Our judgments.
- Our intentions.
- Our choices.
- Our responses.
- Our efforts.

### 5.2 Not Fully Within Our Control

Examples include:

- Other people's opinions.
- Past events.
- Unexpected natural events.
- External recognition.
- Many outcomes of our actions.

Stoicism encourages individuals to focus on their own judgments and actions rather than becoming excessively attached to external circumstances.

This does not mean ignoring external problems. It means distinguishing between what can be influenced and what cannot be directly controlled.

## 6. Stoic Virtue Ethics

Stoicism considers virtue the central component of a good life.

The four cardinal virtues are:

1. Wisdom.
2. Courage.
3. Justice.
4. Temperance.

A person becomes morally excellent by developing these qualities.

External possessions, wealth, reputation and physical comfort may be preferred, but they are not the ultimate basis of happiness.

## 7. Stoic Emotions and Judgments

Stoic philosophy examines the relationship between judgments and emotional responses.

According to Stoic thinkers, destructive emotional disturbances often involve mistaken judgments about events.

For example:

An individual loses a valuable possession.

The event itself is external, but the person's interpretation may determine whether they respond with excessive distress, acceptance or practical action.

Stoicism does not require emotional numbness.

It aims to develop rational responses and freedom from destructive passions.

## 8. Epicureanism

Epicureanism was founded by Epicurus in Athens around 306 BCE.

It is a philosophical tradition concerned with achieving a pleasant and peaceful life through understanding nature, managing desires and reducing unnecessary suffering.

Epicurus argued that pleasure is the highest good, but his conception of pleasure differs from unrestricted indulgence.

## 9. The Epicurean Understanding of Pleasure

Epicurus emphasized two important conditions:

### 9.1 Aponia

Aponia refers to the absence of bodily pain.

### 9.2 Ataraxia

Ataraxia refers to freedom from mental disturbance or anxiety.

Together, these ideas describe a state of tranquility and well-being.

Epicurean pleasure emphasizes stability rather than continuous excitement.

## 10. Classification of Desires

Epicurus distinguished between different types of desires.

| Type of desire | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Natural and necessary | Essential for basic well-being | Food, water, shelter |
| Natural but unnecessary | Natural but not essential | Luxurious food |
| Empty or vain | Often unlimited and socially manufactured | Excessive wealth, fame |

Epicurus argued that unnecessary desires can produce dissatisfaction when individuals become dependent on them.

Understanding desires helps individuals avoid unnecessary suffering.

## 11. Epicurean Philosophy and Friendship

Epicurus considered friendship extremely important for a happy life.

Friendship provides:

- Emotional support.
- Trust.
- Security.
- Companionship.
- Shared enjoyment.

He also emphasized simple living and intellectual reflection.

## 12. Epicurean Views on Death

Epicurus argued that death should not be feared as an experience of suffering.

His reasoning was that when a person exists, death is not present, and when death occurs, the person is no longer experiencing anything.

This argument challenges the assumption that death must be feared as a personal experience.

Philosophers have raised objections concerning deprivation, lost opportunities and the value of continued existence.

## 13. Stoicism and Epicureanism Compared

| Feature | Stoicism | Epicureanism |
|---|---|---|
| Founder | Zeno of Citium | Epicurus |
| Central ethical aim | Virtue | Pleasure understood as tranquility |
| View of emotions | Examine judgments and avoid destructive passions | Reduce fear, disturbance and unnecessary desires |
| External goods | Not essential for virtue | Can contribute to comfort but should not dominate life |
| Ideal condition | Rational and virtuous living | Ataraxia and aponia |
| Important practice | Self-discipline and rational judgment | Managing desires and understanding nature |

## 14. Other Hellenistic Schools

### 14.1 Cynicism

Cynicism emphasized living according to nature and rejecting unnecessary social conventions and material possessions.

Diogenes of Sinope became its most famous representative.

### 14.2 Skepticism

Ancient skepticism questioned whether human beings could achieve certain knowledge.

Pyrrho of Elis is associated with Pyrrhonian skepticism.

Skeptics often emphasized suspending judgment when evidence did not justify certainty.

## 15. Practical Applications

Hellenistic philosophy remains relevant to everyday life.

**Stoic application:**

Before reacting to an unexpected problem, distinguish between the event itself and the judgments or responses within your control.

**Epicurean application:**

Examine whether a desire genuinely contributes to long-term well-being or merely creates unnecessary dependence.

Both traditions encourage reflection on what actually contributes to a good life.

## 16. Summary

- Hellenistic philosophy developed after the expansion of Greek culture.
- Stoicism was founded by Zeno of Citium.
- Stoicism emphasizes virtue, reason and the distinction between controllable and uncontrollable events.
- Epicureanism was founded by Epicurus.
- Epicureanism emphasizes stable pleasure, tranquility and the management of desires.
- Aponia means absence of bodily pain.
- Ataraxia means freedom from mental disturbance.
- Cynicism and skepticism were other important Hellenistic traditions.
