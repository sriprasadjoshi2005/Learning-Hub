## Learning objective

Translate arithmetic patterns into algebra, simplify expressions and use standard identities for expansion and factorisation.

## Core concepts

- A variable represents a number. A term is a product of numbers and variables; like terms have identical variable parts.
- The distributive law is a(b+c)=ab+ac. It is the foundation of expansion and factorisation.
- Important identities: (a+b)^2=a²+2ab+b²; (a−b)^2=a²−2ab+b²; a²−b²=(a−b)(a+b).
- Also, (a+b)^3=a³+3a²b+3ab²+b³ and (a−b)^3=a³−3a²b+3ab²−b³.
- Factorisation reverses expansion. Look first for a common factor, then identities, grouping or suitable quadratic methods.

## Essential formulae and results

(a±b)²=a²±2ab+b²; a²−b²=(a−b)(a+b).

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** Expand (x+3)(x−2) = x²−2x+3x−6 = x²+x−6.
**Example 2.** Factorise x²−9 = (x−3)(x+3), using the difference of two squares.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- (a+b)^2 is not a²+b².
- Only like terms combine: 3x + 2x² cannot be simplified to 5x³.
- When taking a common factor, divide every term by it.

## Exam approach

1. Identify the topic and write down the relevant definition or formula.
2. Check the conditions: domain, non-zero denominators, interval, sign and units where applicable.
3. Show the essential algebraic steps instead of jumping to an answer.
4. Substitute the result into the original condition whenever extraneous solutions are possible.
5. Keep exact values (fractions, radicals and π) until a decimal approximation is requested.
