title: Electromagnetic Waves and the Electromagnetic Spectrum

Electromagnetic waves are disturbances consisting of changing electric and magnetic fields that propagate through space.

They are responsible for the transmission of light, radio signals, microwaves, infrared radiation, ultraviolet radiation, X-rays and gamma rays.

Electromagnetic theory connects electricity, magnetism and optics.

## 1. Introduction to Electromagnetic Waves

An electromagnetic wave consists of oscillating electric and magnetic fields.

In a plane electromagnetic wave travelling through vacuum:
- The electric field is perpendicular to the direction of propagation.
- The magnetic field is perpendicular to the direction of propagation.
- The electric and magnetic fields are perpendicular to each other.
- Both fields oscillate in phase.

Electromagnetic waves are transverse waves.

## 2. Origin of Electromagnetic Waves

A stationary electric charge produces an electric field.

A steady current produces a magnetic field.

However, accelerating electric charges can generate changing electric and magnetic fields that propagate as electromagnetic radiation.

Changing electric fields and changing magnetic fields are connected through Maxwell's equations.

An electromagnetic wave does not require a material medium.

It can travel through a vacuum.

## 3. Speed of Electromagnetic Waves

In vacuum, electromagnetic waves travel at the speed of light.

c = 1/sqrt(μ0ε0)

Where:
- μ0 = permeability of free space
- ε0 = permittivity of free space

The approximate value is:

c = 3 × 10^8 m/s

The speed of light is a fundamental constant of nature.

In a material medium, the wave speed depends on the electromagnetic properties of the medium.

## 4. Relationship Between Speed, Frequency and Wavelength

For any periodic wave:

v = fλ

For electromagnetic waves in vacuum:

c = fλ

Where:
- c = speed of light
- f = frequency
- λ = wavelength

Therefore:

λ = c/f

For a fixed propagation medium, frequency and wavelength are inversely proportional.

When an electromagnetic wave enters another medium, its frequency remains unchanged at the boundary, while its wavelength and speed may change.

## 5. Electromagnetic Wave Properties

Important properties include:

1. Electromagnetic waves are transverse.
2. They can travel through vacuum.
3. They carry energy and momentum.
4. They travel at approximately 3 × 10^8 m/s in vacuum.
5. They can undergo reflection, refraction, diffraction and interference.
6. They can be polarised.
7. Their frequency is determined by the source.
8. They are produced by accelerating electric charges.

## 6. Electromagnetic Spectrum

The electromagnetic spectrum is the complete range of electromagnetic radiation arranged according to frequency or wavelength.

From lowest frequency to highest frequency:

1. Radio waves.
2. Microwaves.
3. Infrared radiation.
4. Visible light.
5. Ultraviolet radiation.
6. X-rays.
7. Gamma rays.

As frequency increases:
- Wavelength decreases.
- Photon energy increases.

The energy of a photon is:

E = hf

Where h is Planck's constant.

## 7. Radio Waves

Radio waves occupy the low-frequency, long-wavelength region of the electromagnetic spectrum.

Applications include:
- Radio broadcasting.
- Television transmission.
- Wireless communication.
- Navigation systems.
- Communication with aircraft and spacecraft.

Different frequency bands have different propagation characteristics.

## 8. Microwaves

Microwaves have higher frequencies and shorter wavelengths than conventional radio waves.

Applications include:
- Radar systems.
- Satellite communication.
- Wireless networking.
- Microwave ovens.
- Certain medical and industrial heating systems.

In a microwave oven, electromagnetic radiation interacts strongly with certain polar molecules, producing heating through molecular motion and dielectric losses.

## 9. Infrared Radiation

Infrared radiation lies beyond the red end of visible light.

It is strongly associated with thermal radiation from ordinary-temperature objects.

Applications include:
- Thermal imaging.
- Remote controls.
- Night-vision systems.
- Temperature measurement.
- Optical communication.
- Heating applications.

Objects emit infrared radiation according to their temperature and surface properties.

## 10. Visible Light

Visible light is the portion of the electromagnetic spectrum detectable by the human eye.

Its approximate wavelength range is 400 nm to 700 nm.

The approximate colour sequence from shorter to longer wavelengths is:

Violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange and red.

Violet light has higher frequency than red light.

Visible light is responsible for human vision and conventional optical imaging.

## 11. Ultraviolet Radiation

Ultraviolet radiation has a shorter wavelength than visible violet light.

Applications include:
- Sterilisation.
- Fluorescence.
- Forensic examination.
- Certain medical treatments.
- Industrial curing processes.

Excessive ultraviolet exposure can damage skin and eyes.

The atmosphere, particularly ozone, absorbs a substantial portion of harmful solar ultraviolet radiation.

## 12. X-Rays

X-rays are high-frequency electromagnetic waves.

They can penetrate many materials and are used in medical imaging.

Applications include:
- Radiography.
- Computed tomography.
- Airport security scanning.
- Industrial inspection.
- Crystallography.

X-rays are ionising radiation and can damage biological tissue.

Their use requires suitable exposure control.

## 13. Gamma Rays

Gamma rays occupy the highest-frequency region of the electromagnetic spectrum.

They are commonly associated with nuclear transitions and certain radioactive processes.

Applications include:
- Cancer radiotherapy.
- Sterilisation of medical equipment.
- Nuclear research.
- Industrial inspection.
- Astrophysical observations.

Gamma rays are highly penetrating and ionising.

Appropriate shielding and radiation protection are essential.

## 14. Photon Energy

Electromagnetic radiation can be described in terms of photons.

The energy of one photon is:

E = hf

Using c = fλ:

E = hc/λ

Where:
- E = photon energy
- h = Planck's constant
- f = frequency
- λ = wavelength

Planck's constant:

h = 6.626 × 10^-34 J s

Higher-frequency photons carry more energy.

## 15. Momentum of Electromagnetic Radiation

A photon has momentum despite having zero rest mass.

Photon momentum is:

p = E/c

Using E = hf:

p = h/λ

Electromagnetic radiation can therefore exert radiation pressure when it transfers momentum to a surface.

This principle is relevant to solar sails and precision optical systems.

## 16. Polarisation

Polarisation is the restriction of the oscillations of a transverse wave to a particular orientation.

Electromagnetic waves can be polarised because their electric field oscillations are transverse to the direction of propagation.

Polarisation is used in:
- Sunglasses.
- LCD displays.
- Photography.
- Optical instruments.
- Communication systems.

Unpolarised light contains electric field oscillations in many transverse orientations.

## 17. Numerical Applications

**Example 1: Wavelength**

A radio wave has frequency 100 MHz. Find its wavelength in vacuum.

λ = c/f

λ = (3 × 10^8)/(100 × 10^6)

λ = 3 m

**Example 2: Photon energy**

Find the energy of a photon with frequency 5 × 10^14 Hz.

E = hf

E = 6.626 × 10^-34 × 5 × 10^14

E = 3.313 × 10^-19 J

**Example 3: Photon momentum**

A photon has wavelength 500 nm.

p = h/λ

p = (6.626 × 10^-34)/(500 × 10^-9)

p ≈ 1.325 × 10^-27 kg m/s

## Common Exam Traps

- Electromagnetic waves do not require a material medium.
- Electromagnetic waves are transverse.
- Electric and magnetic fields are perpendicular to each other.
- In vacuum, all electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed.
- Frequency and wavelength are inversely proportional in a fixed medium.
- Frequency does not change when light crosses a stationary boundary.
- Photon energy increases with frequency.
- Gamma rays have higher frequency than X-rays.
- Radio waves have longer wavelengths than microwaves.
- Polarisation is a property of transverse waves.
- Electromagnetic radiation carries both energy and momentum.
