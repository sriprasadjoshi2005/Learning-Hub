title: Nuclear Reactions, Fission, Fusion and Nuclear Energy

Nuclear reactions involve changes in atomic nuclei through interactions with particles or other nuclei. These reactions can release or absorb enormous amounts of energy because nuclear binding energies are much larger than ordinary chemical bond energies.

Two particularly important processes are nuclear fission and nuclear fusion.

## 1. Nuclear Reactions

A nuclear reaction occurs when an incoming particle interacts with a nucleus, producing one or more outgoing particles or nuclei.

A general nuclear reaction is represented as:

\[
a+X\rightarrow Y+b
\]

Where:
- a = incoming particle
- X = target nucleus
- Y = product nucleus
- b = emitted particle

Nuclear equations must obey conservation laws.

## 2. Conservation Laws in Nuclear Reactions

Important conservation principles include:

**Conservation of electric charge:**

Total electric charge before and after the reaction must be equal.

**Conservation of nucleon number:**

The total number of nucleons must be conserved.

**Conservation of energy:**

Total energy, including rest-mass energy and kinetic energy, is conserved.

**Conservation of momentum:**

Total momentum remains constant in an isolated system.

**Conservation of angular momentum:**

Total angular momentum is conserved.

In particle interactions, additional quantum numbers may also be relevant.

## 3. Q-Value of a Nuclear Reaction

The Q-value represents the net energy released or absorbed during a nuclear reaction.

\[
Q=(m_{\text{initial}}-m_{\text{final}})c^2
\]

If:

\[
Q>0
\]

The reaction releases energy and is exothermic.

If:

\[
Q<0
\]

The reaction absorbs energy and is endothermic.

When masses are measured in atomic mass units:

\[
Q(\text{MeV})=\Delta m(\text{u})\times931.5
\]

The Q-value includes changes in rest-mass energy and is related to the kinetic energies of the reaction products.

## 4. Nuclear Fission

Nuclear fission is the splitting of a heavy atomic nucleus into two or more lighter nuclei.

It often releases additional neutrons and a large amount of energy.

For example, uranium-235 can undergo neutron-induced fission:

\[
{}^{235}_{92}U+{}^1_0n
\rightarrow
{}^{141}_{56}Ba+{}^{92}_{36}Kr+3{}^1_0n+\text{Energy}
\]

This is one possible fission channel.

The exact products can vary.

## 5. Why Fission Releases Energy

Very heavy nuclei generally have lower binding energy per nucleon than medium-mass nuclei.

When a heavy nucleus splits into medium-mass nuclei, the resulting products can have greater total binding energy.

The difference is released as energy.

A typical fission event releases approximately 200 MeV of energy.

This is vastly greater than the energy released by a typical chemical reaction involving individual atoms.

## 6. Chain Reactions

A fission event may release neutrons capable of causing further fission events.

This creates a chain reaction.

Three broad situations are possible:

**Subcritical:**

The neutron population decreases over successive generations.

**Critical:**

The neutron population remains approximately constant.

**Supercritical:**

The neutron population increases over successive generations.

The neutron multiplication factor is represented by:

\[
k=\frac{\text{Neutrons in one generation}}{\text{Neutrons in previous generation}}
\]

For an idealised system:
- k < 1: subcritical
- k = 1: critical
- k > 1: supercritical

## 7. Nuclear Reactors

A nuclear reactor is designed to sustain a controlled nuclear fission chain reaction and convert the released energy into useful power.

Important components include:

**Fuel:**
Contains fissile material such as uranium-235 or plutonium-239.

**Moderator:**
Slows down fast neutrons in reactors that use thermal-neutron fission.

**Control rods:**
Absorb neutrons and help regulate the reaction.

**Coolant:**
Transfers heat away from the reactor core.

**Shielding:**
Reduces radiation exposure.

**Containment structure:**
Provides an engineered barrier against the release of radioactive material.

## 8. Energy Conversion in a Nuclear Power Plant

A typical nuclear power station follows this sequence:

1. Nuclear fission releases energy.
2. The reactor transfers heat to a working fluid.
3. Steam is produced in a steam-based power system.
4. Steam rotates a turbine.
5. The turbine drives an electrical generator.
6. The steam is condensed and recirculated.

The process converts nuclear energy into thermal energy, mechanical energy and finally electrical energy.

## 9. Nuclear Fusion

Nuclear fusion is the process in which two light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus.

A major example is deuterium-tritium fusion:

\[
{}^2_1H+{}^3_1H
\rightarrow{}^4_2He+{}^1_0n+\text{Energy}
\]

The reaction releases approximately 17.6 MeV.

Fusion powers the Sun and other stars.

## 10. Why Fusion Requires Extremely High Temperatures

Both reacting nuclei are positively charged.

They repel each other through the electrostatic force.

To approach sufficiently close for the strong nuclear interaction to become important, nuclei require substantial kinetic energy.

Very high temperatures help provide this energy.

Quantum tunnelling also allows some nuclei to overcome the effective Coulomb barrier even when their classical kinetic energy is insufficient.

## 11. Confinement in Fusion Reactors

A major challenge in controlled fusion is maintaining a sufficiently hot and dense plasma for long enough.

Two major approaches are:

**Magnetic confinement:**

Uses magnetic fields to confine electrically charged plasma.

Tokamaks and stellarators are examples.

**Inertial confinement:**

Uses intense laser or particle beams to compress small fuel targets rapidly.

Both approaches attempt to achieve conditions that permit substantial fusion reactions.

## 12. Nuclear Fusion in Stars

Stars generate energy through nuclear fusion.

In stars similar to the Sun, the proton-proton chain is an important fusion process.

The overall transformation can be represented approximately as:

\[
4{}^1_1H\rightarrow{}^4_2He+\text{Energy}
\]

The mass of the resulting helium nucleus is less than the combined initial mass of the participating hydrogen nuclei.

The mass difference is converted into energy.

In more massive stars, the carbon-nitrogen-oxygen cycle becomes an important hydrogen-fusion pathway.

## 13. Fission Versus Fusion

| Feature | Fission | Fusion |
|---|---|---|
| Basic process | Heavy nucleus splits | Light nuclei combine |
| Typical fuel | Uranium-235 | Deuterium and tritium |
| Conditions | Can be sustained using neutron reactions | Requires extremely high temperature and suitable confinement |
| Energy source | Increase in total binding energy | Increase in total binding energy |
| Waste | Produces radioactive fission products | Produces fewer long-lived fission products, but neutron activation remains a concern |
| Current use | Commercial nuclear power | Experimental and developing power systems |

## 14. Nuclear Energy and Environmental Considerations

Nuclear power produces electricity with low direct carbon dioxide emissions during operation.

However, nuclear energy involves important challenges:
- Radioactive waste management.
- Reactor safety.
- High construction and decommissioning costs.
- Uranium mining and fuel processing.
- Long-term storage and monitoring.

Nuclear energy should therefore be evaluated through its complete lifecycle rather than only its electricity-generation stage.

## 15. Worked Numerical Example

A nuclear reaction has an initial total mass of 236.052 u and a final total mass of 236.000 u.

Calculate the energy released.

Mass defect:

\[
\Delta m=236.052-236.000
\]

\[
\Delta m=0.052\text{ u}
\]

Energy released:

\[
Q=0.052\times931.5
\]

\[
\boxed{Q\approx48.44\text{ MeV}}
\]

The positive Q-value indicates that energy is released.

## Common Exam Traps

- Fission means splitting; fusion means combining.
- Both processes can release energy because of changes in binding energy.
- A moderator slows neutrons; a control rod absorbs neutrons.
- Fusion requires high-temperature plasma conditions.
- Nuclear reactions conserve total energy, not necessarily the number of particles of each individual type.
- A positive Q-value indicates an energy-releasing reaction.
- Nuclear power plants generally use heat to drive turbines and generators.
