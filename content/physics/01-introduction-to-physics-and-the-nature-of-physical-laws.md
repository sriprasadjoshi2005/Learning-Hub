Physics is the branch of natural science that studies matter, energy, motion, forces, space, time, and the fundamental interactions that govern the universe.

It attempts to explain how nature behaves, from the motion of planets and the formation of stars to the behaviour of atoms, electrons, and subatomic particles.

Physics is not simply a collection of formulas. It is a systematic way of understanding nature through observation, experimentation, mathematical reasoning, and the development of scientific models.

## 1. Meaning and Scope of Physics

The word physics originates from the Greek word *physis*, meaning nature.

Physics investigates fundamental questions such as:

- Why do objects fall towards Earth?
- How does light travel?
- Why do planets revolve around the Sun?
- How does electricity flow through a circuit?
- What determines the behaviour of atoms?
- How are matter and energy related?
- What happens to space and time at extremely high speeds?

Physics seeks explanations that can be tested experimentally and expressed through mathematical relationships.

### Major branches of physics

| Branch | Main area of study |
|---|---|
| Mechanics | Motion, forces and equilibrium |
| Thermodynamics | Heat, temperature and energy transfer |
| Electromagnetism | Electric charges, currents and magnetic fields |
| Optics | Light and its behaviour |
| Acoustics | Sound and mechanical waves |
| Atomic physics | Structure and behaviour of atoms |
| Nuclear physics | Atomic nuclei and nuclear reactions |
| Quantum physics | Behaviour of matter and energy at microscopic scales |
| Relativity | Space, time, gravity and high-speed phenomena |
| Astrophysics | Physical properties of stars, planets and galaxies |

These branches are interconnected. For example, understanding how a star produces energy requires knowledge of gravity, thermodynamics, nuclear physics, and electromagnetic radiation.

## 2. Physics and Other Sciences

Physics provides fundamental principles used in many other scientific disciplines.

**Physics and Chemistry:**
Chemistry studies the composition, structure, properties and transformations of matter. Atomic physics and quantum mechanics explain electron arrangements, chemical bonding and the behaviour of atoms.

**Physics and Biology:**
Biophysics applies physical principles to biological systems. Examples include blood circulation, nerve impulses, vision, hearing and the mechanics of muscles.

**Physics and Mathematics:**
Mathematics provides the language used to express physical relationships. Algebra, geometry, trigonometry, calculus, vectors and differential equations are essential tools in advanced physics.

**Physics and Engineering:**
Engineering applies physical principles to design and construct machines, buildings, electrical systems, communication devices and transportation systems.

## 3. The Scientific Method in Physics

Physics develops reliable explanations through systematic investigation.

The scientific method commonly involves the following stages:

1. Observation: Identify and observe a natural phenomenon.
2. Question: Formulate a question about the phenomenon.
3. Hypothesis: Propose a possible explanation.
4. Prediction: Determine what should happen if the hypothesis is correct.
5. Experimentation: Design and perform experiments to test the prediction.
6. Analysis: Examine measurements and experimental results.
7. Conclusion: Determine whether the evidence supports the hypothesis.
8. Refinement: Modify the explanation or develop further experiments when necessary.

For example, suppose an object falls towards the ground.

- Observation: Objects released near Earth's surface generally fall downward.
- Question: Does the mass of an object determine how quickly it falls?
- Hypothesis: Heavier objects might fall faster.
- Experiment: Drop objects of different masses under controlled conditions.
- Analysis: Compare their acceleration while accounting for air resistance.
- Conclusion: In ideal free fall, all objects experience the same gravitational acceleration regardless of mass.

Scientific explanations must remain open to testing and revision.

## 4. Physical Laws, Principles and Theories

Scientific laws, principles and theories have related but distinct meanings.

**Physical law:**

A physical law describes a consistent relationship or regularity observed in nature.

Examples:
- Newton's laws of motion.
- Law of universal gravitation.
- Conservation of energy.
- Ohm's law under appropriate conditions.

**Physical principle:**

A principle is a fundamental rule or idea used to understand and predict physical behaviour.

Examples:
- Principle of conservation of momentum.
- Archimedes' principle.
- Principle of superposition.

**Scientific theory:**

A scientific theory is a well-supported explanatory framework that connects observations, experiments and established principles.

Examples:
- Theory of relativity.
- Quantum theory.
- Kinetic theory of gases.

A scientific theory is not merely an untested guess. It is supported by extensive experimental evidence and successful predictions.

## 5. Physical Models and Idealisation

A physical model is a simplified representation of a real physical system.

Models allow scientists to study complicated phenomena by concentrating on the most important variables.

Examples:

**Point mass:**

An object is represented as a point containing its entire mass. Its dimensions are ignored when they are insignificant compared with the distances involved.

This model is useful when studying planetary motion.

**Ideal string:**

A string is assumed to have negligible mass and to be inextensible.

This simplifies problems involving connected bodies and pulleys.

**Ideal gas:**

A gas is modelled as a collection of particles with negligible individual volume and negligible intermolecular forces, except during collisions.

**Frictionless surface:**

A surface is assumed to exert no frictional force.

This allows the effects of other forces to be studied independently.

Models are useful approximations, not necessarily exact descriptions of reality.

## 6. Fundamental Interactions in Nature

Modern physics recognises four fundamental interactions.

| Interaction | Relative strength (approx.) | Range | Main role |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Strong nuclear | 1 | Very short | Binds quarks and helps bind atomic nuclei |
| Electromagnetic | 10^-2 | Infinite | Acts between electrically charged particles |
| Weak nuclear | 10^-13 | Very short | Responsible for processes such as beta decay |
| Gravitational | 10^-38 | Infinite | Acts between objects with mass-energy |

The approximate strengths are illustrative and depend on the energy scale and convention used.

### Strong nuclear interaction

The strong interaction binds quarks together inside protons and neutrons. Its residual effect also helps hold atomic nuclei together.

### Electromagnetic interaction

This interaction acts between electrically charged particles. It is responsible for electric forces, magnetism, light and chemical bonding.

### Weak nuclear interaction

The weak interaction is responsible for certain transformations of subatomic particles, including beta decay.

### Gravitational interaction

Gravity acts between objects possessing mass-energy. It governs falling objects, planetary motion, stars and large-scale cosmic structures.

## 7. Microscopic and Macroscopic Physics

Physics studies nature across a wide range of scales.

**Macroscopic physics:**

Deals with objects and systems that can generally be studied without considering individual atoms.

Examples:
- Cars moving along roads.
- Falling objects.
- Ocean waves.
- Electrical circuits.
- Planetary motion.

**Microscopic physics:**

Deals with atoms, molecules, electrons, nuclei and elementary particles.

Examples:
- Electron energy levels.
- Atomic spectra.
- Quantum tunnelling.
- Nuclear reactions.
- Particle interactions.

Classical physics is often sufficient for everyday macroscopic phenomena. Quantum physics becomes necessary at microscopic scales, while relativity becomes important for extremely high speeds and strong gravitational fields.

## 8. Classical Physics and Modern Physics

**Classical physics** includes the theories developed primarily before the twentieth century.

Its major areas include:
- Newtonian mechanics.
- Classical thermodynamics.
- Classical electromagnetism.
- Geometrical and wave optics.

**Modern physics** developed to explain phenomena that classical theories could not adequately describe.

Its major areas include:
- Special and general relativity.
- Quantum mechanics.
- Atomic and nuclear physics.
- Particle physics.
- Quantum field theory.

Classical physics remains extremely accurate within its applicable range. Modern physics extends our understanding into conditions where classical approximations become insufficient.

## 9. Conservation Laws in Physics

Conservation laws state that certain physical quantities remain constant in an isolated system, provided the relevant conditions are satisfied.

Important conservation laws include:

**Conservation of energy:**

Energy cannot be created or destroyed in an isolated system. It can be transferred or transformed from one form to another.

**Conservation of linear momentum:**

The total linear momentum of an isolated system remains constant when the net external force is zero.

**Conservation of angular momentum:**

The total angular momentum of an isolated system remains constant when the net external torque is zero.

**Conservation of electric charge:**

The total electric charge of an isolated system remains constant.

These principles are among the most powerful tools for solving physics problems.

## 10. Physics in Everyday Life and Technology

Physics forms the foundation of numerous modern technologies.

| Application | Relevant physics |
|---|---|
| Mobile phones | Electromagnetism, semiconductor physics and quantum mechanics |
| Electric motors | Magnetic forces and electromagnetic induction |
| Solar panels | Photoelectric effects and semiconductor physics |

| Satellites | Gravitation and orbital mechanics |

Understanding physics makes it possible to explain not only how these technologies work but also the principles behind their design.

## 11. Limitations and Development of Physical Knowledge

Physical models and theories have domains of validity.

For example:

- Newtonian mechanics works extremely well for ordinary speeds and everyday gravitational conditions.
- Special relativity becomes important when speeds approach the speed of light.
- General relativity provides a more complete description of gravity, especially in strong gravitational fields.
- Quantum mechanics describes microscopic phenomena where classical physics fails.

Scientific knowledge develops through new observations, better measurements and improved theories.

A successful physical theory must explain existing evidence and make predictions that can be tested.

## Common Exam Traps

- A scientific theory is not an untested guess; it is a well-supported explanatory framework.
- A scientific law describes a regularity, whereas a theory explains physical phenomena.
- Gravity is the weakest of the four fundamental interactions in the usual comparison of their strengths.
- Classical mechanics is not useless simply because modern physics exists; it remains accurate within its domain.
- Physical models simplify reality and may ignore effects that are insignificant under specified conditions.
- Energy conservation does not mean that all forms of energy remain individually constant; energy can transform between forms.
