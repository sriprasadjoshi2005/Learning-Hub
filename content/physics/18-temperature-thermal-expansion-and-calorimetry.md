Thermal physics examines temperature, heat, internal energy, and the behaviour of matter when thermal energy is transferred.

This lesson introduces temperature scales, thermal equilibrium, thermal expansion, specific heat capacity, latent heat, and calorimetry.

## 1. Temperature and Heat

Temperature measures the thermal state of a system and determines the direction of spontaneous heat transfer between systems in thermal contact.

Heat is energy transferred between systems because of a temperature difference.

Heat is not a substance stored inside an object. It is energy in transit.

The SI unit of heat is the joule (J).

Temperature is measured in kelvin (K) in the SI system.

## 2. Thermal Equilibrium

Two systems are in thermal equilibrium when there is no net heat transfer between them if placed in thermal contact.

They have the same temperature.

The zeroth law of thermodynamics states:

If system A is in thermal equilibrium with system C, and system B is also in thermal equilibrium with system C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other.

This law provides the foundation for temperature measurement.

## 3. Temperature Scales

Common temperature scales include Celsius, Kelvin, and Fahrenheit.

### Celsius and Kelvin

\[
T(K)=t(^\circ C)+273.15
\]

\[
t(^\circ C)=T(K)-273.15
\]

Absolute zero is:

\[
0\,K=-273.15^\circ C
\]

Kelvin is an absolute temperature scale.

### Celsius and Fahrenheit

\[
F=\frac{9}{5}C+32
\]

\[
C=\frac{5}{9}(F-32)
\]

A temperature interval of 1 kelvin has the same magnitude as an interval of 1 degree Celsius.

## 4. Thermal Expansion

Thermal expansion is the increase in the dimensions of a material when its temperature rises.

Most materials expand when heated and contract when cooled.

Expansion occurs because the average separation between constituent particles generally increases with temperature.

### Linear Expansion

For a solid rod:

\[
\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T
\]

Where:
- alpha = coefficient of linear expansion
- L0 = original length
- delta T = temperature change

The final length is:

\[
L=L_0(1+\alpha\Delta T)
\]

The SI unit of alpha is K^-1.

### Area Expansion

\[
\Delta A=\beta A_0\Delta T
\]

For an isotropic solid with small expansion:

\[
\beta\approx2\alpha
\]

### Volume Expansion

\[
\Delta V=\gamma V_0\Delta T
\]

For an isotropic solid:

\[
\gamma\approx3\alpha
\]

These approximations apply to small temperature changes and ordinary isotropic solids.

## 5. Anomalous Expansion of Water

Water behaves unusually between 0 degrees Celsius and 4 degrees Celsius.

When water is heated from 0 degrees Celsius to 4 degrees Celsius:
- Its volume decreases.
- Its density increases.

At approximately 4 degrees Celsius, water has maximum density under ordinary atmospheric pressure.

Above 4 degrees Celsius, it expands normally when heated.

This phenomenon is important for aquatic ecosystems in cold climates because ice floats and water beneath it can remain liquid.

## 6. Heat Capacity

Heat capacity is the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of an entire body by one kelvin.

\[
C=\frac{Q}{\Delta T}
\]

SI unit:

\[
J/K
\]

Heat capacity depends on the mass and material of the object.

## 7. Specific Heat Capacity

Specific heat capacity is the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 kelvin.

\[
c=\frac{Q}{m\Delta T}
\]

Therefore:

\[
Q=mc\Delta T
\]

SI unit:

\[
J/(kg\,K)
\]

A material with high specific heat capacity requires more energy for the same temperature increase.

Water has a relatively high specific heat capacity, which helps moderate temperatures in coastal regions and large bodies of water.

## 8. Calorimetry

Calorimetry is the measurement of heat transferred during physical or chemical processes.

In an ideal insulated system:

\[
Q_{\text{lost}}=Q_{\text{gained}}
\]

This is based on conservation of energy.

For two substances:

\[
m_1c_1(T_1-T_f)=m_2c_2(T_f-T_2)
\]

The equation assumes the first substance is initially hotter and no energy is lost to the surroundings.

Real calorimeters may require corrections for the heat absorbed by the container and other components.

## 9. Change of State

Matter can change between solid, liquid, and gas.

Common changes include:
- Melting: solid to liquid.
- Freezing: liquid to solid.
- Vaporisation: liquid to gas.
- Condensation: gas to liquid.
- Sublimation: solid directly to gas.
- Deposition: gas directly to solid.

During a phase change of a pure substance at constant pressure, temperature remains approximately constant even though energy is transferred.

## 10. Latent Heat

Latent heat is energy transferred during a change of state without a temperature change.

\[
Q=mL
\]

Where:
- Q = heat transferred
- m = mass
- L = specific latent heat

Types include:
- Specific latent heat of fusion.
- Specific latent heat of vaporisation.

The SI unit of specific latent heat is J/kg.

Vaporisation generally requires more energy per kilogram than melting for a given substance.

## 11. Heating Curves

A heating curve shows temperature against heat supplied.

Its sloping sections represent temperature changes within a phase.

Its horizontal sections represent phase changes at approximately constant temperature.

For example, heating ice initially increases its temperature. At the melting point, added energy changes ice into water. Once melting is complete, further energy raises the water temperature.

## 12. Modes of Heat Transfer

**Conduction:** Heat transfer through microscopic interactions without bulk movement of the material.

**Convection:** Heat transfer through the bulk movement of a fluid.

**Radiation:** Heat transfer through electromagnetic waves; it does not require a material medium.

Examples:
- A metal spoon warming in hot tea: conduction.
- Warm air rising: convection.
- Energy from the Sun reaching Earth: radiation.

## 13. Worked Numerical Examples

**Example 1: Temperature conversion**

Convert 27 degrees Celsius into kelvin.

\[
T=27+273.15
\]

\[
\boxed{T=300.15\,K}
\]

**Example 2: Linear expansion**

A metal rod of length 2 m has a coefficient of linear expansion of 12 × 10^-6 K^-1. Its temperature increases by 50 K.

\[
\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T
\]

\[
\Delta L=12\times10^{-6}\times2\times50
\]

\[
\boxed{\Delta L=0.0012\,m}
\]

The rod expands by 1.2 mm.

**Example 3: Heat supplied**

Calculate the energy required to heat 2 kg of water through 10 K. Take c = 4200 J/(kg K).

\[
Q=mc\Delta T
\]

\[
Q=2\times4200\times10
\]

\[
\boxed{Q=84000\,J}
\]

**Example 4: Latent heat**

How much energy is required to melt 0.5 kg of ice at its melting point if its specific latent heat of fusion is 334000 J/kg?

\[
Q=mL
\]

\[
Q=0.5\times334000
\]

\[
\boxed{Q=167000\,J}
\]

## 14. Applications

- Thermometers and temperature sensors.
- Expansion joints in bridges and railway tracks.
- Thermal insulation.
- Cooling and heating systems.
- Food preparation and storage.
- Climate regulation by oceans.
- Industrial heat exchangers.

## Common Exam Traps

- Heat and temperature are different physical quantities.
- Kelvin is the SI unit of temperature.
- A temperature difference of 10 K equals a difference of 10 degrees Celsius.
- Thermal expansion coefficients have units of inverse temperature.
- During a phase change, energy can be transferred without a temperature increase.
- Specific heat capacity is different from heat capacity.
- Calorimetry calculations must account for all energy transfers in the system.
