---
title: Electrical Energy, Power and Heating Effect
level: intermediate
---

Electrical energy is transferred whenever electric current flows through a circuit. Electrical power describes how quickly this energy is transferred or converted.

These concepts explain the operation of household appliances, heating elements, electric motors and power distribution systems.

## 1. Electrical Work

When a charge moves through a potential difference, electrical work is performed.

W = VQ

Where:
- W = electrical work
- V = potential difference
- Q = charge transferred

The SI unit is the joule (J).

Since Q = It:

W = VIt

This equation gives the electrical energy transferred when voltage and current remain constant.

## 2. Electrical Power

Electrical power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred.

P = W/t

Using W = VIt:

P = VI

Using Ohm's law, equivalent expressions are:

P = I²R

P = V²/R

Where:
- P = power
- V = potential difference
- I = current
- R = resistance

The SI unit is the watt (W).

1 W = 1 J/s

## 3. Electrical Energy Consumption

Electrical energy consumed over a time interval is:

E = Pt

For a constant voltage and current:

E = VIt

The SI unit is the joule.

Electrical energy is also commonly measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh).

1 kWh = 3.6 × 10^6 J

A kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy, not power.

## 4. Electricity Bills

Electrical energy consumption is commonly calculated in kilowatt-hours.

Energy consumed:

E(kWh) = Power(kW) × Time(h)

Cost:

Cost = Energy consumed × Cost per kWh

For example, a 2 kW heater operating for 3 hours consumes:

E = 2 × 3

E = 6 kWh

If the electricity tariff is ₹8 per kWh:

Cost = 6 × 8

Cost = ₹48

## 5. Joule's Law of Heating

When electric current flows through a resistance, electrical energy is converted into heat.

According to Joule's law:

H = I²Rt

Where:
- H = heat produced
- I = current
- R = resistance
- t = time

Using Ohm's law, equivalent forms are:

H = VIt

H = V²t/R

These equations assume the relevant electrical quantities remain constant during the interval.

## 6. Microscopic Origin of Heating

In a conductor:
- Electrons move under an applied electric field.
- They collide with the vibrating lattice and other scattering centres.
- Electrical energy is transferred to the material.
- The material's internal energy and temperature increase.

This process is called Joule heating or resistive heating.

## 7. Applications of the Heating Effect

The heating effect is used in:

- Electric irons.
- Electric kettles.
- Toasters.
- Room heaters.
- Incandescent filament lamps.
- Electric cooking appliances.
- Resistance-based industrial heating.

The heating element is commonly made from a material such as nichrome because it has relatively high resistivity, a high melting point and good resistance to oxidation.

## 8. Fuse and Circuit Protection

An electrical fuse is a safety device designed to interrupt a circuit when current exceeds a specified value.

A fuse contains a wire or element that melts when excessive current generates sufficient heat.

Important points:
- It is connected in series with the circuit.
- It has a specified current rating.
- It protects wiring and equipment from excessive current.
- It must be replaced after it operates.

A fuse should not be replaced with an ordinary piece of thick wire.

## 9. MCB and Other Protection Devices

A miniature circuit breaker (MCB) automatically opens a circuit when an overcurrent condition occurs.

Unlike a traditional fuse, an MCB can generally be reset after the fault has been corrected.

An earth-leakage protection device detects current flowing through an unintended path to earth and disconnects the supply.

Examples include:
- Residual Current Devices (RCDs).
- Residual Current Circuit Breakers (RCCBs).
- Residual Current Circuit Breakers with Overcurrent Protection (RCBOs).

These devices provide different forms of electrical protection and are not interchangeable in every application.

## 10. Short Circuit and Overload

### Short Circuit

A short circuit occurs when an unintended low-resistance path allows excessive current to flow.

Possible causes:
- Damaged insulation.
- Incorrect wiring.
- Contact between live and neutral conductors.

### Overload

An overload occurs when the current drawn by connected equipment exceeds the safe capacity of a circuit.

Possible causes:
- Too many appliances connected to one circuit.
- Excessive load.
- Equipment malfunction.

Both conditions can produce dangerous heating and fire risks.

## 11. Efficiency of Electrical Devices

Efficiency is the ratio of useful output energy to total input energy.

η = (Useful output energy / Input energy) × 100%

For a power-conversion device:

η = (Useful output power / Input power) × 100%

No practical energy-conversion device is perfectly efficient.

Energy losses may occur through:
- Heat.
- Sound.
- Mechanical friction.
- Electromagnetic radiation.
- Electrical resistance.

## 12. Power Rating of Appliances

An appliance's power rating indicates the electrical power it consumes or converts under specified operating conditions.

For a resistive appliance:

P = V²/R

At a fixed rated voltage, a higher power rating generally corresponds to a lower resistance.

For example, a 1000 W heater at 250 V has resistance:

R = V²/P

R = 250²/1000

R = 62.5 Ω

## 13. Transmission of Electrical Power

Electrical power stations transmit electricity over long distances.

For a given transmitted power:

P = VI

Therefore:

I = P/V

Power loss in transmission lines is:

Ploss = I²R

Increasing transmission voltage reduces current for a given power.

Since power loss depends on the square of current, high-voltage transmission significantly reduces resistive losses.

Transformers are used to increase voltage for transmission and reduce it for distribution and safe use.

## 14. Numerical Applications

**Example 1: Electrical power**

A device operates at 220 V and draws 2 A.

P = VI

P = 220 × 2

P = 440 W

**Example 2: Heating effect**

A current of 3 A flows through a 5 Ω resistor for 10 seconds.

H = I²Rt

H = 3² × 5 × 10

H = 450 J

**Example 3: Electricity consumption**

A 1.5 kW appliance operates for 4 hours.

E = Pt

E = 1.5 × 4

E = 6 kWh

## Common Exam Traps

- Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer.
- Electrical energy and power are different physical quantities.
- A kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy.
- Joule heating is proportional to I² for fixed resistance and time.
- Power can be expressed as VI, I²R or V²/R.
- A fuse is connected in series.
- A fuse protects against excessive current.
- An MCB is a resettable protective switching device.
- Short circuits and overloads are different causes of excessive current.
- High-voltage transmission reduces current and resistive power loss.
- Efficiency is usually expressed as a percentage.
