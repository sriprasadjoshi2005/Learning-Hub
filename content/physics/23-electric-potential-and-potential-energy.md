title: Electric Potential and Potential Energy
level: intermediate
---

Electric potential provides another way of describing electric fields. While electric field explains the force experienced by a charge, electric potential describes the electrical potential energy per unit charge at a particular point.

These concepts are essential for understanding circuits, capacitors, batteries and electrostatic systems.

## 1. Electric Potential Energy

Electric potential energy is the energy possessed by a system of charges because of their positions relative to one another.

When a charge is moved against an electric force, work must be done. This work is stored as electric potential energy.

For two point charges:

U = kq1q2 / r

Where:
- U = electric potential energy
- q1 and q2 = charges
- r = separation between the charges
- k = Coulomb constant

The sign of potential energy depends on the signs of the charges.

### Like Charges

For two positive charges or two negative charges:

U is positive.

Work must be done by an external agent to bring like charges closer together from infinity.

### Unlike Charges

For opposite charges:

U is negative.

The attractive interaction releases energy as the charges approach one another from infinity.

## 2. Electric Potential

Electric potential at a point is defined as the electric potential energy per unit positive test charge.

V = U / q

The SI unit is the volt (V).

1 volt = 1 joule / coulomb

Electric potential is a scalar quantity.

Unlike electric field, electric potential does not have a direction.

## 3. Potential Difference

Potential difference between two points is the work done per unit positive charge in moving a test charge between those points.

Vb - Va = Wexternal / q

Here Wexternal represents the work done by an external agent in a slow, controlled movement.

Equivalently, the change in potential energy is:

ΔU = qΔV

The work done by the electric field is:

Welectric = -ΔU

Therefore:

Welectric = q(Va - Vb)

Potential difference is what drives electric current in an electrical circuit.

## 4. Electric Potential Due to a Point Charge

The electric potential at a distance r from an isolated point charge Q, taking zero potential at infinity, is:

V = kQ / r

Important observations:

- Positive charges produce positive potential.
- Negative charges produce negative potential.
- Potential decreases in magnitude as distance increases.
- Potential is inversely proportional to distance.

Unlike the electric field of a point charge, which varies as 1/r², electric potential varies as 1/r.

## 5. Potential Due to Multiple Point Charges

Electric potential follows the principle of superposition.

The total potential at a point is the algebraic sum of the potentials produced by individual charges.

Vnet = V1 + V2 + V3 + ...

For several point charges:

Vnet = k(q1/r1 + q2/r2 + q3/r3 + ...)

Since potential is scalar, ordinary algebraic addition is used rather than vector addition.

## 6. Electric Potential Due to an Electric Dipole

An electric dipole consists of equal and opposite charges separated by a small distance.

For a point far from the dipole compared with its separation:

V = kp cos θ / r²

Where:
- p = dipole moment
- r = distance from the dipole centre
- θ = angle between the dipole moment and the position vector

### Axial Position

Along the dipole axis:

θ = 0 degrees or 180 degrees.

The potential is non-zero.

### Equatorial Position

On the perpendicular bisector:

θ = 90 degrees.

Therefore:

V = 0

The electric potential is zero on the equatorial plane of an ideal dipole because the contributions from its equal and opposite charges cancel.

## 7. Electric Potential Due to a Continuous Charge Distribution

For a continuous charge distribution, the potential is calculated by adding contributions from small charge elements.

dV = k dq / r

Total potential:

V = ∫ k dq / r

The distance r is measured from each charge element to the point where potential is being calculated.

Because electric potential is scalar, no vector decomposition is required.

## 8. Relationship Between Electric Field and Potential

Electric field and electric potential are closely related.

The electric field points in the direction of the greatest decrease in electric potential.

In one dimension:

Ex = -dV/dx

In three dimensions:

E = -∇V

The negative sign indicates that electric potential decreases in the direction of the electric field.

For a uniform electric field:

ΔV = -Ed

when displacement d is along the field direction.

In magnitude:

E = |ΔV| / d

This relationship is useful for calculating electric fields from potential differences.

## 9. Equipotential Surfaces

An equipotential surface is a surface on which electric potential is the same at every point.

Moving a charge along an equipotential surface requires zero work by the electric field.

Important properties:

1. Every point on an equipotential surface has the same potential.
2. Electric field lines are perpendicular to equipotential surfaces.
3. Two equipotential surfaces never intersect.
4. Closely spaced equipotential surfaces indicate a stronger electric field.
5. A conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is an equipotential body.

Examples:
- Concentric spherical surfaces around a point charge.
- Parallel planes around a uniform electric field.

## 10. Electrostatic Potential Energy of a System of Charges

For a system of multiple point charges, total electrostatic potential energy is the sum of the potential energies of all distinct pairs.

For three charges:

U = k(q1q2/r12 + q1q3/r13 + q2q3/r23)

Where rij represents the separation between charges i and j.

Each pair is counted exactly once.

This method can be extended to systems containing any number of point charges.

## 11. Potential Energy of a Dipole in an Electric Field

A dipole placed in a uniform electric field has potential energy:

U = -pE cos θ

Where θ is the angle between the dipole moment and the field.

Important positions:

- θ = 0 degrees: minimum potential energy and stable equilibrium.
- θ = 90 degrees: zero potential energy.
- θ = 180 degrees: maximum potential energy and unstable equilibrium.

The dipole tends to rotate towards alignment with the electric field.

## 12. Electrostatic Shielding

Electrostatic shielding is the protection of a region from external electrostatic fields using a conducting enclosure.

In electrostatic equilibrium:
- The electric field inside the conducting material is zero.
- Excess charge resides on the conductor's surface.
- An empty cavity inside a conductor can be shielded from external electrostatic fields under electrostatic equilibrium.

Applications include:
- Faraday cages.
- Shielded electrical cables.
- Protection of sensitive electronic equipment.
- Metal enclosures around electrical instruments.

## 13. Numerical Applications

**Example 1: Potential due to a point charge**

Calculate the electric potential at a distance of 0.3 m from a charge of +2 μC.

V = kQ/r

V = (9 × 10^9 × 2 × 10^-6)/0.3

V = 60,000 V

**Example 2: Work and potential difference**

A charge of 2 C moves through a potential difference of 12 V.

The change in potential energy is:

ΔU = qΔV

ΔU = 2 × 12

ΔU = 24 J

**Example 3: Electric field from potential difference**

A uniform potential difference of 100 V exists across two parallel plates separated by 0.02 m.

E = |ΔV| / d

E = 100 / 0.02

E = 5000 V/m

## Common Exam Traps

- Electric potential is a scalar quantity.
- Electric field is a vector quantity.
- Potential due to a point charge varies as 1/r.
- Electric field due to a point charge varies as 1/r².
- The potential due to a negative charge is negative when the reference potential at infinity is zero.
- Zero potential does not necessarily mean zero electric field.
- Electric field points towards decreasing electric potential.
- No work is done by the electrostatic field when a charge moves along an equipotential surface.
- The work done by the electric field is the negative of the change in potential energy.
- Potential contributions from multiple charges are added algebraically.

## Quiz

1. Electric potential at a distance r from an isolated point charge varies as:

- [ ] 1/r²
- [x] 1/r
- [ ] r²
- [ ] r

> Electric potential is V = kQ/r, so it is inversely proportional to distance.

2. The work done by the electrostatic field when a charge moves along an equipotential surface is:

- [x] Zero
- [ ] Maximum
- [ ] Always positive
- [ ] Always negative

> Potential difference along an equipotential surface is zero. Therefore, work done by the electrostatic field is also zero.

3. The electric field points in the direction of:

- [ ] Increasing electric potential
- [x] Decreasing electric potential
- [ ] Constant electric potential only
- [ ] Increasing electric potential energy for every possible charge

> Electric field is the negative gradient of potential. Its direction is the direction in which potential decreases most rapidly.
