---
title: Static Equilibrium and Elastic Properties of Solids
level: intermediate
---

Static equilibrium describes the condition in which an object remains at rest without translational or rotational acceleration. It is essential for understanding bridges, buildings, machines, supports, and structural stability.

Elasticity describes how materials deform under applied forces and how they recover their original shapes when those forces are removed.

## 1. Equilibrium of a Body

An object is in mechanical equilibrium when its acceleration is zero.

For translational equilibrium:

\[
\sum\vec F=0
\]

For rotational equilibrium:

\[
\sum\vec\tau=0
\]

Both conditions must be satisfied for complete static equilibrium.

A body can have zero net force but non-zero torque, causing angular acceleration.

Similarly, zero net torque does not guarantee translational equilibrium.

## 2. Types of Equilibrium

**Static equilibrium:** The object remains at rest.

**Dynamic equilibrium:** The object moves with constant linear velocity and constant angular velocity.

Both conditions require zero resultant force and zero resultant torque.

## 3. Conditions for Equilibrium in Two Dimensions

For a body in a plane:

\[
\sum F_x=0
\]

\[
\sum F_y=0
\]

\[
\sum\tau=0
\]

A convenient choice of rotational axis can simplify calculations.

The clockwise and anticlockwise torques must balance.

## 4. Principle of Moments

The moment of a force is the product of force and its perpendicular distance from the axis.

\[
\tau=F d_\perp
\]

For rotational equilibrium:

\[
\sum\tau_{\text{clockwise}}
=
\sum\tau_{\text{anticlockwise}}
\]

This is the principle of moments.

It explains how levers, seesaws, spanners, and balance systems work.

## 5. Centre of Gravity and Stability

The centre of gravity is the point at which the resultant gravitational force effectively acts.

Stability depends on the position of the centre of gravity and the base of support.

**Stable equilibrium:** A small displacement produces a tendency to return to the original position.

**Unstable equilibrium:** A small displacement causes the object to move farther from its original position.

**Neutral equilibrium:** A small displacement leaves the object in a new equilibrium position.

Examples:
- A ball at the bottom of a bowl: stable equilibrium.
- A ball on top of a hill: unstable equilibrium.
- A ball on a horizontal surface: neutral equilibrium.

## 6. Stress

Stress measures the internal restoring force per unit area developed in a material due to an external load.

\[
\text{Stress}=\frac{F}{A}
\]

The SI unit is pascal (Pa).

\[
1\,Pa=1\,N/m^2
\]

Types of stress include:

- Tensile stress: caused by pulling.
- Compressive stress: caused by pushing.
- Shear stress: caused by tangential forces.
- Bulk or volumetric stress: associated with pressure applied uniformly from different directions.

## 7. Strain

Strain measures the relative deformation produced in a material.

It is a ratio and therefore dimensionless.

### Longitudinal Strain

\[
\text{Longitudinal strain}=\frac{\Delta L}{L}
\]

### Volumetric Strain

\[
\text{Volumetric strain}=\frac{\Delta V}{V}
\]

### Shear Strain

For small angular deformation:

\[
\text{Shear strain}\approx\theta
\]

Where theta is the angular deformation in radians.

## 8. Hooke's Law

Within the elastic limit, the deformation of many materials is proportional to the applied force.

For a spring:

\[
F=kx
\]

Where:
- F = magnitude of restoring force
- k = spring constant
- x = extension or compression

The restoring force acts opposite to displacement:

\[
F=-kx
\]

The negative sign indicates the direction of the restoring force.

Hooke's law is valid only within the appropriate proportional region.

## 9. Young's Modulus

Young's modulus measures a material's resistance to longitudinal deformation.

\[
Y=\frac{\text{Longitudinal stress}}{\text{Longitudinal strain}}
\]

\[
Y=\frac{FL}{A\Delta L}
\]

The SI unit is pascal.

A higher Young's modulus indicates greater stiffness under tensile or compressive loading.

## 10. Bulk Modulus

Bulk modulus measures resistance to uniform volume change.

\[
K=-\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta V/V}
\]

The negative sign reflects that increasing pressure generally decreases volume.

The compressibility is the reciprocal of bulk modulus:

\[
\beta=\frac{1}{K}
\]

## 11. Shear Modulus

Shear modulus measures resistance to shape deformation under shear stress.

\[
G=\frac{\text{Shear stress}}{\text{Shear strain}}
\]

\[
G=\frac{F/A}{\theta}
\]

Its SI unit is pascal.

## 12. Stress-Strain Curve

A stress-strain curve shows how a material responds as deformation increases.

Important regions and points include:

1. Proportional region: stress is proportional to strain.
2. Elastic region: deformation is recoverable after unloading.
3. Yield point: significant permanent deformation begins.
4. Plastic region: permanent deformation occurs.
5. Ultimate tensile strength: maximum engineering stress.
6. Fracture point: the material breaks.

Different materials have different stress-strain curves.

Ductile materials can undergo considerable plastic deformation before fracture, whereas brittle materials fracture with relatively little plastic deformation.

## 13. Elastic and Plastic Deformation

Elastic deformation is reversible.

Plastic deformation is permanent.

A material can behave elastically for small loads but become permanently deformed when its elastic limit is exceeded.

Elasticity is not identical to strength. A stiff material resists deformation, while a strong material can withstand large stress before failure.

## 14. Worked Numerical Examples

**Example 1: Equilibrium of a seesaw**

A 300 N force acts 2 m to the left of a pivot. What force must act 3 m to the right to balance it?

Using the principle of moments:

\[
300\times2=F\times3
\]

\[
600=3F
\]

\[
\boxed{F=200\,N}
\]

**Example 2: Stress**

A force of 1000 N acts on an area of 0.02 m².

\[
\text{Stress}=\frac{F}{A}
\]

\[
=\frac{1000}{0.02}
\]

\[
\boxed{5\times10^4\,Pa}
\]

**Example 3: Young's modulus**

A wire of length 2 m and cross-sectional area 1 × 10^-6 m² extends by 0.001 m under a force of 100 N.

\[
Y=\frac{FL}{A\Delta L}
\]

\[
Y=\frac{100\times2}{(10^{-6})(0.001)}
\]

\[
\boxed{Y=2\times10^{11}\,Pa}
\]

## 15. Applications

- Designing bridges and buildings.
- Testing structural materials.
- Manufacturing springs and suspension systems.
- Measuring the stiffness of wires and rods.
- Engineering safety limits.
- Understanding deformation in biological tissues.

## Common Exam Traps

- Complete equilibrium requires both zero resultant force and zero resultant torque.
- Torque depends on perpendicular distance, not simply the distance from the pivot.
- Stress has units, whereas strain is dimensionless.
- Young's modulus measures stiffness, not necessarily strength.
- Hooke's law applies only within the relevant proportional region.
- Plastic deformation is permanent.
- A negative sign in bulk modulus represents volume reduction under increased pressure.
