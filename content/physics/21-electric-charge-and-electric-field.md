Electricity is one of the fundamental areas of physics. It explains phenomena ranging from static electricity and lightning to the operation of electronic devices. The study of electricity begins with electric charge and the forces that charged particles exert on one another.

## 1. Electric Charge

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter responsible for electrical interactions.

There are two types of electric charge:

- Positive charge: associated with protons.
- Negative charge: associated with electrons.

Neutrons have no net electric charge.

The SI unit of electric charge is the coulomb (C).

The magnitude of the elementary charge is:

e = 1.602 × 10^-19 C

A proton carries charge +e, while an electron carries charge -e.

## 2. Basic Properties of Electric Charge

Electric charge has several important properties.

### Additivity

The total charge of a system is the algebraic sum of all individual charges.

Q = q1 + q2 + q3 + ...

Positive and negative charges must be included with their signs.

### Conservation of Charge

Electric charge can neither be created nor destroyed in an isolated system. It can only be transferred from one body to another.

The total charge before and after an interaction remains constant.

### Quantisation of Charge

Charge exists in discrete amounts rather than arbitrary continuous values.

Q = ne

Where:
- Q = total charge
- n = integer
- e = elementary charge

The value of n may be positive, negative, or zero.

### Invariance of Charge

The electric charge of a particle does not depend on its speed or the reference frame from which it is observed.

## 3. Conductors and Insulators

Materials are classified according to how easily electric charges move through them.

**Conductors:** Materials in which electric charges can move relatively freely.

Examples include copper, aluminium, silver and graphite.

**Insulators:** Materials in which electric charges cannot move freely through the material.

Examples include rubber, glass, plastic and dry wood.

**Semiconductors:** Materials whose electrical conductivity lies between that of conductors and insulators.

Examples include silicon and germanium.

Semiconductors are particularly important in modern electronics.

## 4. Methods of Charging

An electrically neutral body can become charged through several methods.

### Charging by Friction

When two different materials are rubbed together, electrons may transfer from one material to another.

The material losing electrons becomes positively charged, while the material gaining electrons becomes negatively charged.

### Charging by Conduction

When a charged body touches a neutral conductor, charge is transferred through direct contact.

The neutral conductor acquires a charge with the same sign as the original charged body.

### Charging by Induction

Charging by induction occurs without direct contact.

A charged object brought near a conductor causes its charges to redistribute. If the conductor is grounded appropriately and the grounding connection is removed before the external charged object, the conductor can acquire a net charge opposite to the inducing object's charge.

## 5. Coulomb's Law

Coulomb's law describes the electrostatic force between two stationary point charges.

The magnitude of the force is:

F = (1 / 4πε0) × (|q1q2| / r²)

Where:
- F = electrostatic force
- q1 and q2 = magnitudes of the charges
- r = distance between the charges
- ε0 = permittivity of free space

The Coulomb constant is:

k = 1 / 4πε0 ≈ 8.99 × 10^9 N m²/C²

Therefore:

F = k|q1q2| / r²

The force:
- Is directly proportional to the product of the magnitudes of the charges.
- Is inversely proportional to the square of their separation.
- Acts along the line joining the two charges.

### Nature of the Force

- Like charges repel.
- Unlike charges attract.

Coulomb's law is mathematically similar to Newton's law of universal gravitation, although gravitational force is always attractive while electrostatic force can attract or repel.

## 6. Coulomb's Law in a Medium

The electrostatic force between charges changes when they are placed in a material medium.

F = (1 / 4πε) × (|q1q2| / r²)

Where ε is the permittivity of the medium.

The relative permittivity is:

εr = ε / ε0

Therefore:

Fmedium = Fvacuum / εr

For an ordinary dielectric medium, the electrostatic force is reduced compared with its value in vacuum.

## 7. Principle of Superposition

When several charges act on a particular charge, the total electrostatic force is the vector sum of the individual forces.

Fnet = F1 + F2 + F3 + ...

Each force is calculated independently using Coulomb's law.

The direction of every force must be considered before calculating the resultant.

For forces acting at right angles:

Fnet = sqrt(Fx² + Fy²)

For forces along the same straight line, assign appropriate positive and negative signs before adding them.

## 8. Electric Field

An electric field is the region around an electric charge in which another electric charge experiences an electrostatic force.

Electric field intensity is defined as the force experienced per unit positive test charge.

E = F / q0

Where:
- E = electric field intensity
- F = force acting on the test charge
- q0 = positive test charge

The SI unit is N/C.

An equivalent unit is V/m.

The electric field is a vector quantity.

For a point charge:

E = k|Q| / r²

The direction is:
- Away from a positive charge.
- Towards a negative charge.

## 9. Electric Field Due to Multiple Charges

The electric field produced by multiple charges follows the principle of superposition.

Enet = E1 + E2 + E3 + ...

The resultant field is obtained by vector addition.

The electric field at a point may be zero even when individual fields produced by different charges are non-zero.

Such points are called neutral points or zero-field points.

## 10. Electric Field Lines

Electric field lines are imaginary curves used to represent the direction and relative strength of an electric field.

Important properties:

1. Field lines originate from positive charges and terminate on negative charges, or extend to infinity.
2. The tangent to a field line gives the direction of the electric field.
3. Field lines never intersect.
4. Closely spaced field lines represent stronger electric fields.
5. Electrostatic field lines do not form closed loops.
6. Field lines meet the surface of a conductor perpendicularly under electrostatic equilibrium.

## 11. Electric Dipole

An electric dipole consists of two equal and opposite charges separated by a small distance.

Its dipole moment is:

p = q × 2a

Where:
- q = magnitude of either charge
- 2a = separation between the charges

The SI unit is C m.

The direction of the electric dipole moment is from the negative charge towards the positive charge.

### Electric Field on the Axial Line

For a dipole with separation 2a, at a point on its axial line at distance r from the centre:

Eaxial = (1 / 4πε0) × [2pr / (r² - a²)²]

For distances much larger than the dipole separation:

Eaxial ≈ 2kp / r³

### Electric Field on the Equatorial Line

At a point on the perpendicular bisector of the dipole:

Eequatorial = (1 / 4πε0) × [p / (r² + a²)^(3/2)]

For distances much larger than the dipole separation:

Eequatorial ≈ kp / r³

The equatorial electric field is opposite to the direction of the dipole moment.

## 12. Dipole in a Uniform Electric Field

An electric dipole placed in a uniform electric field experiences a torque.

τ = pE sin θ

Where θ is the angle between the dipole moment and the electric field.

The torque tends to align the dipole moment with the field.

The potential energy of a dipole is:

U = -pE cos θ

Important positions:

- Stable equilibrium: θ = 0 degrees.
- Unstable equilibrium: θ = 180 degrees.

In a uniform electric field, the net force on an ideal dipole is zero, although the torque may be non-zero.

## 13. Electric Field and Electrostatic Force: Numerical Applications

**Example 1: Force between two charges**

Two charges of +2 μC and +3 μC are separated by 0.3 m in vacuum.

Using Coulomb's law:

F = k|q1q2| / r²

F = (9 × 10^9 × 2 × 10^-6 × 3 × 10^-6) / (0.3)²

F = 0.6 N

The force is repulsive because both charges are positive.

**Example 2: Electric field due to a point charge**

Find the electric field at a distance of 0.2 m from a charge of +4 μC.

E = kQ / r²

E = (9 × 10^9 × 4 × 10^-6) / (0.2)²

E = 9 × 10^5 N/C

The field is directed away from the positive charge.

## Common Exam Traps

- Electric charge is a scalar quantity, whereas electric field and force are vectors.
- The electric field direction is defined using a positive test charge.
- Like charges repel, while unlike charges attract.
- Coulomb's law follows an inverse-square relationship with distance.
- Doubling the distance between two point charges reduces the force to one-fourth.
- Electric field lines never intersect.
- The dipole moment points from negative to positive.
- A dipole in a uniform field may experience torque without experiencing a net force.
- Charge is conserved and quantised.

## Quiz

1. Two point charges are separated by a distance r. If the distance is doubled while the charges remain unchanged, the electrostatic force becomes:

- [ ] Twice the original force
- [x] One-fourth of the original force
- [ ] Half the original force
- [ ] Four times the original force

> Coulomb's law follows an inverse-square relationship. Doubling the separation gives F/4.

2. The direction of the electric dipole moment is:

- [ ] From positive to negative
- [x] From negative to positive
- [ ] Perpendicular to the dipole axis
- [ ] Always in the direction of the electric field

> By convention, electric dipole moment points from the negative charge towards the positive charge.

3. Which statement about electrostatic field lines is correct?

- [ ] They can intersect at points of strong electric field
- [ ] They always form closed loops
- [x] They never intersect one another
- [ ] They always point towards positive charges

> If field lines intersected, the electric field would have two different directions at the same point, which is impossible.
