Magnetism is a fundamental interaction associated with moving electric charges and magnetic materials. Magnetic fields influence moving charged particles, current-carrying conductors and magnetic dipoles.

Magnetic phenomena are essential to electric motors, generators, transformers, speakers and many modern technologies.

## 1. Magnetism

Magnetism is the physical phenomenon associated with magnetic fields and their interactions with matter.

A magnet has two poles:
- North pole.
- South pole.

Basic magnetic interactions:
- Like poles repel.
- Unlike poles attract.

An isolated north or south magnetic pole has not been experimentally established as an ordinary magnetic object.

Cutting a bar magnet produces smaller magnets, each with both north and south poles.

## 2. Magnetic Field

A magnetic field is a region in which a magnetic material, magnetic pole model or moving electric charge experiences magnetic effects.

Magnetic field is represented by the symbol B.

The SI unit is the tesla (T).

Magnetic field is a vector quantity.

## 3. Magnetic Field Lines

Magnetic field lines are imaginary curves used to represent magnetic fields.

Important properties:

1. Outside a bar magnet, field lines emerge from the north pole and enter the south pole.
2. Inside the magnet, they continue from south to north.
3. Magnetic field lines form closed loops.
4. They never intersect.
5. Closely spaced lines represent stronger magnetic fields.
6. The tangent at any point gives the direction of the magnetic field.

Unlike electrostatic field lines, magnetic field lines do not begin or end at isolated magnetic charges in ordinary classical electromagnetism.

## 4. Magnetic Field Due to a Straight Current-Carrying Wire

An electric current produces a magnetic field around its conductor.

For a long straight wire carrying current I, the magnetic field at distance r is:

B = μ0I/(2πr)

Where:
- B = magnetic field
- μ0 = permeability of free space
- I = current
- r = perpendicular distance from the wire

The magnetic field decreases inversely with distance.

## 5. Right-Hand Thumb Rule

The right-hand thumb rule determines the direction of the magnetic field around a straight current-carrying wire.

Procedure:
1. Hold the wire with the right hand.
2. Point the thumb in the direction of conventional current.
3. The curled fingers indicate the direction of the magnetic field.

Reversing the current reverses the magnetic field direction.

## 6. Magnetic Field at the Centre of a Circular Current Loop

For a circular loop of radius R carrying current I:

B = μ0I/(2R)

For a coil with N closely wound turns:

B = μ0NI/(2R)

The magnetic field is directed along the axis of the loop.

Its direction can be determined using the right-hand grip rule.

## 7. Magnetic Field Inside a Long Solenoid

A solenoid is a long cylindrical coil consisting of many turns of insulated wire.

For an ideal long solenoid:

B = μ0nI

Where:
- n = number of turns per unit length
- I = current

For a solenoid with a magnetic core:

B = μnI

Where μ is the permeability of the core material under the assumed linear approximation.

The field inside an ideal long solenoid is approximately uniform, while the external field is comparatively weak.

## 8. Magnetic Field Due to a Toroid

A toroid is a solenoid bent into a closed circular shape.

For an ideal toroid with N turns carrying current I, at a distance r from its centre:

B = μNI/(2πr)

The magnetic field is largely confined within the toroidal core.

The field outside an ideal toroid is zero in the idealised model.

## 9. Biot-Savart Law

The Biot-Savart law gives the magnetic field contribution due to a small current element.

In vector form:

dB = (μ0/4π) × [I(dl × rhat)/r²]

Where:
- I = current
- dl = small current element
- r = distance from the element to the observation point
- rhat = unit vector from the current element to the observation point

The direction is determined by the cross product.

The total magnetic field is obtained by integrating contributions from all current elements.

## 10. Ampere's Circuital Law

Ampere's circuital law relates the magnetic field around a closed path to the current enclosed by that path.

∮ B · dl = μ0Ienclosed

It is especially useful for calculating magnetic fields in highly symmetric arrangements.

Applications include:
- Long straight conductors.
- Ideal solenoids.
- Ideal toroids.

## 11. Magnetic Force on a Moving Charge

A charged particle moving through a magnetic field experiences a magnetic force.

F = q(v × B)

Magnitude:

F = |q|vB sin θ

Where:
- q = charge
- v = particle velocity
- B = magnetic field
- θ = angle between velocity and magnetic field

Important cases:

### Motion Parallel to the Field

θ = 0 degrees

F = 0

### Motion Perpendicular to the Field

θ = 90 degrees

F = |q|vB

The magnetic force is maximum.

The force is perpendicular to both velocity and magnetic field.

## 12. Direction of Magnetic Force

The direction of magnetic force is determined using the right-hand rule for a positive charge.

For a negative charge, the direction is opposite.

Because the magnetic force is perpendicular to the particle's instantaneous velocity, it does no work on the particle.

It can change the direction of velocity without changing the speed in an ideal static magnetic field.

## 13. Circular Motion of a Charged Particle

When a charged particle enters a uniform magnetic field perpendicular to its velocity, it follows a circular path.

Magnetic force provides centripetal force.

|q|vB = mv²/r

Therefore:

r = mv/(|q|B)

The angular frequency is:

ω = |q|B/m

The time period is:

T = 2πm/(|q|B)

The radius increases with mass and speed and decreases with charge magnitude and magnetic field strength.

## 14. Helical Motion

If a charged particle enters a magnetic field at an angle other than 0 or 90 degrees, its velocity has two components:

- Parallel component: remains unchanged in an ideal uniform magnetic field.
- Perpendicular component: produces circular motion.

The combination produces a helical trajectory.

The radius depends on the perpendicular velocity component:

r = mvperpendicular/(|q|B)

The pitch is the distance travelled along the field during one revolution:

Pitch = vparallel × T

## 15. Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

A conductor carrying current in a magnetic field experiences a force.

F = I(L × B)

Magnitude:

F = BIL sin θ

Where:
- I = current
- L = length vector of the conductor
- B = magnetic field
- θ = angle between current direction and magnetic field

The force is maximum when the conductor is perpendicular to the field.

It is zero when the conductor is parallel to the field.

## 16. Torque on a Current-Carrying Coil

A current-carrying rectangular coil in a uniform magnetic field experiences torque.

τ = NIAB sin θ

Where:
- N = number of turns
- I = current
- A = coil area
- B = magnetic field
- θ = angle between the coil's normal and the magnetic field

The magnetic dipole moment of the coil is:

m = NIA

Therefore:

τ = mB sin θ

The torque tends to align the coil's magnetic moment with the magnetic field.

## 17. Moving Coil Galvanometer

A moving coil galvanometer detects small electric currents.

Its operation is based on the torque experienced by a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field.

At equilibrium:

NIAB = kθ

Where:
- k = torsional constant of the suspension
- θ = angular deflection

Therefore:

I = kθ/(NAB)

Deflection is proportional to current under the instrument's operating conditions.

A galvanometer can be converted into an ammeter or voltmeter by adding suitable resistances.

## 18. Numerical Applications

**Example 1: Magnetic field around a straight wire**

A long wire carries 10 A. Find the magnetic field at a distance of 0.1 m.

B = μ0I/(2πr)

B = (4π × 10^-7 × 10)/(2π × 0.1)

B = 2 × 10^-5 T

**Example 2: Force on a moving charge**

A charge of 2 × 10^-6 C moves at 1000 m/s perpendicular to a magnetic field of 0.5 T.

F = qvB

F = 2 × 10^-6 × 1000 × 0.5

F = 1 × 10^-3 N

## Common Exam Traps

- Magnetic field is a vector quantity.
- Magnetic field lines form closed loops.
- The magnetic field around a straight wire forms concentric circles.
- Magnetic force is zero when a charged particle moves parallel to the field.
- Magnetic force is maximum when velocity is perpendicular to the field.
- Magnetic force does no work on a charged particle in a static magnetic field.
- A particle entering a uniform magnetic field perpendicular to its velocity follows circular motion.
- An oblique entry produces helical motion.
- Conventional current direction is used in magnetic right-hand rules.
- The magnetic field inside an ideal long solenoid is approximately uniform.

## Quiz

1. A charged particle moves parallel to a uniform magnetic field. The magnetic force on it is:

- [x] Zero
- [ ] Maximum
- [ ] Equal to qvB
- [ ] Infinite

> F = |q|vB sin θ. When θ = 0 degrees, sin θ = 0.

2. The magnetic field around a long straight current-carrying wire varies with distance r as:

- [ ] r
- [ ] r²
- [x] 1/r
- [ ] 1/r²

> B = μ0I/(2πr), so magnetic field is inversely proportional to distance.

3. A charged particle enters a uniform magnetic field perpendicular to its velocity. Its path is:

- [ ] Straight
- [x] Circular
- [ ] Parabolic
- [ ] Stationary

> The magnetic force remains perpendicular to velocity and acts as centripetal force, producing circular motion.
