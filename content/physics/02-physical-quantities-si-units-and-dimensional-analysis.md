Physics describes natural phenomena using measurable quantities. Length, mass, time, temperature, electric current and many other properties can be measured and expressed numerically.

A physical quantity is a property that can be measured and expressed as a numerical value multiplied by an appropriate unit.

For example:

- Length = 5 metres
- Mass = 3 kilograms
- Time = 10 seconds
- Speed = 20 metres per second

Measurements and units allow physical observations to be communicated consistently and mathematical relationships to be developed.

## 1. Physical Quantities

A physical quantity consists of two essential components:

1. Numerical magnitude.
2. Unit.

For example:

\[
v=15\ \text{m/s}
\]

Here:

- \(v\) represents velocity.
- 15 is its numerical magnitude.
- m/s is its unit.

A numerical value without a unit may be incomplete or misleading.

### Classification of physical quantities

Physical quantities can be classified into fundamental and derived quantities.

**Fundamental quantities:**

These are independent quantities that form the basis of a measurement system.

**Derived quantities:**

These are obtained by combining fundamental quantities mathematically.

For example, speed is derived from length divided by time.

## 2. SI System of Units

The International System of Units, abbreviated SI, is the internationally accepted system of measurement.

It contains seven base quantities and their corresponding base units.

| Fundamental quantity | SI unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Length | metre | m |
| Mass | kilogram | kg |
| Time | second | s |
| Electric current | ampere | A |
| Thermodynamic temperature | kelvin | K |
| Amount of substance | mole | mol |
| Luminous intensity | candela | cd |

These seven units provide the foundation for expressing a large number of derived physical quantities.

### Important distinctions

- The unit of mass is kilogram, not gram.
- The SI unit of temperature is kelvin, not degree Celsius.
- The SI unit of electric current is ampere.
- The SI unit of amount of substance is mole.

## 3. Derived Physical Quantities

Derived quantities are expressed using combinations of base quantities.

| Quantity | Relationship | SI unit |
|---|---|---|
| Area | Length × length | m² |
| Volume | Length × length × length | m³ |
| Speed | Distance / time | m/s |
| Acceleration | Velocity / time | m/s² |
| Force | Mass × acceleration | newton (N) |
| Momentum | Mass × velocity | kg m/s |
| Work | Force × displacement | joule (J) |
| Power | Work / time | watt (W) |
| Pressure | Force / area | pascal (Pa) |
| Density | Mass / volume | kg/m³ |

For example:

\[
F=ma
\]

The SI unit of force is:

\[
\text{kg}\times\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}
=\text{kg m/s}^2
\]

This derived unit is called the newton.

\[
1\ N=1\ \text{kg m/s}^2
\]

### Scientific notation

Scientific notation expresses numbers in the form:

\[
a\times10^n
\]

where:

\[
1\leq |a|<10
\]

Examples:

\[
450000=4.5\times10^5
\]

\[
0.00032=3.2\times10^{-4}
\]

Scientific notation is particularly useful in atomic physics, astronomy and calculations involving extremely small or large quantities.

## 5. Unit Conversion

A physical quantity can be expressed using different units without changing its actual value.

For example:

\[
1\ \text{km}=1000\ \text{m}
\]

To convert kilometres into metres, multiply by 1000.

To convert metres into kilometres, divide by 1000.

### Example 1: Converting speed

Convert 72 km/h into m/s.

We know:

\[
1\ \text{km}=1000\ \text{m}
\]

\[
1\ \text{hour}=3600\ \text{seconds}
\]

Therefore:

\[
72\ \frac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}
=72\times\frac{1000}{3600}\ \text{m/s}
\]

\[
=20\ \text{m/s}
\]

A useful shortcut is:

\[
\text{km/h to m/s}:\quad \times\frac{5}{18}
\]

\[
\text{m/s to km/h}:\quad \times\frac{18}{5}
\]

### Example 2: Converting area

Convert 5 cm² into m².

Since:

\[
1\ \text{cm}=10^{-2}\ \text{m}
\]

Squaring both sides:

\[
1\ \text{cm}^2=10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2
\]

Therefore:

\[
5\ \text{cm}^2=5\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2
\]

**Important:** When converting area or volume, the conversion factor must also be squared or cubed.

## 6. Dimensions of Physical Quantities

Dimensions describe how a physical quantity depends on fundamental quantities.

The commonly used fundamental dimensions in elementary mechanics are:

- Mass: [M]
- Length: [L]
- Time: [T]

Additional dimensions include:

- Electric current: [I]
- Temperature: [Θ]
- Amount of substance: [N]
- Luminous intensity: [J]

The dimensions of a quantity are written using powers of these fundamental dimensions.

### Examples

**Velocity:**

\[
v=\frac{\text{displacement}}{\text{time}}
\]

Therefore:

\[
[v]=[LT^{-1}]
\]

**Acceleration:**

\[
a=\frac{\text{velocity}}{\text{time}}
\]

\[
[a]=[LT^{-2}]
\]

**Force:**

\[
F=ma
\]

\[
[F]=[M][LT^{-2}]
\]

\[
[F]=[MLT^{-2}]
\]

**Work:**

\[
W=Fs
\]

\[
[W]=[MLT^{-2}][L]
\]

\[
[W]=[ML^2T^{-2}]
\]

### Important dimensional formulae

| Quantity | Dimensional formula |
|---|---|
| Area | [L²] |
| Volume | [L³] |
| Velocity | [LT^-1] |
| Acceleration | [LT^-2] |
| Momentum | [MLT^-1] |
| Force | [MLT^-2] |
| Impulse | [MLT^-1] |
| Work | [ML²T^-2] |
| Energy | [ML²T^-2] |
| Power | [ML²T^-3] |
| Pressure | [ML^-1T^-2] |
| Density | [ML^-3] |
| Frequency | [T^-1] |
| Gravitational constant | [M^-1L³T^-2] |

## 7. Dimensional Analysis

Dimensional analysis is a method of studying physical equations by examining the dimensions of their quantities.

It is used to:

1. Check the dimensional consistency of equations.
2. Derive possible relationships between physical quantities.
3. Convert units from one system to another.
4. Identify possible errors in mathematical expressions.

### Principle of dimensional homogeneity

A physically meaningful equation must have the same dimensions on both sides.

For example:

\[
s=ut+\frac{1}{2}at^2
\]

Consider each term.

Displacement:

\[
[s]=[L]
\]

First term:

\[
[ut]=[LT^{-1}][T]=[L]
\]

Second term:

\[
[at^2]=[LT^{-2}][T^2]=[L]
\]

All terms have the dimensions of length.

Therefore, the equation is dimensionally consistent.

### Example: Checking an equation

Consider:

\[
v=u+at
\]

Dimensions:

\[
[v]=[LT^{-1}]
\]

\[
[u]=[LT^{-1}]
\]

\[
[at]=[LT^{-2}][T]=[LT^{-1}]
\]

Every term has the same dimensions, so the equation is dimensionally consistent.

Now consider:

\[
v=u+at^2
\]

The dimensions of the second term are:

\[
[at^2]=[LT^{-2}][T^2]=[L]
\]

This cannot be added to velocity, which has dimensions [LT^-1].

Therefore, this equation is dimensionally incorrect.

## 8. Applications of Dimensional Analysis

Dimensional analysis can help derive the form of a physical relationship.

Suppose the time period \(T\) of a simple pendulum depends on its length \(l\) and gravitational acceleration \(g\).

Assume:

\[
T=kl^ag^b
\]

where \(k\) is dimensionless.

Writing dimensions:

\[
[T]=[L]^a[LT^{-2}]^b
\]

\[
[T]=[L^{a+b}T^{-2b}]
\]

Comparing powers of dimensions:

For length:

\[
a+b=0
\]

For time:

\[
-2b=1
\]

Therefore:

\[
b=-\frac12
\]

And:

\[
a=\frac12
\]

Hence:

\[
T=k\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}
\]

Dimensional analysis determines the form of the relationship, but it cannot determine the numerical constant \(k\).

For a simple pendulum undergoing small oscillations:

\[
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}
\]

The constant \(2\pi\) must be obtained through further physical analysis.

## 9. Limitations of Dimensional Analysis

Dimensional analysis is powerful but has important limitations.

It cannot:

- Determine pure numerical constants such as 2, 1/2 or 2π.
- Establish whether terms should be added or subtracted.
- Derive relationships involving dimensionless functions without additional information.
- Distinguish between quantities having identical dimensions.
- Guarantee that a dimensionally correct equation is physically correct.

For example, work and torque both have dimensions:

\[
[ML^2T^{-2}]
\]

However, they represent different physical concepts.

Dimensional consistency is necessary for an equation to be physically meaningful, but it is not sufficient to prove its correctness.

## 10. Dimensionless Quantities

Some physical quantities have no dimensions.

Examples include:

- Strain.
- Refractive index.
- Coefficient of friction.
- Relative density.
- Efficiency.
- Poisson's ratio.

For example, refractive index is:

\[
n=\frac{\text{Speed of light in vacuum}}{\text{Speed of light in medium}}
\]

Both numerator and denominator have the same dimensions.

Therefore:

\[
[n]=1
\]

Dimensionless quantities are especially useful in comparing physical systems and developing universal relationships.

## 11. Order of Magnitude and Estimation

Order of magnitude is an approximate representation of the scale of a quantity using the nearest power of ten.

For example:

\[
3.2\times10^5
\]

has an order of magnitude of approximately:

\[
10^5
\]

Estimation helps identify unreasonable numerical answers.

Examples:

- Human height is approximately of the order of 10^0 metres.
- Thickness of a sheet of paper is approximately of the order of 10^-4 metres.
- Earth's radius is approximately of the order of 10^6 metres.
- Speed of light is approximately of the order of 10^8 metres per second.

## Common Exam Traps

- The SI unit of mass is kilogram, not gram.
- The SI unit of temperature is kelvin.
- Area and volume conversion factors must be squared and cubed respectively.
- Dimensional consistency does not prove that an equation is physically correct.
- Work and torque have identical dimensions but are different physical quantities.
- A dimensionless quantity can still have a named unit in some contexts; plane angle, for example, is measured in radians.
- Dimensional analysis cannot determine pure numerical constants.
- Frequency and angular frequency both have dimensions of inverse time, even though they describe different quantities.
