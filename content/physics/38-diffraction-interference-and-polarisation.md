title: Diffraction, Interference and Polarisation
level: intermediate
---

Diffraction, interference and polarisation are important wave phenomena that provide evidence for the wave nature of light.

Although these phenomena are related, each describes a different aspect of wave behaviour.

## 1. Interference of Light

Interference occurs when two or more coherent light waves overlap and combine.

The resultant intensity depends on their phase difference.

Constructive interference produces increased intensity.

Destructive interference produces reduced intensity.

The pattern may contain alternating bright and dark regions.

## 2. Diffraction of Light

Diffraction is the spreading or bending of waves around obstacles and through openings.

It becomes significant when the size of the obstacle or opening is comparable to the wavelength.

Diffraction is not restricted to light. Sound waves and water waves also exhibit diffraction.

## 3. Single-Slit Diffraction

Consider monochromatic light passing through a narrow slit of width a.

The wave spreads after passing through the slit and produces a diffraction pattern on a distant screen.

The pattern consists of:
- A broad central bright maximum.
- Weaker secondary maxima.
- Dark minima between maxima.

## 4. Condition for Minima in Single-Slit Diffraction

For a slit of width a, dark fringes occur approximately when:

a sin θ = mλ

Where:
- a = slit width
- θ = angle from the central axis
- λ = wavelength
- m = 1, 2, 3, ...

The equation gives the angular positions of the diffraction minima.

For small angles:

sin θ ≈ θ

Therefore:

θ ≈ mλ/a

## 5. Width of the Central Maximum

For a screen at distance D, with small diffraction angles:

The first minima occur at approximately:

y = ±λD/a

Therefore, the central maximum width is:

W = 2λD/a

The central maximum is twice as wide as the distance from the centre to the first minimum.

## 6. Factors Affecting Diffraction

Diffraction becomes more noticeable when:
- Wavelength increases.
- Slit width decreases.
- Obstacle dimensions become comparable to wavelength.

For visible light, diffraction is usually difficult to notice around large everyday objects because visible wavelengths are very small.

## 7. Difference Between Interference and Diffraction

| Interference | Diffraction |
|---|---|
| Usually involves superposition of waves from distinct coherent sources | Arises from different parts of the same wavefront or aperture |
| Fringe spacing may be uniform in ideal double-slit arrangements | Central maximum is broader in single-slit diffraction |
| Bright fringes can have similar intensity for equal source amplitudes | Secondary maxima generally decrease in intensity |
| Requires coherent contributions | Occurs naturally when waves pass through apertures or around obstacles |

Both phenomena are consequences of wave superposition.

## 8. Diffraction Grating

A diffraction grating contains a large number of equally spaced parallel slits.

Each slit produces diffracted waves.

The waves interfere to produce sharp principal maxima.

For normal incidence:

d sin θ = mλ

Where:
- d = grating spacing
- θ = diffraction angle
- m = diffraction order
- λ = wavelength

For a grating with N lines per unit length:

d = 1/N

A diffraction grating can separate light into different wavelengths.

## 9. Resolving Power of a Diffraction Grating

Resolving power describes the ability to distinguish two nearby wavelengths.

For an ideal grating:

R = λ/Δλ = mN

Where:
- λ = mean wavelength
- Δλ = smallest resolvable wavelength difference
- m = diffraction order
- N = total number of illuminated grating lines

Higher diffraction order and more illuminated lines improve resolving power, within practical limitations.

## 10. Polarisation of Light

Polarisation is the restriction of the direction of oscillation of the electric field of a light wave.

Ordinary unpolarised light contains electric field oscillations in many transverse directions.

Plane-polarised light has its electric field oscillating in one defined transverse direction.

Polarisation demonstrates that light is a transverse wave.

## 11. Polariser and Analyser

A polariser converts unpolarised light into linearly polarised light.

An analyser is another polarising element used to examine the orientation of polarised light.

When the transmission axes of a polariser and analyser are parallel, transmitted intensity is maximum.

When they are perpendicular, ideal transmitted intensity is zero.

## 12. Malus's Law

Malus's law describes the intensity of linearly polarised light passing through an analyser.

I = I0 cos² θ

Where:
- I = transmitted intensity
- I0 = intensity incident on the analyser
- θ = angle between the light's polarisation direction and the analyser transmission axis

Important cases:

θ = 0 degrees:

I = I0

θ = 45 degrees:

I = I0/2

θ = 90 degrees:

I = 0

The last result assumes an ideal analyser and perfectly polarised incident light.

## 13. Polarisation by Reflection

When unpolarised light is reflected from a transparent dielectric surface at a particular angle, the reflected light can become completely plane-polarised.

This angle is called Brewster's angle.

Brewster's law is:

tan iB = n2/n1

Where:
- iB = Brewster's angle
- n1 = refractive index of the incident medium
- n2 = refractive index of the reflecting medium

At Brewster's angle, the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular.

## 14. Polarisation by Scattering

Light can become partially polarised when it is scattered by small particles in the atmosphere.

This phenomenon contributes to the polarisation of skylight.

It is useful in:
- Atmospheric studies.
- Polarisation-based photography.
- Remote sensing.
- Optical measurement systems.

## 15. Applications of Polarisation

Polarisation is used in:

- Polarised sunglasses.
- LCD screens.
- Photography filters.
- Stress analysis of transparent materials.
- Optical communication.
- Scientific instruments.
- Remote sensing.

Polarised sunglasses reduce glare from certain reflected light.

## 16. Numerical Applications

**Example 1: Single-slit diffraction**

A slit has width 0.2 mm and is illuminated by light of wavelength 500 nm.

Find the angle of the first minimum.

a sin θ = λ

sin θ = λ/a

sin θ = (500 × 10^-9)/(0.2 × 10^-3)

sin θ = 0.0025

For a small angle:

θ ≈ 0.0025 radians

**Example 2: Malus's law**

Polarised light of intensity 100 W/m² passes through an analyser at 60 degrees.

I = I0 cos² θ

I = 100 × cos² 60 degrees

I = 100 × 0.25

I = 25 W/m²

**Example 3: Grating equation**

A diffraction grating has spacing 2 × 10^-6 m. Light of wavelength 500 nm is incident normally.

For first order:

d sin θ = λ

sin θ = (500 × 10^-9)/(2 × 10^-6)

sin θ = 0.25

θ ≈ 14.5 degrees

## Common Exam Traps

- Diffraction is wave spreading around obstacles and through openings.
- Diffraction becomes significant when aperture size is comparable to wavelength.
- The central maximum in single-slit diffraction is twice the width of the central-to-first-minimum distance.
- A smaller slit produces greater angular diffraction.
- A diffraction grating produces sharp principal maxima.
- Polarisation is possible for transverse waves.
- Malus's law involves the square of cosine.
- Crossed ideal polarisers transmit no light.
- Brewster's angle is associated with complete polarisation of reflected light under ideal conditions.
- Interference and diffraction both arise from wave superposition, but their physical arrangements differ.
