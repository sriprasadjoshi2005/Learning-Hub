title: Quantum Nature of Light and Photoelectric Effect
level: advanced

Classical wave theory successfully explains many optical phenomena, including reflection, refraction, interference and diffraction.

However, certain experiments involving light cannot be fully explained using classical wave theory alone.

The photoelectric effect provided important evidence that electromagnetic radiation also behaves as discrete packets of energy called photons.

## 1. Classical and Quantum Descriptions of Light

Classical electromagnetic theory describes light as an electromagnetic wave.

Quantum theory describes light as being exchanged and detected in discrete packets called photons.

These two descriptions are not contradictory.

Light exhibits wave-particle duality:
- Wave behaviour appears in interference and diffraction.
- Particle-like behaviour appears in phenomena such as the photoelectric effect.

A complete description requires quantum physics.

## 2. Planck's Quantum Hypothesis

Max Planck proposed that electromagnetic energy is exchanged in discrete amounts rather than arbitrary continuous values in certain physical processes.

The energy of an oscillator is quantised:

E = nhf

Where:
- n = non-negative integer
- h = Planck's constant
- f = frequency

Planck's constant is:

h = 6.626 × 10^-34 J s

This idea played a central role in the development of quantum theory.

## 3. Photons

A photon is a quantum of electromagnetic radiation.

Its energy is:

E = hf

Since c = fλ:

E = hc/λ

Photon momentum is:

p = h/λ

Important properties:
- Photons have zero rest mass.
- Photons carry energy and momentum.
- Their energy depends on frequency.
- Their momentum depends on wavelength.
- They travel at the speed of light in vacuum.

## 4. Photoelectric Effect

The photoelectric effect is the emission of electrons from a material surface when electromagnetic radiation of suitable frequency falls on it.

The emitted electrons are called photoelectrons.

The phenomenon is commonly studied using a photosensitive metal surface.

A typical experimental arrangement contains:
- A photosensitive cathode.
- An anode.
- An evacuated tube.
- A variable voltage source.
- A light source.
- A current-measuring instrument.

When suitable light illuminates the cathode, photoelectrons may be emitted and collected by the anode.

## 5. Experimental Observations

Important observations of the photoelectric effect include:

### Threshold Frequency

For each material, there is a minimum frequency below which photoelectric emission does not occur.

This minimum frequency is called the threshold frequency.

### Immediate Emission

When the incident frequency is above threshold, emission begins without a measurable macroscopic delay under ordinary experimental conditions, even at low light intensity.

### Dependence on Frequency

The maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons increases with incident light frequency.

### Dependence on Intensity

At a fixed frequency above threshold, increasing light intensity increases the number of emitted photoelectrons per unit time under suitable conditions.

The maximum kinetic energy does not increase merely because intensity increases.

## 6. Classical Wave Theory's Difficulty

Classical wave theory predicts that increasing light intensity should increase the energy transferred to electrons.

It would also allow sufficient energy to accumulate over time even for low-frequency light.

However, experiments show:
- There is a threshold frequency.
- Emission occurs immediately above threshold.
- Maximum kinetic energy depends on frequency rather than intensity.

These observations required a quantum explanation.

## 7. Einstein's Photoelectric Equation

Einstein explained the photoelectric effect using photons.

Each electron absorbs energy from an individual photon.

The photon energy is:

E = hf

A portion of this energy is required to release the electron from the material.

This minimum energy is the work function.

The remaining energy becomes the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electron.

Einstein's equation:

hf = φ + Kmax

Therefore:

Kmax = hf - φ

Where:
- φ = work function
- Kmax = maximum kinetic energy

The equation is based on energy conservation.

## 8. Work Function

The work function is the minimum energy required to remove an electron from the surface of a material.

It is commonly expressed in electronvolts (eV).

One electronvolt is:

1 eV = 1.602 × 10^-19 J

Different materials have different work functions.

A photon must have sufficient energy to overcome the work function for photoelectric emission to occur.

## 9. Threshold Frequency

At the threshold frequency, the emitted electron has zero maximum kinetic energy in the idealised equation.

Therefore:

h f0 = φ

Threshold frequency:

f0 = φ/h

For frequencies below f0:

hf is less than φ

No photoelectric emission occurs in the simple one-photon photoelectric model.

## 10. Threshold Wavelength

Since:

c = fλ

The threshold wavelength is:

λ0 = c/f0

Using φ = hf0:

λ0 = hc/φ

Photoelectric emission occurs when the incident wavelength is sufficiently short, meaning the photon frequency is sufficiently high.

## 11. Maximum Kinetic Energy

For incident frequency f:

Kmax = h(f - f0)

This shows that maximum kinetic energy increases linearly with frequency.

The slope of a graph of maximum kinetic energy against frequency is Planck's constant.

## 12. Stopping Potential

Stopping potential is the minimum reverse potential difference required to stop even the fastest photoelectrons from reaching the collector.

The maximum kinetic energy is related to stopping potential by:

Kmax = eVs

Where:
- e = elementary charge magnitude
- Vs = stopping potential magnitude

Combining with Einstein's equation:

eVs = hf - φ

Therefore:

Vs = (h/e)f - φ/e

A graph of stopping potential against frequency is a straight line.

Its slope is h/e.

## 13. Effect of Light Intensity

For fixed frequency above threshold:

Increasing light intensity increases the number of incident photons per second.

This generally increases the photoelectric current because more electrons can be emitted per second.

However:

Kmax = hf - φ

The maximum kinetic energy remains unchanged if frequency is unchanged.

This distinction is one of the most important photoelectric-effect concepts.

## 14. Effect of Frequency

Increasing the frequency of incident radiation increases photon energy.

Since:

E = hf

A higher frequency produces more energetic photons.

For frequency above threshold, the maximum kinetic energy of emitted electrons increases.

For frequency below threshold, increasing intensity alone cannot produce emission in the ordinary one-photon photoelectric effect.

## 15. Photoelectric Current and Potential Difference

The photoelectric current depends on the number of photoelectrons collected per second.

When the collector is made sufficiently positive:
- More emitted electrons are collected.
- Current increases.
- Eventually, saturation current is reached.

At saturation, nearly all emitted photoelectrons are collected.

When a sufficiently negative retarding potential is applied:
- Fewer electrons reach the collector.
- Current decreases.
- Current becomes zero at the stopping potential.

## 16. Applications of the Photoelectric Effect

Photoelectric principles are used in:
- Photodiodes.
- Light sensors.
- Automatic lighting systems.
- Optical detectors.
- Photomultiplier tubes.
- Solar energy technologies, through related semiconductor photovoltaic processes.

The external photoelectric effect and photovoltaic effect are distinct physical mechanisms, although both involve light interacting with electrons.

## 17. Numerical Applications

**Example 1: Photon energy**

Calculate the energy of a photon with frequency 6 × 10^14 Hz.

E = hf

E = 6.626 × 10^-34 × 6 × 10^14

E = 3.976 × 10^-19 J

**Example 2: Photoelectric kinetic energy**

A photon has energy 5 eV. The work function of a metal is 2 eV.

Kmax = E - φ

Kmax = 5 - 2

Kmax = 3 eV

**Example 3: Stopping potential**

If the maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons is 2 eV:

eVs = 2 eV

Therefore:

Vs = 2 V

## Common Exam Traps

- Photon energy is proportional to frequency.
- Photon energy is inversely proportional to wavelength.
- Threshold frequency depends on the material.
- Increasing intensity above threshold increases photoelectric current, not maximum kinetic energy.
- Increasing frequency increases maximum kinetic energy.
- No ordinary one-photon photoelectric emission occurs below threshold frequency.
- Work function is the minimum energy required to release an electron.
- Stopping potential measures maximum photoelectron kinetic energy per unit charge.
- The slope of a Kmax versus frequency graph is Planck's constant.
- Wave-particle duality means light exhibits both wave-like and particle-like behaviour.
