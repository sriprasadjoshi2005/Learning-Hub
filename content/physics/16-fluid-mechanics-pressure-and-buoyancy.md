Fluid mechanics studies the behaviour of liquids and gases at rest and in motion. It is important in engineering, atmospheric science, medicine, transportation, and many natural processes.

This lesson introduces fluid pressure, hydrostatic equilibrium, Pascal's principle, buoyancy, Archimedes' principle, and the conditions under which objects float or sink.

## 1. What Is a Fluid?

A fluid is a substance that can flow and continuously deform under an applied shear stress.

Liquids and gases are fluids.

Liquids:
- Have approximately fixed volume.
- Take the shape of their container.
- Are relatively difficult to compress.

Gases:
- Have no fixed shape or volume.
- Expand to fill their containers.
- Are generally more compressible than liquids.

## 2. Density

Density is mass per unit volume.

\[
\rho=\frac{m}{V}
\]

SI unit:

\[
kg/m^3
\]

Density is an important property used to compare materials and determine whether objects float or sink.

The relative density of a substance is:

\[
\text{Relative density}=
\frac{\text{Density of substance}}{\text{Density of reference substance}}
\]

For solids and liquids, the reference is commonly water at a specified temperature.

Relative density has no units.

## 3. Pressure

Pressure is the normal force acting per unit area.

\[
P=\frac{F}{A}
\]

SI unit:

\[
Pa=N/m^2
\]

Pressure increases when the same force acts over a smaller area.

Examples:
- A sharp knife cuts more effectively than a blunt knife.
- Snowshoes reduce pressure by increasing contact area.
- High-heeled footwear produces greater pressure than flat footwear for the same applied force.

## 4. Pressure in a Static Fluid

Pressure increases with depth because deeper layers support the weight of more fluid above them.

For a fluid of constant density:

\[
P=P_0+\rho gh
\]

Where:
- P = absolute pressure at depth
- P0 = pressure at the surface
- rho = fluid density
- g = gravitational acceleration
- h = depth below the surface

The gauge pressure is:

\[
P_{\text{gauge}}=\rho gh
\]

Absolute pressure is the sum of atmospheric pressure and gauge pressure.

## 5. Important Properties of Fluid Pressure

In a fluid at rest:
- Pressure acts perpendicular to a surface.
- Pressure at the same depth is equal in a connected fluid of uniform density.
- Pressure increases with depth.
- Pressure depends on density and gravitational acceleration.
- Pressure at a given depth does not depend directly on the container's shape.

This explains why differently shaped containers can have equal pressure at the same depth.

## 6. Pascal's Principle

Pascal's principle states that a pressure change applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted throughout the fluid.

For a hydraulic system:

\[
P_1=P_2
\]

\[
\frac{F_1}{A_1}=\frac{F_2}{A_2}
\]

Therefore:

\[
F_2=F_1\frac{A_2}{A_1}
\]

A hydraulic machine can multiply force by using a larger output piston.

However, the output piston moves a smaller distance than the input piston in an ideal system.

Conservation of energy is maintained.

## 7. Buoyant Force

When an object is immersed in a fluid, the fluid exerts an upward force on it.

This force is called buoyant force or upthrust.

The buoyant force occurs because fluid pressure increases with depth, producing a greater upward force on the bottom of an immersed object than the downward force on its top.

## 8. Archimedes' Principle

Archimedes' principle states that the buoyant force acting on an immersed body equals the weight of the fluid displaced by that body.

\[
F_B=\rho_f gV_d
\]

Where:
- rho_f = density of fluid
- V_d = volume of displaced fluid

The buoyant force acts upward through the centre of buoyancy.

For a fully submerged object, the displaced volume equals the object's volume.

For a floating object, only the submerged portion displaces fluid.

## 9. Floating and Sinking

An object floats when its weight is balanced by the buoyant force.

\[
F_B=mg
\]

For an object fully submerged in a fluid:
- If object density is greater than fluid density, it tends to sink.
- If object density is less than fluid density, it tends to rise.
- If the densities are equal, it can remain suspended in equilibrium.

A floating object displaces a quantity of fluid whose weight equals its own weight.

The fraction of a uniform floating object's volume submerged is:

\[
\frac{V_{\text{submerged}}}{V_{\text{total}}}
=
\frac{\rho_{\text{object}}}{\rho_{\text{fluid}}}
\]

## 10. Apparent Weight

The apparent weight of an immersed object is reduced by the buoyant force.

\[
W_{\text{apparent}}=W-F_B
\]

This explains why an object feels lighter when held underwater.

## 11. Surface Tension

Surface tension is the tendency of a liquid surface to minimise its surface area due to cohesive intermolecular forces.

It is defined as force per unit length:

\[
T=\frac{F}{L}
\]

Its SI unit is N/m.

Surface tension explains:
- Formation of spherical droplets.
- Floating of small insects on water.
- Capillary effects.
- The behaviour of soap bubbles.

Surface tension generally decreases as temperature increases.

## 12. Capillarity

Capillarity is the rise or fall of a liquid in a narrow tube due to surface tension and interactions between the liquid and the tube material.

For a circular capillary tube:

\[
h=\frac{2T\cos\theta}{\rho gr}
\]

Where:
- T = surface tension
- theta = contact angle
- rho = liquid density
- r = tube radius

The height of capillary rise is inversely proportional to tube radius.

Water generally rises in clean glass, whereas mercury is depressed.

## 13. Worked Numerical Examples

**Example 1: Pressure at depth**

Calculate the gauge pressure at a depth of 5 m in water. Take rho = 1000 kg/m³ and g = 9.8 m/s².

\[
P=\rho gh
\]

\[
P=1000\times9.8\times5
\]

\[
\boxed{P=49,000\,Pa}
\]

**Example 2: Hydraulic machine**

An input piston has area 0.01 m² and receives a force of 100 N. The output piston has area 0.5 m².

\[
F_2=F_1\frac{A_2}{A_1}
\]

\[
F_2=100\times\frac{0.5}{0.01}
\]

\[
\boxed{F_2=5000\,N}
\]

**Example 3: Buoyant force**

A body displaces 0.003 m³ of water. Calculate the buoyant force using rho = 1000 kg/m³ and g = 9.8 m/s².

\[
F_B=\rho gV
\]

\[
F_B=1000\times9.8\times0.003
\]

\[
\boxed{F_B=29.4\,N}
\]

## 14. Applications

- Hydraulic brakes and lifts.
- Ships and submarines.
- Floating and sinking of objects.
- Water supply systems.
- Dams and underwater structures.
- Medical blood-pressure measurement.
- Capillary action in plants and porous materials.

## Common Exam Traps

- Pressure is force per unit area, not simply force.
- Hydrostatic pressure depends on depth, density, and gravitational acceleration.
- Gauge pressure does not include atmospheric pressure.
- Pascal's principle concerns pressure transmission through an enclosed fluid.
- Buoyant force depends on displaced fluid, not simply the mass of the object.
- Floating objects displace fluid whose weight equals their own weight.
- A floating object may have only part of its volume submerged.
