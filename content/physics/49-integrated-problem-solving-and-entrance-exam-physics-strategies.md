Physics problems often combine multiple concepts. A question involving a moving object may require Newton's laws, energy conservation and momentum. An electrical problem may involve circuit analysis, electromagnetic induction and alternating current.

This lesson develops a systematic approach to solving complex physics problems, identifying suitable equations and avoiding common mathematical and conceptual mistakes.

## 1. The Importance of Integrated Problem Solving

Physics is a connected subject.

Examples of interconnected concepts include:

- Mechanics and energy conservation.
- Circular motion and gravitation.
- Oscillations and differential equations.
- Waves and interference.
- Electricity and magnetism.
- Electromagnetic induction and AC circuits.
- Optics and wave phenomena.
- Quantum physics and atomic structure.
- Nuclear physics and mass-energy equivalence.

A strong problem-solving method begins by identifying the underlying physical principles rather than immediately selecting a formula.

## 2. A Systematic Problem-Solving Method

Use the following procedure:

1. Read the question carefully.
2. Identify the physical system.
3. List the known quantities.
4. Identify the required quantity.
5. Draw a suitable diagram.
6. Choose a coordinate system.
7. Identify relevant physical laws.
8. Write equations symbolically.
9. Substitute numerical values.
10. Calculate the answer.
11. Check dimensions and units.
12. Evaluate whether the result is physically reasonable.

Writing equations before substituting numbers reduces calculation errors.

## 3. Free-Body Diagrams

A free-body diagram shows all external forces acting on a selected object.

Important forces include:
- Weight.
- Normal reaction.
- Friction.
- Tension.
- Spring force.
- Applied force.
- Drag, where relevant.

Newton's second law:

\[
\sum\vec F=m\vec a
\]

Resolve forces into suitable perpendicular directions.

For a horizontal surface:

\[
\sum F_x=ma_x
\]

\[
\sum F_y=ma_y
\]

Do not include internal forces acting between objects inside the selected system when constructing the system's external-force equation.

## 4. Choosing Between Newton's Laws and Energy Conservation

Use Newton's laws when:
- Acceleration is required.
- Forces are explicitly involved.
- Contact forces or tension must be determined.
- Motion is constrained by a particular geometry.

Use energy conservation when:
- Initial and final speeds are required.
- The path is complicated but only endpoint states matter.
- Conservative forces dominate.
- Work by non-conservative forces can be calculated.

Mechanical energy conservation:

\[
K_i+U_i=K_f+U_f
\]

When non-conservative work is present:

\[
K_i+U_i+W_{nc}=K_f+U_f
\]

## 5. Momentum and Collision Problems

For an isolated system:

\[
\vec p_i=\vec p_f
\]

For two objects moving in one dimension:

\[
m_1u_1+m_2u_2=m_1v_1+m_2v_2
\]

In a perfectly inelastic collision, the objects move together after impact.

\[
v=\frac{m_1u_1+m_2u_2}{m_1+m_2}
\]

Kinetic energy is generally not conserved in inelastic collisions.

## 6. Circular Motion and Gravitation

Centripetal acceleration:

\[
a_c=\frac{v^2}{r}
\]

Centripetal force:

\[
F_c=\frac{mv^2}{r}
\]

Newtonian gravitational force:

\[
F=\frac{GMm}{r^2}
\]

For a circular orbit, equate gravitational force to centripetal force:

\[
\frac{GMm}{r^2}=\frac{mv^2}{r}
\]

Therefore:

\[
v=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}
\]

This method connects gravitation with circular motion.

## 7. Oscillation Problems

For simple harmonic motion:

\[
a=-\omega^2x
\]

Displacement:

\[
x=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)
\]

Velocity:

\[
v=-A\omega\sin(\omega t+\phi)
\]

Maximum speed:

\[
v_{\max}=A\omega
\]

Maximum acceleration:

\[
a_{\max}=A\omega^2
\]

The total energy is:

\[
E=\frac12m\omega^2A^2
\]

The acceleration is always directed towards the equilibrium position in ideal SHM.

## 8. Wave Problems

Wave speed:

\[
v=f\lambda
\]

For a string under tension:

\[
v=\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}
\]

Where mu is the mass per unit length.

For two coherent waves, interference depends on their phase difference.

Constructive interference:

\[
\Delta x=n\lambda
\]

Destructive interference:

\[
\Delta x=\left(n+\frac12\right)\lambda
\]

These conditions assume suitable coherent waves of equal or comparable amplitudes.

## 9. Electrostatics and Circuit Problems

Coulomb's law:

\[
F=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2}
\]

Electric field:

\[
E=\frac{F}{q}
\]

Electric potential difference:

\[
V=\frac{W}{q}
\]

Capacitance:

\[
C=\frac{Q}{V}
\]

Ohm's law:

\[
V=IR
\]

Electrical power:

\[
P=VI=I^2R=\frac{V^2}{R}
\]

For series resistors:

\[
R_s=R_1+R_2+\cdots
\]

For parallel resistors:

\[
\frac1{R_p}=\frac1{R_1}+\frac1{R_2}+\cdots
\]

## 10. Kirchhoff's Laws

Kirchhoff's current law:

The algebraic sum of currents at a junction is zero.

\[
\sum I=0
\]

Kirchhoff's voltage law:

The algebraic sum of potential differences around a closed loop is zero.

\[
\sum V=0
\]

Choose current directions consistently. A negative calculated current indicates that the actual current flows opposite to the assumed direction.

## 11. Magnetism and Electromagnetic Induction

Magnetic force on a moving charge:

\[
F=qvB\sin\theta
\]

Magnetic force on a current-carrying conductor:

\[
F=BIL\sin\theta
\]

Magnetic flux:

\[
\Phi=BA\cos\theta
\]

Faraday's law:

\[
\mathcal E=-N\frac{d\Phi}{dt}
\]

The negative sign represents Lenz's law.

A changing magnetic flux through a circuit produces an induced electromotive force.

## 12. Optics Problems

For a thin lens:

\[
\frac1f=\frac1v-\frac1u
\]

Using the Cartesian sign convention:

- Distances measured in the positive direction are positive.
- Distances measured in the negative direction are negative.

Magnification:

\[
m=\frac{h_i}{h_o}=\frac{v}{u}
\]

For refraction at a plane boundary, Snell's law is:

\[
n_1\sin i=n_2\sin r
\]

The critical angle for light travelling from a denser to a rarer medium satisfies:

\[
\sin C=\frac{n_2}{n_1}
\]

where n1 is greater than n2.

## 13. Modern Physics Problems

Photon energy:

\[
E=hf=\frac{hc}{\lambda}
\]

Einstein's photoelectric equation:

\[
hf=\phi+K_{\max}
\]

De Broglie wavelength:

\[
\lambda=\frac{h}{p}
\]

Radioactive decay:

\[
N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}
\]

Mass-energy equivalence:

\[
E=mc^2
\]

These equations are especially important in quantum and nuclear physics problems.

## 14. Dimensional Analysis as a Verification Tool

Dimensional analysis helps identify incorrect equations.

For example, kinetic energy is:

\[
K=\frac12mv^2
\]

Dimensions:

\[
[M][LT^{-1}]^2
\]

\[
=ML^2T^{-2}
\]

This matches the dimensions of work and energy.

Dimensional analysis can verify dimensional consistency but cannot establish that an equation is physically correct.

## 15. Worked Integrated Problem: Block Sliding Down a Smooth Incline

A block starts from rest at the top of a smooth inclined plane.

The vertical height is 5 m.

Take:

\[
g=9.8\text{ m/s}^2
\]

Find the speed at the bottom.

Since the plane is smooth, mechanical energy is conserved.

At the top:

\[
K_i=0
\]

\[
U_i=mgh
\]

At the bottom:

\[
K_f=\frac12mv^2
\]

\[
U_f=0
\]

Conservation of mechanical energy:

\[
mgh=\frac12mv^2
\]

Cancel mass:

\[
gh=\frac12v^2
\]

Therefore:

\[
v=\sqrt{2gh}
\]

\[
v=\sqrt{2(9.8)(5)}
\]

\[
\boxed{v\approx9.90\text{ m/s}}
\]

Notice that the final speed does not depend on the mass of the block or the angle of the smooth incline when the vertical height is fixed.

## 16. Worked Integrated Problem: Series RLC Circuit at Resonance

A series RLC circuit has:

- L = 0.1 H.
- C = 100 microfarad.
- R = 20 ohm.

Calculate the resonant frequency.

At resonance:

\[
X_L=X_C
\]

The angular resonant frequency is:

\[
\omega_0=\frac1{\sqrt{LC}}
\]

Substitute:

\[
\omega_0=\frac1{\sqrt{0.1\times100\times10^{-6}}}
\]

\[
\omega_0=316.23\text{ rad/s}
\]

Frequency:

\[
f_0=\frac{\omega_0}{2\pi}
\]

\[
\boxed{f_0\approx50.33\text{ Hz}}
\]

At resonance, the ideal series circuit has impedance equal to its resistance.

## 17. Entrance-Exam Strategy

**Conceptual questions:**

- Identify the physical principle before calculating.
- Understand the direction of vectors.
- Distinguish similar physical quantities.
- Use diagrams where appropriate.

**Numerical questions:**

- Convert units first.
- Write the relevant equation.
- Substitute values carefully.
- Check the final unit.
- Estimate the answer to detect unreasonable results.

**Multiple-choice questions:**

- Eliminate dimensionally incorrect choices.
- Check limiting cases.
- Look for sign and direction errors.
- Avoid selecting a familiar formula without checking its conditions.

## 18. Common Mathematical Mistakes

- Confusing degrees and radians.
- Forgetting vector directions.
- Using diameter instead of radius.
- Mixing centimetres and metres.
- Ignoring negative signs in optics.
- Applying energy conservation when non-conservative work is present without accounting for it.
- Using peak AC values in RMS formulas.
- Confusing frequency with angular frequency.

## 19. Efficient Revision Strategy

A strong revision plan should include:

1. Conceptual understanding.
2. Formula derivations.
3. Standard numerical problems.
4. Graph interpretation.
5. Mixed-topic problems.
6. Timed practice.
7. Error analysis.
8. Repeated revision of weak concepts.

Do not rely exclusively on memorising equations. Understand their assumptions and physical meaning.

## Common Exam Traps

- Energy and momentum are different conservation principles.
- Mechanical energy is conserved only when non-conservative work is absent or properly accounted for.
- Centripetal force is the net inward force, not an additional independent force.
- Current and voltage must follow consistent sign conventions in circuit analysis.
- A negative answer may represent direction rather than an incorrect calculation.
- Dimensional consistency does not prove an equation is correct.
- Always check whether an equation assumes constant acceleration, ideal conditions, small oscillations or another restriction.

## Quiz

1. A block slides down a smooth incline from a vertical height h. Its final speed, starting from rest, is:

- [ ] gh
- [ ] sqrt(gh)
- [x] sqrt(2gh)
- [ ] 2gh

> Conservation of mechanical energy gives mgh = (1/2)mv². Rearranging gives v = sqrt(2gh).

2. In a series RLC circuit at resonance, the impedance is:

- [ ] Zero for every circuit
- [x] Equal to the resistance
- [ ] Equal to the sum of inductive and capacitive reactances
- [ ] Infinite

> At resonance, inductive and capacitive reactances cancel. The impedance becomes Z = R.

3. Which method is particularly useful for checking the dimensional consistency of a physical equation?

- [ ] Boolean algebra
- [ ] Geometrical optics
- [x] Dimensional analysis
- [ ] Nuclear fission

> Dimensional analysis compares the fundamental dimensions on both sides of an equation to detect dimensional inconsistencies.
