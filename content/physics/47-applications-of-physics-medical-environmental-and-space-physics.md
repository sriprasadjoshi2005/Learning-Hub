Physics is not limited to laboratory experiments or mathematical descriptions of natural laws. It provides the principles behind medical imaging, environmental monitoring, renewable energy, satellite technology, astronomy and space exploration.

This lesson connects the major branches of physics with practical technologies and scientific applications.

## 1. Medical Physics

Medical physics applies physical principles to the diagnosis, treatment and monitoring of diseases.

Important areas include:
- Diagnostic imaging.
- Radiation therapy.
- Nuclear medicine.
- Ultrasound.
- Magnetic resonance imaging.
- Medical instrumentation.

Medical physics combines electromagnetism, nuclear physics, wave physics, quantum mechanics and electronics.

## 2. X-Ray Imaging

X-rays are high-frequency electromagnetic waves capable of penetrating many materials.

In medical imaging, X-rays pass through the body and are detected on the opposite side.

Different tissues absorb X-rays to different degrees.

**Bone:**

Generally absorbs more X-rays than soft tissue because of its composition and density.

**Soft tissue:**

Allows a larger fraction of X-rays to pass through.

The resulting differences in detected intensity create an image.

## 3. X-Ray Production

X-rays can be produced in an X-ray tube.

Important components include:
- Cathode.
- Heated filament.
- Accelerating potential difference.
- Anode target.

Electrons emitted by the cathode are accelerated towards the target.

When they interact with the target material, X-rays are produced through processes including:
- Bremsstrahlung radiation.
- Characteristic X-ray emission.

The maximum photon energy is:

\[
E_{\max}=eV
\]

Where V is the accelerating potential difference.

## 4. Computed Tomography

Computed tomography (CT) uses multiple X-ray measurements taken from different directions.

A computer reconstructs cross-sectional images of the body.

By combining many slices, a three-dimensional representation can be obtained.

CT is useful for examining:
- Bone injuries.
- Internal bleeding.
- Lung structures.
- Tumours.
- Complex anatomical regions.

Because CT uses ionising radiation, exposure must be managed appropriately.

## 5. Magnetic Resonance Imaging

MRI uses strong magnetic fields and radiofrequency pulses to obtain detailed images of internal body structures.

It relies primarily on the magnetic properties of hydrogen nuclei in the body.

The main concepts include:
- Nuclear magnetic moments.
- Alignment in a magnetic field.
- Resonance.
- Relaxation.
- Spatial magnetic-field gradients.

MRI does not use ionising X-ray radiation.

It is especially useful for imaging soft tissues, the brain, spinal structures and joints.

## 6. Ultrasound Imaging

Ultrasound uses high-frequency sound waves, typically above the upper limit of human hearing.

A transducer emits sound pulses into the body.

When the waves encounter boundaries between tissues, some energy is reflected.

The reflected echoes are detected and processed into an image.

The time delay provides information about the depth of reflecting structures.

For a pulse travelling to a boundary and back:

\[
d=\frac{vt}{2}
\]

Where:
- d = depth.
- v = sound speed in the medium.
- t = round-trip travel time.

The factor of two accounts for the outward and return journeys.

## 7. Doppler Ultrasound

The Doppler effect describes changes in observed frequency caused by relative motion between a wave source and an observer.

Doppler ultrasound uses this principle to measure blood-flow velocity.

The frequency shift depends on:
- Blood velocity.
- Transmitted ultrasound frequency.
- Angle between the ultrasound beam and the flow direction.
- Speed of sound in tissue.

It is useful in cardiovascular diagnosis.

## 8. Radiation Therapy

Radiation therapy uses ionising radiation to damage the DNA of cancer cells.

Common techniques include:
- External-beam radiotherapy.
- Brachytherapy.
- Stereotactic radiotherapy.

The aim is to deliver a prescribed radiation dose to a target while limiting exposure to healthy tissue.

Radiation dose is measured in gray (Gy).

\[
1\text{ Gy}=1\text{ J/kg}
\]

The sievert (Sv) is used for radiation protection quantities that account for biological effects.

Gray and sievert are not interchangeable units.

## 9. Environmental Physics

Environmental physics applies physical laws to the atmosphere, oceans, land, climate and energy systems.

It includes:
- Atmospheric radiation.
- Heat transfer.
- Fluid dynamics.
- Solar energy.
- Wind energy.
- Pollution measurement.
- Climate processes.

## 10. Greenhouse Effect

The greenhouse effect is a natural process in which atmospheric gases absorb and emit infrared radiation.

The Earth's surface absorbs incoming solar energy and emits infrared radiation.

Greenhouse gases, including carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour, interact with this outgoing radiation.

This affects the Earth's energy balance.

Human activities that increase greenhouse-gas concentrations enhance the natural greenhouse effect and contribute to global warming.

## 11. Solar Energy

Solar energy can be converted into useful forms through several physical processes.

**Photovoltaic conversion:**

Solar cells convert light directly into electrical energy.

**Solar thermal conversion:**

Solar radiation is absorbed to heat a fluid or material.

Applications include:
- Solar water heaters.
- Concentrated solar power.
- Solar cookers.
- Photovoltaic electricity generation.

## 12. Wind Energy

Wind turbines convert kinetic energy in moving air into mechanical rotational energy and then electrical energy.

The kinetic energy of an air mass is:

\[
K=\frac12mv^2
\]

The available wind power passing through an area A is:

\[
P_{\mathrm{wind}}=\frac12\rho Av^3
\]

Where:
- rho = air density.
- A = swept area.
- v = wind speed.

The actual electrical output is lower because of aerodynamic, mechanical and electrical losses.

The cubic dependence on wind speed makes wind speed an especially important factor.

## 13. Space Physics

Space physics studies physical processes occurring in space and in the environments of planets, stars and other celestial bodies.

Important subjects include:
- Solar activity.
- Planetary magnetospheres.
- Cosmic rays.
- Space plasma.
- Solar wind.
- Radiation belts.
- Interactions between solar particles and planetary atmospheres.

## 14. Satellites and Orbital Motion

A satellite remains in orbit because its gravitational attraction provides the centripetal force required for orbital motion.

For a circular orbit:

\[
\frac{GMm}{r^2}=\frac{mv^2}{r}
\]

Simplifying:

\[
v=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}
\]

Orbital period:

\[
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM}}
\]

Where:
- M = mass of the central body.
- m = satellite mass.
- r = orbital radius.
- G = gravitational constant.

## 15. Geostationary Satellites

A geostationary satellite:
- Orbits in the equatorial plane.
- Moves in the same direction as Earth's rotation.
- Has an orbital period equal to Earth's rotational period relative to distant stars, approximately one sidereal day.
- Appears stationary relative to an observer on Earth's surface.

Such satellites are useful for:
- Communication.
- Broadcasting.
- Weather observation.

## 16. Astronomy and Astrophysics

Astrophysics applies physical laws to celestial objects.

It studies:
- Stars.
- Planets.
- Galaxies.
- Nebulae.
- Black holes.
- Neutron stars.
- Dark matter.
- Cosmic radiation.

Spectroscopy allows astronomers to study the chemical composition, temperature, motion and physical conditions of distant objects.

## 17. Redshift and the Expanding Universe

When light from a distant astronomical source is shifted towards longer wavelengths, it is called redshift.

For sufficiently small relative speeds in the classical approximation:

\[
z\approx\frac{v}{c}
\]

Where z is redshift.

For cosmological observations, redshift is commonly defined as:

\[
1+z=\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{observed}}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{emitted}}}
\]

The expansion of the universe is associated with cosmological redshift.

## 18. Worked Numerical Example: Ultrasound

An ultrasound pulse travels through tissue at 1540 m/s.

The reflected pulse returns after 100 microseconds.

Calculate the depth of the reflecting boundary.

Convert time:

\[
t=100\times10^{-6}\text{ s}
\]

Distance travelled in both directions:

\[
vt=1540(100\times10^{-6})
\]

\[
=0.154\text{ m}
\]

Depth:

\[
d=\frac{0.154}{2}
\]

\[
\boxed{d=0.077\text{ m}=7.7\text{ cm}}
\]

## Common Exam Traps

- X-rays are electromagnetic waves, not sound waves.
- MRI uses magnetic fields and radiofrequency pulses.
- Ultrasound uses mechanical sound waves.
- CT uses X-ray measurements from multiple directions.
- Radiation dose in gray measures absorbed energy per unit mass.
- Wind power depends on the cube of wind speed.
- A geostationary satellite must have a suitable equatorial orbit.
- Cosmological redshift is related to the expansion of space.
