Atomic physics studies the internal structure of atoms and the behaviour of electrons within them.

The development of atomic models transformed our understanding of matter and led to quantum mechanics, spectroscopy, semiconductor physics and modern electronics.

## 1. Structure of the Atom

An atom consists of:
- A positively charged nucleus.
- Negatively charged electrons surrounding the nucleus.

The nucleus contains:
- Protons.
- Neutrons.

Protons carry positive electric charge.

Neutrons have no net electric charge.

Electrons carry negative electric charge.

Most of the mass of an atom is concentrated in its nucleus.

The atom is electrically neutral when the number of electrons equals the number of protons.

## 2. Atomic Number and Mass Number

Atomic number Z is the number of protons in the nucleus.

Z = Number of protons

Mass number A is the total number of protons and neutrons.

A = Z + N

Where N is the number of neutrons.

The number of neutrons is:

N = A - Z

Atoms of the same element have the same atomic number.

## 3. Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

They have:
- The same atomic number.
- Different mass numbers.
- Similar chemical properties in many ordinary situations.
- Different nuclear properties.

For example, hydrogen has three commonly discussed isotopes:

- Protium: one proton, no neutron.
- Deuterium: one proton, one neutron.
- Tritium: one proton, two neutrons.

## 4. Early Atomic Models

Several atomic models were proposed as experimental knowledge developed.

### Thomson's Model

J. J. Thomson proposed that negative electrons were embedded in a positively charged distribution.

It is often called the plum pudding model.

The model could not explain the results of Rutherford's scattering experiment.

### Rutherford's Nuclear Model

Rutherford proposed that:
- Most of the atom is empty space.
- Positive charge is concentrated in a small nucleus.
- Electrons occupy the region surrounding the nucleus.

The model explained the large-angle scattering of a small fraction of alpha particles.

However, classical physics could not explain the stability of electrons in orbit around the nucleus.

### Bohr's Model

Niels Bohr introduced quantised electron orbits for the hydrogen atom.

Electrons could occupy certain permitted energy levels without continuously radiating energy.

Radiation was emitted or absorbed when electrons transitioned between energy levels.

## 5. Rutherford's Alpha-Particle Scattering Experiment

Rutherford directed alpha particles at a thin gold foil.

Most alpha particles passed through with little or no deflection.

Some were deflected through small angles.

A very small number were deflected through large angles or returned towards the source.

Conclusions:

1. Most of the atom is empty space.
2. Positive charge is concentrated in a very small region.
3. Most atomic mass is concentrated in the nucleus.
4. The nucleus is much smaller than the overall atom.

This experiment established the nuclear model of the atom.

## 6. Bohr's Postulates

Bohr proposed several important ideas for hydrogen-like atoms.

### Stationary Orbits

Electrons can occupy certain allowed stationary states without radiating energy.

### Quantisation of Angular Momentum

The electron's angular momentum is restricted to:

mvr = nħ

Where:
- m = electron mass
- v = electron speed
- r = orbital radius
- n = positive integer
- ħ = h/(2π)

### Radiation During Transitions

An electron emits or absorbs radiation when moving between allowed energy levels.

The photon energy is:

hf = |Ef - Ei|

Where Ei and Ef are the initial and final energy levels.

## 7. Radius of Bohr Orbits

For a hydrogen-like atom with nuclear charge +Ze:

rn = a0n²/Z

Where:
- rn = radius of the nth orbit
- a0 = Bohr radius
- n = principal quantum number
- Z = atomic number

The Bohr radius is approximately:

a0 = 5.29 × 10^-11 m

For hydrogen, Z = 1.

Therefore, the radius increases with n².

## 8. Energy Levels of Hydrogen-Like Atoms

The energy of the nth level is:

En = -13.6Z²/n² eV

For hydrogen:

En = -13.6/n² eV

The negative sign indicates that the electron is in a bound state relative to the zero-energy reference at infinity.

For hydrogen:

Ground state:

E1 = -13.6 eV

First excited state:

E2 = -3.4 eV

Second excited state:

E3 ≈ -1.51 eV

As n increases, energy approaches zero from below.

## 9. Ground State and Excited States

The ground state is the lowest-energy state of an atom.

An excited state is any state with energy greater than the ground-state energy.

An electron can move to a higher energy level by absorbing an appropriate amount of energy.

It can return to a lower energy level by emitting a photon.

Atoms do not generally absorb arbitrary photon energies when making transitions between discrete bound states.

## 10. Atomic Emission Spectra

When excited atoms transition from higher to lower energy states, photons are emitted.

The photon frequency is:

f = ΔE/h

The photon wavelength is:

λ = hc/ΔE

Because atomic energy levels are quantised, only certain photon energies are emitted.

This produces a line emission spectrum.

Each element has a characteristic spectral pattern.

## 11. Atomic Absorption Spectra

An atom can absorb photons whose energies match allowed transitions to higher energy levels.

A continuous spectrum passing through a cooler gas may therefore show dark absorption lines at characteristic wavelengths.

The absorption and emission spectra of an element are closely related because they involve the same energy-level differences.

## 12. Hydrogen Spectral Series

Hydrogen has several important spectral series.

### Lyman Series

Transitions terminate at n = 1.

These lines generally lie in the ultraviolet region.

### Balmer Series

Transitions terminate at n = 2.

Many prominent Balmer lines lie in the visible region.

### Paschen Series

Transitions terminate at n = 3.

These lines lie in the infrared region.

### Brackett Series

Transitions terminate at n = 4.

These lines lie in the infrared region.

### Pfund Series

Transitions terminate at n = 5.

These lines also lie in the infrared region.

## 13. Rydberg Formula

The wavelengths of hydrogen spectral lines can be calculated using the Rydberg formula.

1/λ = RH(1/n1² - 1/n2²)

Where:
- RH = Rydberg constant
- n1 = lower energy level
- n2 = higher energy level
- n2 is greater than n1

For hydrogen:

RH ≈ 1.097 × 10^7 m^-1

The formula predicts the wavelengths emitted when an electron transitions from n2 to n1.

## 14. Ionisation Energy

Ionisation energy is the minimum energy required to remove an electron completely from an atom or ion in a specified initial state.

For ground-state hydrogen:

Ionisation energy = 13.6 eV

This is the energy needed to move the electron from n = 1 to the zero-energy state at infinity.

For a hydrogen-like atom:

Ground-state ionisation energy = 13.6Z² eV

## 15. Limitations of Bohr's Model

Bohr's model successfully explains many properties of hydrogen-like atoms.

However, it has important limitations:

- It does not accurately describe multi-electron atoms.
- It does not fully explain spectral line intensities.
- It does not account for the fine structure of spectral lines.
- It does not provide a complete description of electron probability distributions.
- It assumes definite electron orbits, which are not part of modern quantum mechanics.

Modern atomic theory uses quantum states and orbitals rather than fixed classical electron trajectories.

## 16. Quantum Numbers and Atomic Orbitals

Modern quantum mechanics describes electron states using quantum numbers.

### Principal Quantum Number (n)

Determines the main energy level and approximate orbital size.

n = 1, 2, 3, ...

### Azimuthal Quantum Number (l)

Determines orbital angular momentum and subshell type.

l = 0, 1, ..., n - 1

Common subshells:

- l = 0: s
- l = 1: p
- l = 2: d
- l = 3: f

### Magnetic Quantum Number (ml)

Describes the orientation of orbital angular momentum.

ml = -l, ..., 0, ..., +l

### Spin Quantum Number (ms)

Describes the intrinsic spin projection of an electron.

ms = +1/2 or -1/2

These quantum numbers are central to the modern description of atomic structure.

## 17. Pauli Exclusion Principle

The Pauli exclusion principle states that no two electrons in the same atom can have identical values for all four quantum numbers.

Consequently, an individual orbital can contain a maximum of two electrons with opposite spin projections.

This principle helps explain the organisation of electrons in atoms and the structure of the periodic table.

## 18. Applications of Atomic Spectra

Atomic spectroscopy is used in:

- Identifying chemical elements.
- Studying stars and galaxies.
- Analysing material composition.
- Measuring gas concentrations.
- Environmental monitoring.
- Plasma diagnostics.
- Chemical analysis.

Astronomers use spectral lines to determine the composition, temperature and motion of distant objects.

## 19. Numerical Applications

**Example 1: Hydrogen energy level**

Find the energy of the electron in the second Bohr orbit of hydrogen.

En = -13.6/n² eV

E2 = -13.6/2²

E2 = -3.4 eV

**Example 2: Photon energy during a transition**

An electron in hydrogen moves from n = 3 to n = 2.

Initial energy:

E3 = -13.6/9 ≈ -1.51 eV

Final energy:

E2 = -3.4 eV

Photon energy:

ΔE = E3 - E2

ΔE = -1.51 - (-3.4)

ΔE ≈ 1.89 eV

The atom emits a photon with energy approximately 1.89 eV.

**Example 3: Ionisation energy**

The ground-state electron of hydrogen has energy -13.6 eV.

The ionised state has energy zero.

Required energy:

ΔE = 0 - (-13.6)

ΔE = 13.6 eV

## Common Exam Traps

- Atomic number is the number of protons.
- Mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons.
- Isotopes have the same atomic number but different mass numbers.
- Rutherford's experiment established the nuclear model.
- Bohr's model applies successfully to hydrogen-like systems.
- Bound-state energy levels are negative when zero energy is defined at infinity.
- Emission occurs when an electron moves to a lower energy state.
- Absorption occurs when an electron moves to a higher energy state.
- Photon energy equals the magnitude of the energy difference between levels.
- The Lyman series terminates at n = 1.
- The Balmer series terminates at n = 2.
- Modern quantum mechanics describes orbitals, not fixed classical electron paths.
