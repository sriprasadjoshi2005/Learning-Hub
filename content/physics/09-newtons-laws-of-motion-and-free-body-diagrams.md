Dynamics is the branch of mechanics that studies the relationship between forces and the motion of objects.

Kinematics describes how objects move, whereas dynamics explains why their motion changes.

The foundation of classical dynamics is provided by Newton's three laws of motion.

These laws explain inertia, acceleration, momentum and interactions between objects. They are essential for understanding friction, connected bodies, circular motion, collisions and many other areas of physics.

## 1. Force and Its Nature

A force is an interaction that can change or tend to change the state of motion of an object.

A force can:

- Change an object's speed.
- Change its direction of motion.
- Change its shape.
- Produce rotational motion.
- Maintain an object in equilibrium.

Force is a vector quantity because it has both magnitude and direction.

SI unit:

\[
\text{newton (N)}
\]

One newton is the force that produces an acceleration of 1 m/s² in a mass of 1 kg.

\[
1\ N=1\ \text{kg m/s}^2
\]

Forces can be broadly classified into contact forces and non-contact forces.

**Contact forces:**

These require physical contact.

Examples:
- Friction.
- Normal reaction.
- Tension.
- Applied force.
- Air resistance.
- Spring force.

**Non-contact forces:**

These act without direct physical contact.

Examples:
- Gravitational force.
- Electric force.
- Magnetic force.

## 2. Newton's First Law of Motion

Newton's first law states:

An object remains at rest or continues moving with constant velocity in a straight line unless acted upon by a non-zero resultant external force.

This law is also called the law of inertia.

### Inertia

Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes in its state of motion.

Mass is a measure of an object's inertia.

A larger mass indicates greater resistance to acceleration.

Examples:

- A heavy truck is harder to accelerate than a small bicycle.
- A passenger tends to move forward when a moving bus suddenly stops.
- A stationary object remains stationary unless an external resultant force acts on it.

### Equilibrium

When the net external force on an object is zero:

\[
\sum\vec{F}=0
\]

The object has zero acceleration.

It may be at rest or moving with constant velocity.

This is called translational equilibrium.

## 3. Newton's Second Law of Motion

Newton's second law relates the resultant force acting on an object to the rate of change of its momentum.

\[
\vec{F}_{net}=\frac{d\vec{p}}{dt}
\]

Momentum is:

\[
\vec{p}=m\vec{v}
\]

For constant mass:

\[
\vec{F}_{net}=m\frac{d\vec{v}}{dt}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\vec{F}_{net}=m\vec{a}}
\]

This is the familiar form of Newton's second law.

### Meaning

Acceleration is:

- Directly proportional to net force.
- Inversely proportional to mass.
- Directed along the net force.

### Example

A net force of 20 N acts on a body of mass 5 kg.

\[
F=ma
\]

\[
20=5a
\]

\[
a=4\ \text{m/s}^2
\]

## 4. Newton's Third Law of Motion

Newton's third law states:

For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

If object A exerts a force on object B, object B simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force on object A.

\[
\vec{F}_{AB}=-\vec{F}_{BA}
\]

### Important properties

- Action and reaction are equal in magnitude.
- They act in opposite directions.
- They act on different objects.
- They occur simultaneously.
- They are of the same physical interaction.

### Examples

**Walking:**

The foot pushes the ground backward. The ground exerts a forward frictional force on the foot.

**Swimming:**

A swimmer pushes water backward. The water pushes the swimmer forward.

**Rocket propulsion:**

A rocket expels gases backward. The gases exert an opposite force on the rocket.

### Common misconception

Action and reaction do not cancel each other because they act on different objects.

Forces acting on the same object must be considered when calculating its net force.

## 5. Momentum

Linear momentum is the product of mass and velocity.

\[
\vec{p}=m\vec{v}
\]

SI unit:

\[
\text{kg m/s}
\]

Momentum is a vector quantity.

Its direction is the same as the velocity of the object.

A large mass or high velocity produces large momentum.

Momentum is important in collisions, recoil and conservation laws.

## 6. Impulse and Change in Momentum

Impulse is the product of force and the time interval over which it acts.

For a constant force:

\[
\vec{J}=\vec{F}\Delta t
\]

Impulse is equal to the change in momentum:

\[
\vec{J}=\Delta\vec{p}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\vec{F}\Delta t=m\vec{v}-m\vec{u}
\]

This is the impulse-momentum theorem.

### Example

A 2 kg object changes its velocity from 3 m/s to 8 m/s.

Change in momentum:

\[
\Delta p=2(8-3)
\]

\[
\Delta p=10\ \text{kg m/s}
\]

If the change occurs in 0.5 s:

\[
F_{avg}=\frac{10}{0.5}
\]

\[
F_{avg}=20\ \text{N}
\]

### Applications of impulse

Airbags and padded surfaces increase the time taken for momentum to change.

Since:

\[
F_{avg}=\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}
\]

Increasing the stopping time reduces the average force for the same change in momentum.

## 7. Types of Forces in Mechanics

Several forces commonly appear in Newton's laws problems.

### Weight

Weight is the gravitational force acting on an object.

Near Earth's surface:

\[
W=mg
\]

It acts vertically downward.

### Normal reaction

The normal reaction is the contact force exerted by a surface perpendicular to the contact surface.

It is represented by N or R.

It is not always equal to mg.

Its value depends on the geometry and other forces acting on the object.

### Tension

Tension is the pulling force transmitted through a stretched string, rope or cable.

In an ideal massless string passing over a frictionless pulley, the tension is the same throughout the string.

### Friction

Friction is a contact force that opposes relative motion or the tendency of relative motion between surfaces.

Its detailed laws will be studied in the next lesson.

### Spring force

For an ideal spring obeying Hooke's law:

\[
F=-kx
\]

Where:

- k is the spring constant.
- x is displacement from equilibrium.

The negative sign indicates that the force acts opposite to displacement.

## 8. Free-Body Diagrams

A free-body diagram is a simplified drawing that shows all external forces acting on a selected object.

It is one of the most important problem-solving tools in mechanics.

### Steps for drawing a free-body diagram

1. Select the object or system being analysed.
2. Isolate it from its surroundings.
3. Represent the object using a simple shape or point.
4. Draw every external force acting on it.
5. Label each force.
6. Choose suitable coordinate axes.
7. Resolve forces into components if necessary.

### Common forces

- Weight: downward.
- Normal reaction: perpendicular to a contact surface.
- Tension: along the string, pulling away from the object.
- Friction: along the contact surface.
- Applied force: in the direction of the applied interaction.
- Spring force: opposite to the displacement from equilibrium.

Do not draw forces that the object exerts on other objects in its own free-body diagram.

## 9. Applying Newton's Second Law Along Coordinate Axes

For two-dimensional problems, Newton's second law can be resolved into perpendicular components.

Along x-axis:

\[
\sum F_x=ma_x
\]

Along y-axis:

\[
\sum F_y=ma_y
\]

This allows horizontal and vertical equations to be solved independently.

### Example: A block on a horizontal surface

A horizontal force of 30 N acts on a 5 kg block. Ignore friction.

Horizontal equation:

\[
\sum F_x=ma_x
\]

\[
30=5a_x
\]

\[
a_x=6\ \text{m/s}^2
\]

Vertical acceleration is zero.

Therefore:

\[
N-mg=0
\]

\[
N=mg
\]

If g = 10 m/s²:

\[
N=50\ \text{N}
\]

## 10. Normal Reaction on an Inclined Plane

Consider a block resting on an inclined plane making an angle θ with the horizontal.

The weight acts vertically downward.

Resolve the weight into components:

Parallel to the plane:

\[
mg\sin\theta
\]

Perpendicular to the plane:

\[
mg\cos\theta
\]

If no other force acts perpendicular to the plane and there is no perpendicular acceleration:

\[
N=mg\cos\theta
\]

The component parallel to the plane tends to move the block downward.

If friction is absent:

\[
a=g\sin\theta
\]

This result will be developed further in the study of friction and inclined-plane dynamics.

## 11. Connected Bodies and Tension

Connected-body problems involve two or more objects linked by a string.

The objects may have different masses but share the same acceleration magnitude when connected by an ideal inextensible string over a suitable pulley.

Consider two masses connected over an ideal pulley.

Let:

\[
m_2>m_1
\]

The heavier mass moves downward and the lighter mass moves upward.

For mass m1:

\[
T-m_1g=m_1a
\]

For mass m2:

\[
m_2g-T=m_2a
\]

Adding the equations:

\[
(m_2-m_1)g=(m_1+m_2)a
\]

Therefore:

\[
a=\frac{(m_2-m_1)g}{m_1+m_2}
\]

Substituting into either equation gives the tension.

This is an idealised Atwood machine.

## 12. Apparent Weight and Elevators

An object placed on a weighing scale experiences a normal reaction.

The scale reading is equal to the normal reaction, not necessarily the gravitational force.

For an elevator accelerating upward:

\[
N-mg=ma
\]

Therefore:

\[
N=m(g+a)
\]

For an elevator accelerating downward:

\[
mg-N=ma
\]

Therefore:

\[
N=m(g-a)
\]

If the elevator moves at constant velocity:

\[
N=mg
\]

If it is in free fall:

\[
a=g
\]

\[
N=0
\]

The object experiences apparent weightlessness.

## 13. Solved Numerical Problems

### Example 1: Force and acceleration

A 10 kg object is acted upon by a resultant force of 50 N.

\[
F=ma
\]

\[
50=10a
\]

\[
a=5\ \text{m/s}^2
\]

### Example 2: Impulse

A force of 40 N acts on an object for 0.2 seconds.

\[
J=F\Delta t
\]

\[
J=40(0.2)
\]

\[
J=8\ \text{N s}
\]

### Example 3: Elevator apparent weight

A person has mass 60 kg. An elevator accelerates upward at 2 m/s². Take g = 10 m/s².

\[
N=m(g+a)
\]

\[
N=60(10+2)
\]

\[
N=720\ \text{N}
\]

The scale reads 720 N.

## 14. Conditions of Equilibrium

An object is in translational equilibrium when:

\[
\sum\vec{F}=0
\]

In two dimensions:

\[
\sum F_x=0
\]

\[
\sum F_y=0
\]

An object may be stationary or moving with constant velocity.

Rotational equilibrium additionally requires zero net torque.

Rotational equilibrium will be studied in detail in the mechanics phase.

## Common Exam Traps

- Newton's first law applies when the resultant external force is zero.
- Mass measures inertia; weight is gravitational force.
- Newton's second law uses the net force, not an individual force.
- Newton's third-law forces act on different objects.
- Action and reaction do not cancel in the free-body diagram of one object.
- Impulse equals change in momentum.
- Normal reaction is not always equal to weight.
- Tension acts along a string and pulls away from the object.
- Apparent weight is the normal reaction, not always mg.
- A stationary object may still have several forces acting on it, provided they balance.

## Quiz

1. Which statement correctly describes Newton's third law?

- [ ] Action and reaction act on the same object.
- [x] Action and reaction are equal and opposite forces acting on different objects.
- [ ] Reaction always occurs after action.
- [ ] Action is always greater than reaction.

> Newton's third law states that interacting objects exert equal and opposite forces on each other simultaneously.

2. A 4 kg object experiences a net force of 20 N. What is its acceleration?

- [ ] 4 m/s²
- [x] 5 m/s²
- [ ] 8 m/s²
- [ ] 16 m/s²

> Using F = ma, acceleration = 20/4 = 5 m/s².

3. A 50 kg person is in an elevator accelerating upward at 2 m/s². Take g = 10 m/s². What is the apparent weight?

- [ ] 400 N
- [ ] 500 N
- [x] 600 N
- [ ] 700 N

> The apparent weight is N = m(g + a) = 50(10 + 2) = 600 N.
