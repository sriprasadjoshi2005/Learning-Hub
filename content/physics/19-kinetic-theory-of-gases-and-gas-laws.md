---
title: Kinetic Theory of Gases and Gas Laws
level: intermediate
---

Kinetic theory explains the macroscopic properties of gases using the motion of their microscopic particles.

It connects measurable quantities such as pressure, temperature, and volume to the motion and collisions of gas molecules.

The ideal gas model is a useful approximation for many gases, particularly at relatively low pressures and high temperatures.

## 1. Molecular Nature of Gases

A gas consists of a very large number of rapidly moving particles.

These particles:
- Move continuously and randomly.
- Travel in approximately straight lines between collisions.
- Collide with one another and with container walls.
- Exert pressure through collisions with the walls.
- Possess kinetic energy associated with their motion.

The macroscopic behaviour of a gas results from the collective behaviour of its enormous number of particles.

## 2. Assumptions of the Ideal Gas Model

The ideal gas model assumes:

1. Gas molecules are treated as point particles with negligible individual volume compared with the container.
2. Molecules move randomly.
3. Collisions between molecules and container walls are perfectly elastic.
4. Intermolecular forces are negligible except during collisions.
5. The duration of a collision is negligible compared with the time between collisions.
6. Molecular motion obeys classical mechanics.

Real gases deviate from these assumptions under certain conditions.

## 3. Pressure from Molecular Collisions

Gas pressure arises from molecules colliding with the walls of their container.

Each collision changes molecular momentum and produces an impulse on the wall.

The combined effect of enormous numbers of collisions produces measurable pressure.

From kinetic theory:

\[
P=\frac{1}{3}\rho c_{\text{rms}}^2
\]

Where:
- P = gas pressure
- rho = gas density
- c_rms = root-mean-square molecular speed

An equivalent form is:

\[
PV=\frac{1}{3}Nm\overline{c^2}
\]

Where N is the number of molecules and m is the mass of one molecule.

## 4. Temperature and Molecular Kinetic Energy

The average translational kinetic energy of a molecule in an ideal monatomic gas is:

\[
\overline{KE}=\frac{3}{2}k_BT
\]

Where:
- k_B = Boltzmann constant
- T = absolute temperature

\[
k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\,J/K
\]

This shows that absolute temperature is directly proportional to average translational kinetic energy.

At the same temperature, different ideal gases have the same average translational kinetic energy per molecule.

## 5. Root-Mean-Square Speed

The root-mean-square speed is defined as:

\[
c_{\text{rms}}=\sqrt{\overline{c^2}}
\]

For an ideal gas:

\[
c_{\text{rms}}=\sqrt{\frac{3k_BT}{m}}
\]

Using molar mass M:

\[
c_{\text{rms}}=\sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}
\]

Where:
- R = universal gas constant
- M = molar mass in kg/mol

At a fixed temperature, lighter gas molecules generally have higher RMS speeds.

## 6. Boyle's Law

For a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature:

\[
P\propto\frac{1}{V}
\]

Therefore:

\[
P_1V_1=P_2V_2
\]

If volume decreases, pressure increases, provided temperature and amount of gas remain constant.

## 7. Charles's Law

For a fixed amount of gas at constant pressure:

\[
V\propto T
\]

Therefore:

\[
\frac{V_1}{T_1}=\frac{V_2}{T_2}
\]

Temperature must be expressed in kelvin.

## 8. Pressure Law

For a fixed amount of gas at constant volume:

\[
P\propto T
\]

Therefore:

\[
\frac{P_1}{T_1}=\frac{P_2}{T_2}
\]

Again, temperature must be in kelvin.

## 9. Avogadro's Law

At constant temperature and pressure, the volume of an ideal gas is proportional to the amount of gas.

\[
V\propto n
\]

Where n is the number of moles.

Equal volumes of ideal gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.

## 10. Ideal Gas Equation

Combining the gas laws gives:

\[
PV=nRT
\]

Where:
- P = absolute pressure
- V = volume
- n = number of moles
- R = universal gas constant
- T = absolute temperature

\[
R=8.314\,J/(mol\,K)
\]

Another form is:

\[
PV=Nk_BT
\]

Where N is the number of molecules.

The ideal gas equation connects the macroscopic state of a gas to its molecular properties.

## 11. Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures

For a mixture of non-reacting ideal gases:

\[
P_{\text{total}}=P_1+P_2+\cdots+P_n
\]

Each gas contributes a partial pressure equal to the pressure it would exert if it occupied the container alone at the same temperature.

## 12. Degrees of Freedom

Degrees of freedom describe the independent ways in which a molecule can store energy.

A monatomic gas has three translational degrees of freedom.

A simple diatomic gas at ordinary temperatures can have:
- Three translational degrees of freedom.
- Two rotational degrees of freedom.

Vibrational modes may become important at higher temperatures.

The equipartition theorem assigns an average energy of:

\[
\frac{1}{2}k_BT
\]

per quadratic degree of freedom in thermal equilibrium under classical conditions.

## 13. Internal Energy of an Ideal Gas

For an ideal monatomic gas:

\[
U=\frac{3}{2}nRT
\]

More generally:

\[
U=\frac{f}{2}nRT
\]

Where f is the number of active quadratic degrees of freedom under the classical equipartition model.

For an ideal gas, internal energy depends only on temperature.

## 14. Real Gases

Real gas molecules have finite size and interact with one another.

The ideal gas approximation becomes less accurate at:
- High pressure.
- Low temperature.
- Conditions close to condensation.

The van der Waals equation improves the ideal gas model:

\[
\left(P+\frac{an^2}{V^2}\right)(V-nb)=nRT
\]

Where:
- a accounts approximately for intermolecular attractions.
- b accounts approximately for molecular volume.

## 15. Worked Numerical Examples

**Example 1: Boyle's law**

A gas occupies 4 L at 100 kPa. Its volume is reduced to 2 L at constant temperature. Find its final pressure.

\[
P_1V_1=P_2V_2
\]

\[
100\times4=P_2\times2
\]

\[
\boxed{P_2=200\,kPa}
\]

**Example 2: Charles's law**

A gas occupies 2 L at 300 K. Find its volume at 450 K under constant pressure.

\[
\frac{V_1}{T_1}=\frac{V_2}{T_2}
\]

\[
V_2=\frac{2\times450}{300}
\]

\[
\boxed{V_2=3\,L}
\]

**Example 3: Ideal gas equation**

Calculate the volume occupied by 2 moles of ideal gas at 300 K and 100000 Pa.

\[
PV=nRT
\]

\[
V=\frac{nRT}{P}
\]

\[
V=\frac{2\times8.314\times300}{100000}
\]

\[
\boxed{V\approx0.0499\,m^3}
\]

## 16. Applications

- Understanding atmospheric pressure.
- Predicting gas behaviour in engines.
- Designing gas storage systems.
- Explaining molecular diffusion.
- Understanding refrigeration and compression.
- Studying stellar and planetary atmospheres.

## Common Exam Traps

- Gas law calculations require absolute temperature in kelvin.
- Boyle's law requires constant temperature and fixed amount of gas.
- Charles's law requires constant pressure and fixed amount of gas.
- Pressure in the ideal gas equation is absolute pressure, not gauge pressure.
- Average kinetic energy is proportional to absolute temperature.
- RMS speed is not the same as average speed.
- Real gases do not behave ideally under all conditions.

## Quiz

1. The average translational kinetic energy of an ideal gas molecule is proportional to:

- [ ] Celsius temperature
- [x] Absolute temperature
- [ ] Gas volume only
- [ ] Molecular mass only

> Average translational kinetic energy is (3/2)kBT, so it is proportional to absolute temperature.

2. A gas has a volume of 6 L at 200 kPa. If its volume becomes 3 L at constant temperature, its final pressure is:

- [ ] 100 kPa
- [ ] 200 kPa
- [x] 400 kPa
- [ ] 600 kPa

> Boyle's law gives P2 = (200 × 6)/3 = 400 kPa.

3. Which condition causes the greatest deviation from ideal gas behaviour?

- [ ] Low pressure and high temperature
- [ ] Very low density
- [x] High pressure and low temperature
- [ ] Large separation between molecules

> At high pressure and low temperature, molecular volume and intermolecular forces become increasingly significant.
