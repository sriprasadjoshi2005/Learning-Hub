Nuclear physics studies the structure, properties and behaviour of atomic nuclei. It explains radioactivity, nuclear stability, radioactive decay, nuclear radiation and many processes responsible for energy production in stars.

Unlike atomic physics, which primarily studies electrons and their interactions with nuclei, nuclear physics focuses on the nucleus itself.

## 1. Structure of the Atomic Nucleus

An atomic nucleus consists of two types of particles:

- **Protons:** Positively charged particles.
- **Neutrons:** Electrically neutral particles.

Together, protons and neutrons are called nucleons.

The atomic number is the number of protons:

\[
Z=\text{Number of protons}
\]

The mass number is the total number of nucleons:

\[
A=Z+N
\]

Therefore:

\[
N=A-Z
\]

Where N is the number of neutrons.

A nucleus is represented as:

\[
{}^A_ZX
\]

For example:

\[
{}^{23}_{11}\mathrm{Na}
\]

This sodium nucleus contains 11 protons and 12 neutrons.

## 2. Isotopes, Isobars and Isotones

These terms describe relationships between different nuclei.

**Isotopes:**

Atoms with the same atomic number but different mass numbers.

Example:

\[
{}^{1}_{1}H,\quad {}^{2}_{1}H,\quad {}^{3}_{1}H
\]

They have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

**Isobars:**

Nuclei with the same mass number but different atomic numbers.

Example:

\[
{}^{40}_{18}Ar,\quad {}^{40}_{20}Ca
\]

**Isotones:**

Nuclei having the same number of neutrons but different numbers of protons.

Example:

\[
{}^{14}_{6}C,\quad {}^{15}_{7}N
\]

Both contain eight neutrons.

## 3. Nuclear Size and Density

The approximate nuclear radius is:

\[
R=R_0A^{1/3}
\]

Where:

\[
R_0\approx1.2\times10^{-15}\text{ m}
\]

The radius increases with the cube root of the mass number.

Since nuclear volume is proportional to \(R^3\):

\[
V\propto A
\]

Nuclear mass is also approximately proportional to A.

Therefore, nuclear density is approximately constant for most nuclei.

This explains why adding nucleons increases nuclear size without substantially changing its average density.

## 4. Nuclear Forces

The nucleus contains positively charged protons that electrically repel one another.

However, nuclei remain bound because of the strong nuclear interaction.

Important properties of nuclear forces include:

- They are extremely strong at short distances.
- They are attractive over a suitable range of nuclear distances.
- They become weak at larger separations.
- They exhibit saturation: a nucleon interacts strongly with nearby nucleons rather than equally with every nucleon.
- They are approximately charge-independent under comparable conditions.

The strong nuclear interaction is responsible for binding nucleons together.

## 5. Mass Defect

The mass of a stable nucleus is generally smaller than the sum of the masses of its separate nucleons.

The difference is called the mass defect.

\[
\Delta m=Zm_p+Nm_n-m_{\text{nucleus}}
\]

Where:
- \(m_p\) = proton mass
- \(m_n\) = neutron mass
- \(m_{\text{nucleus}}\) = actual nuclear mass

The missing mass corresponds to the energy released when the nucleus forms.

## 6. Nuclear Binding Energy

Einstein's mass-energy equivalence gives:

\[
E=mc^2
\]

Therefore, nuclear binding energy is:

\[
B=\Delta mc^2
\]

When mass defect is measured in atomic mass units:

\[
B(\text{MeV})=\Delta m(\text{u})\times931.5
\]

Binding energy is the energy required to separate a nucleus completely into its individual nucleons.

## 7. Binding Energy Per Nucleon

Binding energy per nucleon is:

\[
\frac{B}{A}
\]

It provides an important indication of nuclear stability.

A larger binding energy per nucleon generally indicates a more tightly bound nucleus.

The binding energy per nucleon curve:
- Rises rapidly for light nuclei.
- Reaches a broad maximum around iron and nickel.
- Decreases gradually for very heavy nuclei.

This explains why both nuclear fusion of light nuclei and nuclear fission of heavy nuclei can release energy.

## 8. Radioactivity

Radioactivity is the spontaneous transformation of unstable atomic nuclei into more stable configurations.

Radioactive decay is a nuclear process and is generally unaffected by ordinary changes in temperature, pressure or chemical state.

Three important types of radiation are:

### Alpha Radiation

An alpha particle is a helium nucleus.

\[
{}^4_2He
\]

Alpha decay:

\[
{}^A_ZX\rightarrow{}^{A-4}_{Z-2}Y+{}^4_2He
\]

Effects:
- Mass number decreases by four.
- Atomic number decreases by two.
- High ionising ability.
- Low penetrating ability.

### Beta Radiation

Beta-minus decay occurs when a neutron changes into a proton.

\[
n\rightarrow p+e^-+\bar{\nu}_e
\]

The emitted electron is called a beta-minus particle.

Nuclear representation:

\[
{}^A_ZX\rightarrow{}^A_{Z+1}Y+e^-+\bar{\nu}_e
\]

Beta-plus decay involves a proton transforming into a neutron:

\[
p\rightarrow n+e^++\nu_e
\]

The emitted positron has the same mass as an electron but opposite electric charge.

### Gamma Radiation

Gamma radiation consists of high-energy electromagnetic photons emitted by an excited nucleus.

\[
{}^A_ZX^*\rightarrow{}^A_ZX+\gamma
\]

Gamma emission does not change the atomic number or mass number.

It generally has high penetrating ability and relatively low ionising ability compared with alpha particles.

## 9. Radioactive Decay Law

Radioactive decay is statistical.

The rate of decay is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present.

\[
-\frac{dN}{dt}=\lambda N
\]

Where:
- N = number of undecayed nuclei.
- lambda = decay constant.
- t = time.

Rearranging:

\[
\frac{dN}{N}=-\lambda dt
\]

Integrating:

\[
\ln N=-\lambda t+C
\]

Using the initial condition N = N0 at t = 0:

\[
N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}
\]

This is the radioactive decay law.

## 10. Half-Life and Mean Life

Half-life is the time required for half the original radioactive nuclei to decay.

\[
T_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{\lambda}
\]

Since ln 2 is approximately 0.693:

\[
T_{1/2}=\frac{0.693}{\lambda}
\]

Mean life is:

\[
\tau=\frac{1}{\lambda}
\]

Therefore:

\[
T_{1/2}=0.693\tau
\]

After n half-lives:

\[
N=\frac{N_0}{2^n}
\]

## 11. Activity of a Radioactive Sample

Activity is the number of nuclear disintegrations per second.

\[
A=-\frac{dN}{dt}
\]

Therefore:

\[
A=\lambda N
\]

SI unit: becquerel (Bq).

One becquerel means one nuclear disintegration per second.

Activity also decreases exponentially:

\[
A=A_0e^{-\lambda t}
\]

## 12. Worked Numerical Example

A radioactive sample initially contains 8000 unstable nuclei. Its half-life is 5 days.

Find the number of nuclei remaining after 15 days.

Number of half-lives:

\[
n=\frac{15}{5}=3
\]

Remaining nuclei:

\[
N=\frac{8000}{2^3}
\]

\[
N=\frac{8000}{8}
\]

\[
\boxed{N=1000}
\]

Therefore, 1000 nuclei remain undecayed.

## 13. Carbon Dating

Carbon-14 is a radioactive isotope used to estimate the age of previously living materials.

Living organisms continuously exchange carbon with their environment. After death, this exchange stops and carbon-14 gradually decays.

By measuring the remaining carbon-14 relative to stable carbon isotopes, scientists can estimate the time since the organism died.

Its half-life is approximately 5730 years.

The method is useful for archaeological and geological investigations within suitable age ranges.

## 14. Applications of Radioactivity

- Medical diagnosis and nuclear imaging.
- Cancer radiotherapy.
- Sterilisation of medical equipment.
- Industrial thickness measurement.
- Tracing chemical and biological processes.
- Archaeological dating.
- Nuclear research.

Radioactive materials require appropriate shielding, monitoring and controlled handling because ionising radiation can damage living tissue.

## Common Exam Traps

- Isotopes have the same atomic number, not the same mass number.
- Mass defect is converted into binding energy using E = mc².
- Alpha decay decreases A by four and Z by two.
- Beta-minus decay increases Z by one without changing A.
- Gamma emission changes neither A nor Z.
- Half-life does not depend on the initial quantity of a pure radioactive isotope.
- Activity is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei.

## Quiz

1. A nucleus has mass number 35 and atomic number 17. How many neutrons does it contain?

- [ ] 17
- [ ] 35
- [x] 18
- [ ] 52

> Number of neutrons equals mass number minus atomic number. Therefore, N = 35 - 17 = 18.

2. During alpha decay, the atomic number of the parent nucleus:

- [ ] Increases by two
- [ ] Decreases by one
- [x] Decreases by two
- [ ] Remains unchanged

> An alpha particle contains two protons and two neutrons. Its emission decreases the parent nucleus's atomic number by two.

3. A radioactive isotope has a half-life of 10 hours. What fraction remains after 30 hours?

- [ ] One-half
- [ ] One-fourth
- [x] One-eighth
- [ ] One-sixteenth

> Thirty hours correspond to three half-lives. The remaining fraction is (1/2)^3 = 1/8.
