Geometrical optics explains many properties of light, but it cannot fully explain phenomena such as interference, diffraction and polarisation.

Wave optics describes light using its wave nature and provides a deeper understanding of how light propagates and interacts with obstacles and openings.

## 1. Wave Nature of Light

Light exhibits both wave-like and particle-like behaviour.

Wave optics explains:
- Interference.
- Diffraction.
- Polarisation.
- Reflection and refraction through wavefronts.

The wave description is especially useful when the dimensions of openings and obstacles are comparable to the wavelength of light.

## 2. Wavefront

A wavefront is an imaginary surface joining all points of a wave that are in the same phase.

In other words, every point on a wavefront has undergone the same number of oscillations.

The direction of propagation of a wave is perpendicular to its wavefront in an isotropic medium.

## 3. Types of Wavefronts

### Spherical Wavefront

A point source produces spherical wavefronts in a uniform medium.

The wavefront expands outward from the source.

### Cylindrical Wavefront

A long line source produces approximately cylindrical wavefronts.

### Plane Wavefront

At a sufficiently large distance from a source, a small portion of a spherical wavefront can be approximated as plane.

Plane wavefronts are also produced by collimated optical systems.

## 4. Huygens' Principle

Huygens' principle states that every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets.

The secondary wavelets spread forward through the medium.

The new wavefront is the forward envelope of these secondary wavelets.

This principle provides a geometrical method for understanding wave propagation.

## 5. Propagation of a Plane Wavefront

Consider a plane wavefront travelling through a uniform medium.

Every point on the original wavefront generates secondary wavelets.

After a time interval t, each wavelet has a radius:

r = vt

Where v is the wave speed.

The common forward tangent to these wavelets forms the new wavefront.

Because the medium is uniform, the new wavefront remains parallel to the original plane wavefront.

## 6. Huygens' Explanation of Reflection

Consider a plane wavefront incident on a reflecting surface.

Different points of the wavefront reach the surface at different times.

Each point on the surface produces a secondary reflected wavelet.

The common tangent to these wavelets forms the reflected wavefront.

The geometry of this construction gives:

Angle of incidence = Angle of reflection

Therefore, Huygens' principle explains the law of reflection.

## 7. Huygens' Explanation of Refraction

When light travels from one medium into another, its speed changes.

Consider a plane wavefront entering a medium with a different wave speed.

The portion entering the second medium first begins travelling at the new speed while the remaining portion continues in the original medium.

The resulting wavefront changes orientation.

The geometry leads to:

sin i / sin r = v1/v2

Since:

n1/n2 = v2/v1

We obtain:

n1 sin i = n2 sin r

This is Snell's law.

Thus, refraction can be understood using wavefront propagation.

## 8. Principle of Superposition

The principle of superposition states that when two or more waves overlap, the resultant displacement is the algebraic sum of their individual displacements at each point.

y = y1 + y2 + ...

This principle is responsible for interference.

For light waves, the electric field amplitudes combine.

The resulting intensity depends on the phase relationship between the waves.

## 9. Coherent Sources

Two sources are coherent when they produce waves of the same frequency and maintain a constant phase difference.

Coherence is essential for obtaining a stable interference pattern.

Two independent ordinary light sources generally do not produce a stable interference pattern because their phase difference changes rapidly.

Coherent light can be produced by splitting light from a single source.

## 10. Path Difference and Phase Difference

Path difference is the difference in the distances travelled by two waves before reaching a point.

For a path difference Δx:

Phase difference:

φ = 2πΔx/λ

Where λ is the wavelength.

A path difference of one wavelength corresponds to a phase difference of 2π radians.

## 11. Constructive and Destructive Interference

### Constructive Interference

Constructive interference occurs when waves arrive in phase.

Condition:

Δx = nλ

Where n is an integer.

The resultant amplitude is maximum when the individual amplitudes are equal and the waves overlap perfectly.

### Destructive Interference

Destructive interference occurs when waves arrive in opposite phase.

Condition:

Δx = (n + 1/2)λ

The resultant amplitude is minimum.

For equal amplitudes, complete destructive interference can occur.

## 12. Intensity and Amplitude

For a harmonic light wave, intensity is proportional to the square of its amplitude.

I ∝ A²

If the resultant amplitude doubles, the intensity becomes four times as large.

For two coherent waves with intensities I1 and I2 and phase difference φ:

I = I1 + I2 + 2sqrt(I1I2)cos φ

For equal intensities I0:

I = 4I0 cos²(φ/2)

This equation describes how intensity varies with phase difference.

## 13. Conditions for Sustained Interference

A stable interference pattern requires:
- Coherent sources.
- The same frequency.
- A constant phase difference.
- Similar polarisation states.
- Suitable intensity balance for clearly visible fringes.

For many practical demonstrations, the light source is divided into two coherent beams.

## 14. Young's Double-Slit Experiment

Young's double-slit experiment demonstrates the wave nature of light.

A coherent light source illuminates two narrow slits.

The slits act as coherent sources.

Their waves overlap on a screen and produce alternating bright and dark fringes.

For a screen at distance D and slit separation d:

Path difference:

Δx ≈ dy/D

Where y is the distance from the central line on the screen.

### Bright Fringes

dy/D = nλ

Therefore:

yn = nλD/d

### Dark Fringes

dy/D = (n + 1/2)λ

The fringe width is:

β = λD/d

Where:
- β = distance between consecutive bright fringes
- λ = wavelength
- D = screen distance
- d = slit separation

## 15. Importance of Young's Experiment

Young's experiment established strong experimental evidence for the wave nature of light.

It demonstrates that:
- Light can interfere.
- Bright and dark fringes arise from superposition.
- Fringe spacing depends on wavelength.
- Fringe spacing increases with screen distance.
- Fringe spacing decreases with slit separation.

## 16. Numerical Applications

**Example 1: Fringe width**

In a double-slit experiment:
- Wavelength = 600 nm.
- Screen distance = 2 m.
- Slit separation = 0.5 mm.

β = λD/d

β = (600 × 10^-9 × 2)/(0.5 × 10^-3)

β = 2.4 × 10^-3 m

β = 2.4 mm

**Example 2: Phase difference**

Two waves have a path difference of λ/4.

φ = 2πΔx/λ

φ = 2π(λ/4)/λ

φ = π/2 radians

## Common Exam Traps

- A wavefront joins points having the same phase.
- Wave propagation is perpendicular to the wavefront in an isotropic medium.
- Huygens' principle uses secondary wavelets.
- Coherent sources have the same frequency and a constant phase difference.
- Constructive interference occurs for path difference nλ.
- Destructive interference occurs for path difference (n + 1/2)λ.
- Light intensity is proportional to the square of amplitude.
- Young's fringe width increases with wavelength.
- Fringe width increases with screen distance.
- Fringe width decreases when slit separation increases.

## Quiz

1. A wavefront is a surface joining points that have:

- [ ] Different frequencies
- [x] The same phase
- [ ] Different amplitudes only
- [ ] Zero displacement at all times

> A wavefront is defined as a surface of constant phase.

2. For constructive interference, the path difference between two coherent waves must be:

- [ ] (n + 1/2)λ
- [x] nλ
- [ ] λ/4 for every bright fringe
- [ ] An arbitrary value

> Constructive interference occurs when the phase difference is an integer multiple of 2π, corresponding to a path difference of nλ.

3. In Young's double-slit experiment, if the slit separation is doubled while all other quantities remain constant, the fringe width:

- [x] Becomes half
- [ ] Doubles
- [ ] Becomes four times
- [ ] Remains unchanged

> Fringe width is β = λD/d. Doubling d reduces β to half its original value.
