Geometrical optics, also called ray optics, studies the propagation of light using straight-line rays.

It explains reflection, refraction, image formation by mirrors and lenses, and the operation of many optical instruments.

## 1. Nature of Light in Geometrical Optics

Geometrical optics treats light as rays travelling along defined paths.

This approximation works well when the dimensions of obstacles and optical components are much larger than the wavelength of light.

In a uniform transparent medium, light travels in straight lines.

This principle is called rectilinear propagation of light.

## 2. Reflection of Light

Reflection occurs when light strikes a surface and returns into the original medium.

The angle of incidence is the angle between the incident ray and the normal.

The angle of reflection is the angle between the reflected ray and the normal.

## 3. Laws of Reflection

There are two laws of reflection:

1. The incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in the same plane.
2. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.

i = r

These laws apply to both plane and curved reflecting surfaces at each point of incidence.

## 4. Regular and Diffuse Reflection

### Regular Reflection

Regular reflection occurs when parallel light rays strike a smooth surface and remain reflected in an organised direction.

Examples:
- Plane mirrors.
- Polished metal.
- Smooth glass surfaces.

### Diffuse Reflection

Diffuse reflection occurs when light strikes an irregular surface and is reflected in different directions.

Examples:
- Paper.
- Walls.
- Rough surfaces.

Diffuse reflection allows objects to be visible from many viewing directions.

The laws of reflection still apply at each microscopic point on the surface.

## 5. Plane Mirrors

A plane mirror produces an image with the following properties:

- Virtual.
- Erect.
- Same size as the object.
- Laterally inverted.
- Located as far behind the mirror as the object is in front.

The image distance equals the object distance in magnitude.

## 6. Spherical Mirrors

A spherical mirror is a mirror whose reflecting surface forms part of a sphere.

There are two types.

### Concave Mirror

The reflecting surface curves inward.

A concave mirror is a converging mirror for paraxial parallel rays.

### Convex Mirror

The reflecting surface curves outward.

A convex mirror is a diverging mirror for paraxial parallel rays.

## 7. Important Terms for Spherical Mirrors

- Pole (P): centre of the mirror surface.
- Centre of curvature (C): centre of the sphere of which the mirror is a part.
- Radius of curvature (R): distance between P and C.
- Principal axis: straight line joining P and C.
- Principal focus (F): point where paraxial rays parallel to the principal axis converge or appear to diverge from.
- Focal length (f): distance between P and F.

For a spherical mirror under the paraxial approximation:

f = R/2

## 8. Mirror Formula

The mirror formula relates object distance, image distance and focal length.

1/f = 1/v + 1/u

Where:
- f = focal length
- v = image distance
- u = object distance

A consistent sign convention must be used.

Under the Cartesian sign convention:
- Distances measured in the direction of incident light are positive.
- Distances measured opposite to incident light are negative.
- Heights above the principal axis are positive.
- Heights below the principal axis are negative.

For an object placed in front of a mirror, u is generally negative.

A concave mirror has negative focal length under this convention, while a convex mirror has positive focal length.

## 9. Magnification of Spherical Mirrors

Linear magnification is:

m = hi/ho

For spherical mirrors:

m = -v/u

Where:
- hi = image height
- ho = object height

Interpretation:

- Positive magnification indicates an erect image.
- Negative magnification indicates an inverted image.
- Magnitude greater than one indicates an enlarged image.
- Magnitude less than one indicates a diminished image.

## 10. Image Formation by Concave Mirrors

The image formed by a concave mirror depends on object position.

| Object position | Image position | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| At infinity | At focus | Real, inverted, highly diminished |
| Beyond C | Between C and F | Real, inverted, diminished |
| At C | At C | Real, inverted, same size |
| Between C and F | Beyond C | Real, inverted, enlarged |
| At F | At infinity | Real, inverted, highly enlarged in the ideal model |
| Between F and P | Behind mirror | Virtual, erect, enlarged |

These results assume an ideal spherical mirror and paraxial rays.

## 11. Image Formation by Convex Mirrors

A convex mirror always forms an image that is:

- Virtual.
- Erect.
- Diminished.
- Located behind the mirror between P and F for a finite object distance.

Convex mirrors provide a wide field of view.

Applications include vehicle side mirrors and security mirrors.

## 12. Refraction of Light

Refraction is the change in direction of light when it travels from one transparent medium into another at an angle.

It occurs because the speed of light changes when it enters a medium with a different refractive index.

If light enters a medium with higher refractive index:
- Its speed decreases.
- Its wavelength decreases.
- Its frequency remains unchanged.

If it enters a medium with lower refractive index:
- Its speed increases.
- Its wavelength increases.
- Its frequency remains unchanged.

## 13. Refractive Index

The absolute refractive index of a medium is:

n = c/v

Where:
- n = refractive index
- c = speed of light in vacuum
- v = speed of light in the medium

The refractive index is dimensionless.

A higher refractive index indicates a lower light speed in that medium.

## 14. Snell's Law

Snell's law describes the relationship between the angles of incidence and refraction.

n1 sin i = n2 sin r

Where:
- n1 = refractive index of the first medium
- n2 = refractive index of the second medium
- i = angle of incidence
- r = angle of refraction

For light travelling from air into glass, the ray generally bends towards the normal.

For light travelling from glass into air, the ray generally bends away from the normal, provided the ray enters the second medium.

## 15. Lateral Displacement Through a Parallel-Sided Slab

When light passes through a rectangular glass slab:
- It bends towards the normal at the first surface.
- It bends away from the normal at the second surface.
- The emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray.
- The emergent ray is laterally displaced.

For a slab of thickness t:

d = t sin(i - r)/cos r

Where:
- d = lateral displacement
- i = angle of incidence
- r = angle of refraction

The displacement increases with slab thickness and depends on the angles of incidence and refraction.

## 16. Total Internal Reflection

Total internal reflection occurs when light travels from an optically denser medium to a less dense medium and the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle.

Two conditions are necessary:

1. Light must travel from higher refractive index to lower refractive index.
2. The angle of incidence must exceed the critical angle.

At the critical angle, the refracted ray travels along the boundary.

For light travelling from medium 1 to medium 2, with n1 greater than n2:

sin C = n2/n1

Where C is the critical angle.

## 17. Applications of Total Internal Reflection

Applications include:

- Optical fibres.
- Medical endoscopes.
- Prisms in binoculars.
- Light pipes.
- Certain reflective optical systems.

Optical fibres guide light through repeated total internal reflection at the core-cladding boundary.

## 18. Apparent Depth

An object inside a transparent medium may appear closer to the surface when viewed from a medium with lower refractive index.

For near-normal viewing:

Refractive index = Real depth / Apparent depth

This relationship is an approximation for paraxial viewing.

## 19. Numerical Applications

**Example 1: Refractive index**

Light travels through a medium at 2 × 10^8 m/s.

n = c/v

n = (3 × 10^8)/(2 × 10^8)

n = 1.5

**Example 2: Critical angle**

Light travels from glass of refractive index 1.5 into air of refractive index 1.

sin C = 1/1.5

sin C = 0.667

C ≈ 41.8 degrees

**Example 3: Mirror formula**

An object is placed 30 cm in front of a concave mirror with focal length 15 cm.

Using the Cartesian convention:

u = -30 cm

f = -15 cm

1/f = 1/v + 1/u

-1/15 = 1/v - 1/30

1/v = -1/30

v = -30 cm

The image forms 30 cm in front of the mirror.

## Common Exam Traps

- Reflection and refraction are different phenomena.
- Angles of incidence and refraction are measured from the normal.
- Frequency remains unchanged during refraction at a stationary boundary.
- Light bends towards the normal when entering a higher refractive-index medium at an angle.
- A convex mirror always produces a virtual, erect and diminished image of a real object.
- A concave mirror can produce real or virtual images.
- Total internal reflection requires travel from higher to lower refractive index.
- The angle of incidence must exceed the critical angle for total internal reflection.
- The focal length of a spherical mirror is half its radius of curvature.
- Sign conventions must be applied consistently in mirror calculations.

## Quiz

1. When light enters a medium with a higher refractive index from a lower refractive-index medium at an angle, it generally:

- [ ] Bends away from the normal
- [x] Bends towards the normal
- [ ] Travels with increased speed
- [ ] Changes its frequency

> Light slows down in the higher-index medium and bends towards the normal. Its frequency remains unchanged.

2. Total internal reflection can occur when light travels:

- [ ] From air to glass at any angle
- [x] From a higher refractive-index medium to a lower refractive-index medium above the critical angle
- [ ] From a lower refractive-index medium to a higher refractive-index medium
- [ ] Through a vacuum only

> Total internal reflection requires travel from an optically denser to a less dense medium, with incidence angle greater than the critical angle.

3. A spherical mirror has radius of curvature 40 cm. Its focal length in magnitude is:

- [ ] 10 cm
- [x] 20 cm
- [ ] 40 cm
- [ ] 80 cm

> For a spherical mirror, the focal length is half the radius of curvature: f = R/2 = 20 cm.
