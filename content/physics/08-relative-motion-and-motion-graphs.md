title: Relative Motion and Motion Graphs
level: intermediate
---

Motion is always described relative to a reference frame. An object may appear stationary to one observer and moving to another.

Relative motion studies how the position, velocity and acceleration of one object are measured with respect to another object or reference frame.

Motion graphs provide another powerful way to analyse movement. By examining slopes, areas and changes in graphical patterns, physical quantities can be determined without relying only on equations.

## 1. Reference Frames

A reference frame is a coordinate system and a means of measuring time used to describe the position and motion of objects.

Examples include:

- An observer standing on the ground.
- A passenger inside a moving train.
- An observer inside an accelerating car.
- A satellite observing Earth.

Motion depends on the chosen reference frame.

For example, a passenger sitting inside a train moving at 60 km/h:

- Is at rest relative to the train.
- Moves at 60 km/h relative to the ground, assuming the train travels in a straight line at constant speed.

This illustrates that rest and motion are relative concepts.

## 2. Position of One Object Relative to Another

Suppose two objects A and B have position vectors:

\[
\vec{r}_A
\]

and:

\[
\vec{r}_B
\]

The position of A relative to B is:

\[
\vec{r}_{AB}=\vec{r}_A-\vec{r}_B
\]

Similarly:

\[
\vec{r}_{BA}=\vec{r}_B-\vec{r}_A
\]

Therefore:

\[
\vec{r}_{AB}=-\vec{r}_{BA}
\]

The relative position indicates where one object is located from the perspective of another.

## 3. Relative Velocity

Relative velocity is the velocity of one object as measured by an observer moving with another object.

The relative velocity of A with respect to B is:

\[
\vec{v}_{AB}=\vec{v}_A-\vec{v}_B
\]

This equation is especially useful when objects move along the same straight line.

### Objects moving in the same direction

Suppose:

\[
v_A=30\ \text{m/s}
\]

\[
v_B=20\ \text{m/s}
\]

Then:

\[
v_{AB}=30-20
\]

\[
v_{AB}=10\ \text{m/s}
\]

A observes B moving away from A at 10 m/s if A is the faster object and both move in the same positive direction.

### Objects moving in opposite directions

Suppose:

\[
v_A=20\ \text{m/s}
\]

\[
v_B=-15\ \text{m/s}
\]

Then:

\[
v_{AB}=20-(-15)
\]

\[
v_{AB}=35\ \text{m/s}
\]

Their relative speed is 35 m/s.

### Important distinction

Relative velocity is a vector. Relative speed is the magnitude of relative velocity.

## 4. Relative Acceleration

Relative acceleration is:

\[
\vec{a}_{AB}=\vec{a}_A-\vec{a}_B
\]

If two objects have equal acceleration, their relative acceleration is zero.

Their relative velocity remains constant, assuming the accelerations remain equal.

This is useful when studying vehicles that accelerate together or objects moving in connected systems.

## 5. River-Boat Problems

River-boat problems involve the combination of a boat's velocity relative to water and the water's velocity relative to the ground.

The fundamental vector relationship is:

\[
\vec{v}_{BG}=\vec{v}_{BW}+\vec{v}_{WG}
\]

Where:

- B = boat.
- W = water.
- G = ground.

### Boat travelling downstream

When the boat travels in the direction of the current:

\[
v_{\text{downstream}}=v_b+v_r
\]

Where:

- \(v_b\) = boat speed relative to water.
- \(v_r\) = river speed.

### Boat travelling upstream

When the boat travels against the current:

\[
v_{\text{upstream}}=v_b-v_r
\]

### Example

A boat travels at 5 m/s relative to water. The river current is 2 m/s.

Downstream speed:

\[
5+2=7\ \text{m/s}
\]

Upstream speed:

\[
5-2=3\ \text{m/s}
\]

These expressions assume the boat is directed parallel to the river current.

## 6. Crossing a River

Suppose a river has width d.

A boat moves perpendicular to the riverbank relative to the water with speed \(v_b\), while the river flows parallel to the bank with speed \(v_r\).

If the boat is directed straight across:

Time to cross:

\[
t=\frac{d}{v_b}
\]

Downstream drift:

\[
x=v_rt
\]

The boat reaches the opposite bank at an angle because its ground velocity contains both perpendicular and downstream components.

### Example

A river is 100 m wide.

A boat travels perpendicular to the river at 4 m/s relative to water. The current speed is 3 m/s.

Crossing time:

\[
t=\frac{100}{4}=25\ \text{s}
\]

Downstream drift:

\[
x=3(25)=75\ \text{m}
\]

The boat reaches the opposite bank 75 m downstream from the point directly opposite its starting position.

## 7. Rain-Man Problems

Rain-man problems involve the relative velocity of rain and a moving observer.

The velocity of rain relative to a person is:

\[
\vec{v}_{RP}=\vec{v}_R-\vec{v}_P
\]

Where:

- \(v_R\) = rain velocity relative to ground.
- \(v_P\) = person's velocity relative to ground.

The apparent direction of rainfall depends on this relative velocity.

For example, when a person runs forward during vertically falling rain, the rain appears to approach from the front.

An umbrella must be tilted in the direction opposite to the apparent incoming rain.

## 8. Motion in Accelerating Reference Frames

A reference frame moving with constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is also inertial.

A frame accelerating relative to an inertial frame is non-inertial.

In a non-inertial frame, fictitious or pseudo forces may be introduced to apply Newton's laws in the usual form.

For a frame accelerating with acceleration \(\vec{a}_f\), the pseudo force on a mass m is:

\[
\vec{F}_{pseudo}=-m\vec{a}_f
\]

The pseudo force acts opposite to the acceleration of the reference frame.

Example:

When a bus accelerates forward, passengers appear to move backward relative to the bus.

This apparent backward tendency is explained by inertia. In the accelerating bus frame, it can be represented using a backward pseudo force.

## 9. Displacement-Time Graphs

A displacement-time graph represents position as a function of time.

The gradient represents velocity.

\[
v=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}
\]

### Common graph patterns

**Horizontal line:**

Position remains constant.

Velocity is zero.

**Straight line with positive gradient:**

Constant positive velocity.

**Straight line with negative gradient:**

Constant negative velocity.

**Curved line:**

Velocity changes with time.

**Increasing positive gradient:**

Positive velocity is increasing.

**Decreasing positive gradient:**

Positive velocity is decreasing.

A graph's gradient at a point represents instantaneous velocity.

## 10. Velocity-Time Graphs

The velocity-time graph provides information about both acceleration and displacement.

Gradient:

\[
a=\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}
\]

Signed area:

\[
\Delta x=\int v(t)\,dt
\]

### Common patterns

**Horizontal line above the time axis:**

Constant positive velocity.

**Horizontal line below the time axis:**

Constant negative velocity.

**Straight line with positive slope:**

Constant positive acceleration.

**Straight line with negative slope:**

Constant negative acceleration.

**Line crossing the time axis:**

Velocity changes sign, indicating a reversal of direction.

### Distance and displacement from a velocity-time graph

Signed area gives displacement.

Total distance is obtained by adding the magnitudes of the areas above and below the time axis.

This distinction is important when an object reverses direction.

## 11. Acceleration-Time Graphs

An acceleration-time graph represents acceleration as a function of time.

The signed area represents change in velocity:

\[
\Delta v=\int a(t)\,dt
\]

For constant acceleration:

\[
\Delta v=at
\]

A positive area increases velocity in the positive direction.

A negative area decreases velocity in the positive direction or increases its negative magnitude, depending on the initial velocity.

## 12. Position-Time Graph and Velocity-Time Relationship

Position, velocity and acceleration are connected through differentiation.

\[
v=\frac{dx}{dt}
\]

\[
a=\frac{dv}{dt}
\]

Therefore:

\[
a=\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}
\]

The reverse relationships use integration:

\[
\Delta x=\int v(t)\,dt
\]

\[
\Delta v=\int a(t)\,dt
\]

These relationships allow motion to be analysed using graphs, calculus and kinematic equations.

## 13. Interpreting Piecewise Motion

Some objects undergo different types of motion during different time intervals.

For example:

- Accelerate for the first 5 seconds.
- Travel at constant velocity for the next 10 seconds.
- Decelerate during the final 5 seconds.

Such motion can be represented using piecewise equations.

Each interval must be analysed separately, while ensuring that position and velocity are consistent at transition points.

For example, if acceleration changes at a particular time, velocity may remain continuous even though acceleration changes suddenly.

## 14. Solved Numerical Problems

### Example 1: Relative velocity

Two cars move in the same direction.

Car A travels at 25 m/s and car B at 15 m/s.

Find the velocity of B relative to A.

\[
v_{BA}=v_B-v_A
\]

\[
v_{BA}=15-25
\]

\[
v_{BA}=-10\ \text{m/s}
\]

The negative sign indicates that B appears to move backward relative to A.

### Example 2: Relative acceleration

Two objects have accelerations of 5 m/s² and 2 m/s² in the same positive direction.

Relative acceleration of A with respect to B:

\[
a_{AB}=5-2
\]

\[
a_{AB}=3\ \text{m/s}^2
\]

### Example 3: Distance from a velocity-time graph

An object travels at 10 m/s for 5 seconds, then at 20 m/s for another 3 seconds, always in the same direction.

First interval:

\[
s_1=10(5)=50\ \text{m}
\]

Second interval:

\[
s_2=20(3)=60\ \text{m}
\]

Total displacement:

\[
s=50+60=110\ \text{m}
\]

Since the object never reverses direction, total distance is also 110 m.

## Common Exam Traps

- Relative velocity must use a consistent sign convention.
- Velocity of A relative to B is not the same as velocity of B relative to A.
- Relative velocity is obtained by vector subtraction.
- River current changes the boat's ground velocity.
- Crossing a river directly does not generally mean the boat reaches the point directly opposite its starting point.
- A horizontal displacement-time graph means zero velocity.
- A horizontal velocity-time graph means zero acceleration, not zero velocity.
- Signed area under a velocity-time graph gives displacement.
- Total distance requires adding the magnitudes of areas when direction changes.
- A negative velocity indicates direction, not necessarily a negative speed.

## Quiz

1. Two vehicles move in the same direction at 30 m/s and 20 m/s. What is the magnitude of their relative velocity?

- [ ] 50 m/s
- [x] 10 m/s
- [ ] 20 m/s
- [ ] 30 m/s

> Relative velocity is the difference between their velocities: 30 - 20 = 10 m/s.

2. A boat travels perpendicular to a riverbank at 4 m/s relative to water. The river is 80 m wide and flows at 3 m/s. What is the downstream drift?

- [ ] 40 m
- [ ] 50 m
- [x] 60 m
- [ ] 80 m

> Crossing time = 80/4 = 20 s. Downstream drift = 3 × 20 = 60 m.

3. What does the signed area under a velocity-time graph represent?

- [ ] Acceleration
- [ ] Total distance in every situation
- [x] Displacement
- [ ] Instantaneous velocity

> The signed area is the integral of velocity with respect to time, giving displacement. Total distance requires summing the magnitudes of the areas.
