title: Optical Instruments and the Human Eye
level: intermediate
---

Optical instruments use mirrors, lenses and other optical components to form images that cannot be observed clearly with the unaided eye.

The human eye is a natural optical instrument, while cameras, microscopes and telescopes are engineered systems based on similar principles.

## 1. The Human Eye

The human eye is a biological optical system that detects visible light and converts it into signals interpreted by the brain.

Its main optical components are:

- Cornea.
- Iris.
- Pupil.
- Eye lens.
- Ciliary muscles.
- Retina.
- Optic nerve.

Light enters through the cornea and pupil, passes through the eye lens and is focused onto the retina.

The retina contains photoreceptor cells that convert light into electrical signals.

## 2. Accommodation of the Eye

Accommodation is the ability of the eye to change the focal length of its lens to focus objects at different distances.

The ciliary muscles control the shape of the eye lens.

### Viewing a Distant Object

- Ciliary muscles relax.
- The lens becomes thinner.
- Focal length increases.
- Optical power decreases.

### Viewing a Nearby Object

- Ciliary muscles contract.
- The lens becomes thicker.
- Focal length decreases.
- Optical power increases.

The eye adjusts its optical power to keep images focused on the retina.

## 3. Near Point and Far Point

The far point is the farthest distance at which the eye can see an object clearly without accommodation.

For a normal eye, the far point is approximately infinity.

The near point is the closest distance at which the eye can focus clearly with maximum accommodation.

For a typical young adult with normal vision, the conventional near point is approximately 25 cm.

These values vary with age and individual visual conditions.

## 4. Defects of Vision

Several conditions affect the ability of the eye to focus images correctly.

### Myopia

Myopia is short-sightedness.

A person with myopia can see nearby objects clearly but has difficulty seeing distant objects.

The image of a distant object forms in front of the retina.

Possible causes include:
- Excessive eye length.
- Excessive optical power of the eye.

Correction:

A concave lens is used to diverge incoming rays so that the eye focuses the image on the retina.

### Hypermetropia

Hypermetropia is long-sightedness.

A person has difficulty seeing nearby objects clearly.

The image of a nearby object tends to form behind the retina.

Possible causes include:
- Shorter-than-normal eyeball.
- Insufficient optical power.

Correction:

A convex lens increases the effective converging power of the optical system.

### Presbyopia

Presbyopia is an age-related reduction in accommodation.

It generally occurs because the eye lens becomes less flexible and the accommodation mechanism becomes less effective.

Correction may involve:
- Convex lenses for near vision.
- Bifocal lenses.
- Progressive lenses.
- Other suitable optical correction.

### Astigmatism

Astigmatism occurs when the eye's optical power varies with direction, commonly because of irregular curvature of the cornea or lens.

It can produce blurred or distorted vision.

Correction generally uses cylindrical or toric lenses.

## 5. Simple Microscope

A simple microscope is a single convex lens used to magnify a small object.

The object is placed within the focal length of the lens.

The lens produces a virtual, erect and enlarged image.

### Magnifying Power

Magnifying power is the ratio of the angle subtended by the image at the eye to the angle subtended by the object when viewed directly at the least distance of distinct vision.

For the final image at infinity:

M = D/f

For the final image at the near point:

M = 1 + D/f

Where:
- D = least distance of distinct vision, conventionally 25 cm
- f = focal length of the lens

## 6. Compound Microscope

A compound microscope uses two convex lenses:

- Objective lens.
- Eyepiece lens.

The objective lens has a short focal length and forms a real, inverted and enlarged intermediate image.

The eyepiece acts as a magnifier and produces the final virtual image.

### Magnifying Power

The total magnifying power is:

M = mo × me

Where:
- mo = magnification produced by the objective.
- me = magnifying power of the eyepiece.

For normal adjustment, an approximate expression is:

|M| ≈ (L/fo)(D/fe)

Where:
- L = tube length
- fo = focal length of objective
- fe = focal length of eyepiece
- D = least distance of distinct vision

This approximation assumes the usual compound microscope geometry and a final image at infinity.

## 7. Astronomical Telescope

An astronomical telescope is used to observe distant celestial objects.

It consists of:

- Objective lens with long focal length.
- Eyepiece with short focal length.

The objective forms a real, inverted and diminished image of a distant object near its focal plane.

The eyepiece magnifies this intermediate image.

## 8. Telescope in Normal Adjustment

In normal adjustment, the final image is formed at infinity.

The intermediate image lies at the focal plane of the eyepiece.

The distance between the lenses is approximately:

L = fo + fe

The magnitude of angular magnification is:

M = fo/fe

The final image is inverted relative to the original object.

A larger objective focal length and smaller eyepiece focal length increase angular magnification.

## 9. Reflecting Telescope

A reflecting telescope uses a curved mirror as its objective rather than a large objective lens.

Advantages include:

- Mirrors do not produce chromatic aberration in the same way as lenses.
- Large mirrors can be manufactured and supported from behind.
- Reflecting systems can collect large amounts of light.
- Many practical astronomical telescopes use reflecting designs.

## 10. Camera

A camera forms an image using a lens system and a light-sensitive detector.

The main components include:
- Lens.
- Aperture.
- Shutter.
- Image sensor or photographic film.
- Focusing mechanism.

The lens forms a real, inverted image on the detector.

The aperture controls the amount of incoming light.

The shutter controls exposure duration.

## 11. Aperture and Depth of Field

The aperture is the opening through which light enters an optical system.

A larger aperture allows more light to enter.

Depth of field describes the range of object distances that appear acceptably sharp in an image.

In general:
- Larger apertures produce shallower depth of field.
- Smaller apertures produce greater depth of field.

Actual depth of field also depends on focal length, focusing distance and the acceptable image blur.

## 12. Resolution and Resolving Power

Resolution is the ability of an optical instrument to distinguish two closely spaced objects as separate.

The resolving power of a telescope improves when:
- The aperture diameter increases.
- The wavelength decreases.

For a circular aperture, the Rayleigh criterion gives the approximate minimum angular separation:

θmin = 1.22λ/D

Where:
- λ = wavelength
- D = aperture diameter

This is an important result in astronomy and optical instrument design.

## 13. Optical Fibre and Endoscopy

An optical fibre guides light through a thin transparent core surrounded by cladding.

The refractive index of the core is higher than that of the cladding.

Light can be guided through repeated total internal reflection.

Applications include:
- High-speed communication.
- Medical endoscopy.
- Industrial inspection.
- Fibre-optic sensors.

## 14. Numerical Applications

**Example 1: Simple microscope**

A convex lens has focal length 5 cm. Find the magnifying power when the final image is at infinity.

M = D/f

M = 25/5

M = 5

**Example 2: Telescope**

An astronomical telescope has objective focal length 100 cm and eyepiece focal length 5 cm.

M = fo/fe

M = 100/5

M = 20

The magnitude of angular magnification is 20.

**Example 3: Myopia correction**

A person has a far point of 2 m. Find the approximate focal length of the correcting lens for distant vision.

The lens must form a virtual image at the person's far point.

For an object at infinity:

u = -infinity

v = -2 m

Using the lens formula:

1/f = 1/v - 1/u

1/f = -1/2 - 0

f = -2 m

The required lens is concave.

## Common Exam Traps

- Myopia is corrected using a concave lens.
- Hypermetropia is corrected using a convex lens.
- Presbyopia is associated with reduced accommodation.
- A simple microscope uses a convex lens.
- A compound microscope uses objective and eyepiece lenses.
- A telescope uses a long-focal-length objective and short-focal-length eyepiece.
- In normal telescope adjustment, the final image is at infinity.
- The objective of a reflecting telescope is a mirror.
- The human eye forms a real, inverted image on the retina.
- Increasing aperture generally improves light collection and angular resolution.

- [ ] A convex lens

- [ ] A plane mirror
- [ ] A cylindrical lens in every case

> Myopia causes distant images to form in front of the retina. A concave lens diverges incoming light and moves the image towards the retina.

- [ ] At the objective lens
- [ ] At the near point
- [x] At infinity
- [ ] At the centre of the objective lens

> Normal adjustment places the intermediate image at the eyepiece focal plane, producing parallel emerging rays and a final image at infinity.
