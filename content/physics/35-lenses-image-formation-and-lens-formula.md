Lenses are transparent optical components that refract light to form images. They are essential in spectacles, cameras, microscopes, telescopes and many scientific instruments.

Understanding lenses requires applying the laws of refraction and studying the relationship between object distance, image distance and focal length.

## 1. Introduction to Lenses

A lens is a transparent refracting medium bounded by two surfaces, at least one of which is curved.

Lenses are classified into two main types.

### Convex Lens

A convex lens is thicker at the centre than at the edges.

It is also called a converging lens because it converges paraxial rays parallel to its principal axis.

### Concave Lens

A concave lens is thinner at the centre than at the edges.

It is also called a diverging lens because it causes paraxial parallel rays to diverge.

## 2. Important Terms

- Optical centre (O): point through which a ray passes approximately undeviated in a thin lens.
- Principal axis: straight line passing through the optical centre and centres of curvature.
- Principal focus (F): point where paraxial rays parallel to the principal axis converge or appear to diverge from.
- Focal length (f): distance between the optical centre and principal focus.
- Aperture: effective diameter of the lens.

A thin lens has two principal foci, one on each side.

## 3. Principal Rays for a Convex Lens

Three standard rays are useful for drawing ray diagrams.

1. A ray parallel to the principal axis refracts through the principal focus on the opposite side.
2. A ray passing through the optical centre travels approximately undeviated.
3. A ray passing through the principal focus on the object side emerges parallel to the principal axis.

The intersection of refracted rays determines the image position.

## 4. Principal Rays for a Concave Lens

For a concave lens:

1. A ray parallel to the principal axis diverges after refraction as if it came from the principal focus on the same side.
2. A ray passing through the optical centre travels approximately undeviated.
3. A ray directed towards the principal focus on the opposite side emerges parallel to the principal axis.

The backward extensions of diverging rays determine the virtual image.

## 5. Lens Formula

The thin-lens formula relates object distance, image distance and focal length.

1/f = 1/v - 1/u

Where:
- f = focal length
- v = image distance
- u = object distance

Under the Cartesian sign convention:
- Distances measured in the direction of incident light are positive.
- Distances measured opposite to incident light are negative.
- Heights above the principal axis are positive.
- Heights below the principal axis are negative.

For a real object placed to the left of a lens:

u is negative.

A convex lens has positive focal length.

A concave lens has negative focal length.

## 6. Magnification of a Lens

Linear magnification is:

m = hi/ho

For a thin lens:

m = v/u

Where:
- hi = image height
- ho = object height

A positive magnification indicates an erect image.

A negative magnification indicates an inverted image.

The magnitude determines whether the image is enlarged or diminished.

## 7. Image Formation by a Convex Lens

The nature of the image depends on object position.

| Object position | Image position | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| At infinity | At focus | Real, inverted, highly diminished |
| Beyond 2F | Between F and 2F | Real, inverted, diminished |
| At 2F | At 2F | Real, inverted, same size |
| Between F and 2F | Beyond 2F | Real, inverted, enlarged |
| At F | At infinity | Real, inverted, highly enlarged in the ideal model |
| Between F and O | Same side as object | Virtual, erect, enlarged |

## 8. Image Formation by a Concave Lens

For a real object at any finite distance, a concave lens forms an image that is:

- Virtual.
- Erect.
- Diminished.
- Located between the optical centre and principal focus on the same side as the object.

This makes concave lenses useful for correcting certain vision defects.

## 9. Lens Maker's Formula

The lens maker's formula relates focal length to the refractive index and curvature of the lens surfaces.

For a thin lens in air:

1/f = (n - 1)(1/R1 - 1/R2)

Where:
- n = refractive index of lens material relative to surrounding air
- R1 = radius of curvature of the first surface
- R2 = radius of curvature of the second surface

The signs of R1 and R2 must follow the chosen Cartesian convention.

The formula assumes a thin lens and paraxial rays.

## 10. Power of a Lens

The power of a lens is the reciprocal of its focal length in metres.

P = 1/f

The SI-derived unit is the dioptre (D).

1 D = 1 m^-1

A convex lens has positive power.

A concave lens has negative power.

For example, a convex lens with focal length 0.5 m has:

P = 1/0.5

P = +2 D

## 11. Combination of Thin Lenses in Contact

When thin lenses are placed in contact, their combined power is approximately the sum of their individual powers.

P = P1 + P2 + P3 + ...

For two lenses:

1/F = 1/f1 + 1/f2

Where F is the effective focal length.

This relationship is useful in designing compound optical systems.

## 12. Lenses Separated by a Distance

For two thin lenses separated by a distance d in air:

1/F = 1/f1 + 1/f2 - d/(f1f2)

This formula assumes a suitable paraxial thin-lens arrangement and the same sign convention throughout.

When the separation is zero, it reduces to the contact-lens relationship.

## 13. Optical Aberrations

An ideal lens forms a perfect image, but practical lenses introduce imperfections.

### Spherical Aberration

Rays passing through different regions of a spherical lens may not converge at exactly the same point.

### Chromatic Aberration

Different wavelengths refract by different amounts because the refractive index depends on wavelength.

This may produce coloured fringes around images.

### Methods of Reducing Aberrations

- Using suitable lens combinations.
- Employing smaller apertures.
- Using aspherical surfaces.
- Combining materials with different dispersion properties.
- Applying optical design corrections.

## 14. Numerical Applications

**Example 1: Convex lens**

An object is placed 30 cm in front of a convex lens with focal length 15 cm.

Using the Cartesian convention:

u = -30 cm

f = +15 cm

1/f = 1/v - 1/u

1/15 = 1/v + 1/30

1/v = 1/15 - 1/30

1/v = 1/30

v = +30 cm

The image is formed 30 cm on the opposite side of the lens.

Magnification:

m = v/u = 30/(-30) = -1

The image is real, inverted and equal in size.

**Example 2: Power of a concave lens**

A concave lens has focal length -0.25 m.

P = 1/f

P = 1/(-0.25)

P = -4 D

**Example 3: Lenses in contact**

Two lenses have powers +3 D and -1 D.

Ptotal = 3 - 1

Ptotal = +2 D

The combination behaves as a converging system.

## Common Exam Traps

- Convex lenses converge paraxial parallel rays.
- Concave lenses diverge paraxial parallel rays.
- A convex lens can form real or virtual images.
- A concave lens forms a virtual, erect and diminished image for a real object.
- Lens formula and mirror formula use different algebraic forms.
- Lens magnification is v/u.
- The power of a lens is measured in dioptres.
- A concave lens has negative power.
- Powers of thin lenses in contact add algebraically.
- Chromatic aberration occurs because refractive index depends on wavelength.

## Quiz

1. A convex lens forms a real image when the object is placed:

- [ ] Between the lens and its principal focus
- [x] Beyond its principal focus
- [ ] Exactly at the optical centre
- [ ] Only at infinity

> A convex lens forms a real image when the object is outside its focal length. An object inside the focal length produces a virtual image.

2. A concave lens of focal length -0.5 m has power:

- [ ] +2 D
- [x] -2 D
- [ ] +0.5 D
- [ ] -0.5 D

> P = 1/f = 1/(-0.5) = -2 dioptres.

3. Two thin lenses with powers +4 D and +2 D are placed in contact. Their combined power is:

- [ ] +2 D
- [ ] +3 D
- [x] +6 D
- [ ] +8 D

> For thin lenses in contact, combined power is the algebraic sum: 4 + 2 = 6 D.
