# Lesson 2: History and Development of Psychology

Psychology developed through centuries of philosophical discussion, scientific investigation and experimentation. Early thinkers explored questions about the mind, knowledge and human nature. During the nineteenth century, psychology began developing into an independent scientific discipline.

## 1. Philosophical Foundations

Before psychology became an experimental science, many questions about the mind were investigated through philosophy.

### 1.1 Socrates

Socrates emphasised self-examination and understanding human knowledge.

His approach encouraged individuals to question assumptions and examine their beliefs through dialogue.

### 1.2 Plato

Plato discussed the relationship between the mind, knowledge and the body.

He believed that knowledge involved more than information obtained through the senses.

### 1.3 Aristotle

Aristotle examined memory, perception, learning and human behaviour.

His observations contributed to early discussions about psychological processes.

### 1.4 René Descartes

Descartes proposed a distinction between the mind and the physical body, commonly associated with mind-body dualism.

He also discussed reflexive behaviour and the role of the nervous system.

### 1.5 John Locke

Locke argued that the mind develops through experience.

His concept of *tabula rasa*, meaning blank slate, suggested that experience plays a major role in the formation of knowledge.

## 2. Emergence of Scientific Psychology

Psychology became an independent experimental discipline during the nineteenth century.

### 2.1 Wilhelm Wundt

<underline>Wilhelm Wundt</underline> established a psychology laboratory at the University of Leipzig in Germany in 1879.

This event is commonly treated as a major milestone in the establishment of experimental psychology.

Wundt investigated conscious experience using controlled experimental methods.

He is often called the father of experimental psychology.

### 2.2 Structuralism

Structuralism was associated with Edward B. Titchener, who developed ideas influenced by Wundt.

Its main objective was to analyse conscious experience into basic components.

These components included:

- Sensations.
- Images.
- Feelings.

Structuralists used introspection, a method involving the examination and reporting of one's conscious experiences.

**Limitation:** Introspection depends heavily on personal reports and may produce subjective or inconsistent results.

## 3. Major Schools of Psychology

Different schools of thought developed to explain psychological processes and behaviour.

### 3.1 Functionalism

Functionalism was strongly associated with William James.

It examined the functions and purposes of consciousness and behaviour.

Instead of asking only what the mind contains, functionalists asked how mental processes help individuals adapt to their environments.

Important ideas included:

- Adaptation.
- Consciousness.
- Individual differences.
- Practical applications of psychology.

### 3.2 Psychoanalysis

Psychoanalysis was developed by Sigmund Freud.

Freud emphasised the importance of unconscious mental processes, childhood experiences and internal conflicts.

Major concepts included:

- Unconscious mind.
- Repression.
- Dreams.
- Defence mechanisms.
- Psychosexual development.

Freud's theories influenced psychotherapy and personality psychology, although many specific claims remain debated and are not supported by modern experimental evidence.

### 3.3 Behaviourism

Behaviourism emerged as an influential school during the early twentieth century.

It focused on observable behaviour rather than relying primarily on introspection.

Important contributors included:

- John B. Watson.
- Ivan Pavlov.
- B. F. Skinner.

Behaviourists investigated how environmental stimuli and consequences influence behaviour.

### 3.4 Gestalt Psychology

Gestalt psychology developed in Germany and emphasised that psychological experiences should be understood as organised wholes.

Important contributors included:

- Max Wertheimer.
- Wolfgang Köhler.
- Kurt Koffka.

A central principle is that the whole is different from the mere sum of its parts.

For example, people generally perceive an organised shape rather than experiencing each individual line independently.

### 3.5 Humanistic Psychology

Humanistic psychology emphasised human potential, personal growth and individual experience.

Important contributors included:

- Abraham Maslow.
- Carl Rogers.

Its major ideas include:

- Free will.
- Self-concept.
- Personal responsibility.
- Self-actualisation.
- Personal growth.

### 3.6 Cognitive Psychology

Cognitive psychology investigates internal information-processing activities.

It examines:

- Attention.
- Perception.
- Memory.
- Language.
- Reasoning.
- Problem-solving.

The cognitive revolution during the twentieth century renewed scientific interest in mental processes.

## 4. Contemporary Perspectives

Modern psychology includes several complementary approaches.

| Perspective | Main focus |
|---|---|
| Biological | Brain, genes, hormones and nervous system |
| Behavioural | Learning and environmental influences |
| Cognitive | Thinking, memory and information processing |
| Psychodynamic | Unconscious processes and internal conflicts |
| Humanistic | Personal growth and individual experience |
| Evolutionary | Adaptive functions of psychological traits |
| Sociocultural | Social relationships and cultural influences |

These perspectives may explain different aspects of the same behaviour.

For example, anxiety can be examined through brain activity, learned associations, thought patterns, childhood experiences and social pressures.

## 5. Development of Psychology in India

Psychology in India has developed through interactions between Indian philosophical traditions and modern scientific approaches.

### 5.1 Ancient Indian Thought

Indian philosophical traditions explored consciousness, perception, attention, memory, self-awareness and the nature of the mind.

Important traditions include:

- Yoga.
- Vedanta.
- Buddhist philosophy.
- Nyaya philosophy.

These traditions provide historical and philosophical perspectives on psychological questions.

### 5.2 Modern Indian Psychology

Modern Indian psychology developed through universities, research institutions and professional practice.

The University of Calcutta played an important role in the establishment of experimental psychology in India.

The first experimental psychology laboratory in India was established there in 1915 under the direction of N. N. Sengupta.

Indian psychologists have contributed to areas including cognitive processes, social psychology, education, personality and indigenous psychological approaches.

## 6. Comparing Major Schools

| School | Central question |
|---|---|
| Structuralism | What are the basic elements of conscious experience? |
| Functionalism | What functions do mental processes serve? |
| Psychoanalysis | How do unconscious processes influence behaviour? |
| Behaviourism | How is behaviour learned through environmental interaction? |
| Gestalt | How are experiences organised into meaningful wholes? |
| Humanistic | How do people pursue growth and fulfilment? |
| Cognitive | How do people process and use information? |

## 7. Summary

- Psychology developed from philosophical investigations into an experimental science.
- Wundt established a major psychology laboratory in 1879.
- Structuralism examined the components of conscious experience.
- Functionalism examined the purpose of mental processes.
- Psychoanalysis emphasised unconscious processes.
- Behaviourism focused on observable behaviour and learning.
- Gestalt psychology examined organised perception.
- Humanistic psychology emphasised personal growth.
- Cognitive psychology investigates information processing.
- Modern psychology combines multiple perspectives.
