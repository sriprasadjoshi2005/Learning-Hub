# Lesson 33: Mood Disorders and Depressive Disorders

Mood disorders involve significant disturbances in emotional states that affect thinking, behaviour, motivation and everyday functioning. This lesson examines depressive disorders, bipolar disorders, their possible causes and major treatment approaches.

## 1. Understanding Mood

### 1.1 Meaning of Mood

Mood is a relatively lasting emotional state that influences how an individual experiences and interprets everyday situations.

Unlike brief emotions, moods may persist for hours, days or longer periods.

### 1.2 Normal Mood Variations

People naturally experience changes in mood due to:

- Daily experiences.
- Relationships.
- Physical health.
- Sleep patterns.
- Environmental conditions.
- Personal achievements and difficulties.

These variations do not automatically indicate a psychological disorder.

### 1.3 Mood Disorders

Mood disorders involve persistent or recurring disturbances in emotional functioning that cause significant distress or impairment.

## 2. Depressive Disorders

### 2.1 Major Depressive Disorder

Major depressive disorder (MDD) involves one or more major depressive episodes.

Common symptoms include:

- Persistent sadness or depressed mood.
- Loss of interest or pleasure.
- Changes in appetite or body weight.
- Sleep disturbances.
- Fatigue or reduced energy.
- Feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt.
- Difficulty concentrating.
- Recurrent thoughts about death or suicide.

A depressive episode generally involves symptoms lasting at least two weeks, with significant changes from previous functioning.

### 2.2 Persistent Depressive Disorder

Persistent depressive disorder, historically called dysthymia, involves a chronic pattern of depressed mood.

In adults, the depressed mood generally persists for at least two years, although symptom intensity may vary.

### 2.3 Seasonal Pattern

Some individuals experience depressive episodes that follow a recurring seasonal pattern.

For example, symptoms may become more prominent during particular seasons and improve during others.

This pattern is commonly associated with changes in daylight exposure and biological rhythms.

## 3. Bipolar Disorders

### 3.1 Meaning of Bipolar Disorders

Bipolar disorders involve episodes of significant mood disturbance, including periods of elevated or irritable mood and periods of depression.

### 3.2 Mania

Mania is a distinct period of abnormally elevated, expansive or irritable mood accompanied by increased energy and activity.

Common features include:

- Reduced need for sleep.
- Increased talking.
- Racing thoughts.
- Inflated self-confidence.
- Increased goal-directed activity.
- Impulsive or risky decisions.

A manic episode typically lasts at least one week, or any duration if hospitalisation is necessary.

### 3.3 Hypomania

Hypomania involves elevated or irritable mood and increased energy similar to mania, but with lower severity.

It lasts at least four consecutive days and does not cause the marked impairment or require the hospitalisation characteristic of mania.

### 3.4 Bipolar I Disorder

Bipolar I disorder is characterised by at least one manic episode.

Depressive episodes may also occur, but they are not required for the diagnosis.

### 3.5 Bipolar II Disorder

Bipolar II disorder involves:

- At least one hypomanic episode.
- At least one major depressive episode.
- No history of a manic episode.

Bipolar II disorder is not simply a milder form of Bipolar I disorder. Its depressive episodes can be severe and significantly impair functioning.

## 4. Differences Between Major Depressive Disorder and Bipolar Disorders

| Feature | Major Depressive Disorder | Bipolar Disorders |
|---|---|---|
| Depressive episodes | Present | May be present |
| Manic episodes | Absent | Present in Bipolar I |
| Hypomanic episodes | Absent | Present in Bipolar II |
| Mood pattern | Depressive episodes | Depressive and elevated mood episodes |
| Diagnosis | Based on depressive episodes | Based on manic or hypomanic episodes and other criteria |

The distinction is important because treatment approaches may differ.

## 5. Biological Factors

### 5.1 Genetic Influences

Genetic factors contribute to vulnerability to depressive and bipolar disorders.

However, inherited vulnerability does not mean that a person will necessarily develop a disorder.

### 5.2 Neurotransmitters

Neurotransmitters are chemical messengers that help nerve cells communicate.

Research has examined the possible roles of:

- Serotonin.
- Dopamine.
- Noradrenaline.

Mood disorders cannot be explained by a simple deficiency or excess of a single neurotransmitter.

### 5.3 Brain Structures

Research has identified associations between mood disorders and changes in the functioning of brain networks involved in:

- Emotional regulation.
- Motivation.
- Reward processing.
- Memory.
- Decision-making.

### 5.4 Circadian Rhythms

Circadian rhythms regulate biological processes across approximately 24-hour cycles.

Disruptions in sleep and daily rhythms may contribute to mood instability and are particularly relevant in bipolar disorders.

## 6. Psychological and Social Factors

### 6.1 Cognitive Factors

Negative thinking patterns may contribute to depressive experiences.

Examples include:

- Viewing oneself as worthless.
- Expecting negative outcomes.
- Interpreting failures as permanent.
- Ignoring positive experiences.

### 6.2 Stressful Life Events

Bereavement, relationship difficulties, financial problems and prolonged stress may contribute to the development or worsening of depressive symptoms.

### 6.3 Social Isolation

Limited social support and loneliness may increase vulnerability to emotional difficulties.

## 7. Treatment Approaches

### 7.1 Psychotherapy

Psychotherapy helps individuals understand emotional difficulties and develop healthier ways of thinking and behaving.

Common approaches include:

- Cognitive behavioural therapy.
- Interpersonal therapy.
- Behavioural activation.
- Family-focused therapy.

### 7.2 Medication

Depending on the diagnosis, healthcare professionals may prescribe:

- Antidepressants for certain depressive disorders.
- Mood stabilisers for bipolar disorders.
- Certain antipsychotic medications for manic or depressive episodes.

Medication must be selected and monitored by qualified healthcare professionals.

### 7.3 Electroconvulsive Therapy

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is a medical treatment that uses controlled electrical stimulation under general anaesthesia to produce a brief seizure.

It may be considered in selected severe cases, particularly when rapid treatment is needed or other approaches have not been effective.

### 7.4 Lifestyle and Support

Supportive measures may include:

- Maintaining regular sleep patterns.
- Following consistent daily routines.
- Participating in physical activity.
- Developing supportive relationships.
- Avoiding harmful substance use.
- Seeking professional care when symptoms persist.

These measures complement professional treatment rather than replacing it.

## 8. Suicide Risk and Professional Support

Severe depressive episodes may involve thoughts of death or suicide.

Such thoughts should be taken seriously. Individuals experiencing them should receive prompt support from qualified mental health professionals or emergency services when there is immediate danger.

## 9. Summary

Mood disorders involve significant disturbances in emotional functioning. Major depressive disorder involves depressive episodes, while bipolar disorders involve manic or hypomanic episodes, often alongside depression. Biological, psychological and social factors contribute to these conditions. Treatment may include psychotherapy, medication and supportive interventions.

   - [ ] At least one major depressive episode
   - [x] At least one manic episode
   - [ ] At least one hypomanic episode only
   - [ ] Persistent anxiety for two years

   > Bipolar I disorder is defined by the occurrence of at least one manic episode, regardless of whether a major depressive episode has occurred.

2. How long must a depressive episode generally last to meet the duration criterion for major depressive disorder?
   - [ ] At least three days
   - [ ] At least one week
   - [x] At least two weeks
   - [ ] At least six months

   > A major depressive episode generally requires at least two weeks of symptoms, including depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure.

3. Which statement correctly describes hypomania?
   - [ ] It is always more severe than mania.
   - [ ] It involves only depressive symptoms.
   - [x] It involves elevated or irritable mood without the marked impairment characteristic of mania.
   - [ ] It is identical to ordinary happiness.

   > Hypomania involves a noticeable change in mood and energy lasting at least four consecutive days, but it does not produce the marked impairment characteristic of mania.
