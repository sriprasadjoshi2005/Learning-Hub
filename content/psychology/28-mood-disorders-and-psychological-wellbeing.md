# Lesson 28: Mood Disorders and Psychological Well-Being

Mood disorders involve significant disturbances in emotional states that affect thinking, behaviour and everyday functioning. They include depressive disorders and bipolar disorders. Understanding these conditions requires examining emotional regulation, biological processes, cognitive patterns, social influences and evidence-based treatment.

## 1. Meaning of Mood

Mood refers to a relatively sustained emotional state that influences how an individual experiences and interprets situations.

Examples include:
- Happiness.
- Sadness.
- Irritability.
- Calmness.
- Anxiety.
- Emptiness.

Mood differs from an individual emotion because it often lasts longer and may not have one identifiable cause.

## 2. Meaning of Mood Disorders

Mood disorders involve clinically significant disturbances in mood that produce distress or impairment.

Major diagnostic groupings include:
- Depressive disorders.
- Bipolar and related disorders.

Mood disorders can influence:
- Energy.
- Motivation.
- Sleep.
- Appetite.
- Concentration.
- Self-esteem.
- Decision-making.
- Social relationships.

## 3. Depressive Disorders

Depressive disorders involve persistent disturbances in mood and related psychological or physical functioning.

They are not simply temporary sadness or ordinary disappointment.

### 3.1 Major Depressive Disorder

Major depressive disorder involves one or more major depressive episodes.

Symptoms may include:
- Persistent depressed mood.
- Reduced interest or pleasure.
- Changes in appetite or weight.
- Sleep disturbances.
- Fatigue.
- Feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt.
- Difficulty concentrating.
- Psychomotor changes.
- Recurrent thoughts of death or suicide.

A diagnosis requires an appropriate symptom pattern, duration and level of impairment.

### 3.2 Anhedonia

Anhedonia refers to a reduced ability to experience pleasure or interest in activities.

It is an important symptom associated with depression.

### 3.3 Persistent Depressive Disorder

Persistent depressive disorder involves a chronic pattern of depressed mood.

Symptoms may be less intense than those of a major depressive episode but can continue for a prolonged period.

## 4. Bipolar Disorders

Bipolar disorders involve significant changes in mood, energy and activity levels.

They include episodes of depression and mania or hypomania, depending on the specific diagnosis.

### 4.1 Mania

Mania involves a distinct period of abnormally elevated, expansive or irritable mood accompanied by increased energy or activity.

Possible features include:
- Reduced need for sleep.
- Increased talkativeness.
- Racing thoughts.
- Inflated self-esteem.
- Increased goal-directed activity.
- Impulsive or risky behaviour.

A manic episode causes significant impairment and may require hospitalisation.

### 4.2 Hypomania

Hypomania involves a similar pattern of mood and energy changes but is less severe than mania.

It does not produce the same degree of functional impairment or require hospitalisation because of the episode itself.

### 4.3 Bipolar I Disorder

Bipolar I disorder requires at least one manic episode.

Depressive episodes may also occur but are not required for the diagnosis.

### 4.4 Bipolar II Disorder

Bipolar II disorder involves:
- At least one hypomanic episode.
- At least one major depressive episode.
- No history of a manic episode.

Bipolar II disorder is not simply a milder version of bipolar I disorder because depressive symptoms can be severe and disabling.

## 5. Comparison of Depressive and Bipolar Disorders

| Feature | Depressive disorders | Bipolar disorders |
|---|---|---|
| Main disturbance | Depressive mood symptoms | Depressive and elevated mood episodes |
| Mania | Not a defining feature | Present in bipolar I |
| Hypomania | Not a defining feature | Present in bipolar II |
| Energy changes | Often reduced | May increase during elevated episodes |
| Diagnosis | Depends on specific depressive pattern | Depends on manic, hypomanic and depressive episode history |

## 6. Biological Factors

Research has investigated several biological influences on mood disorders.

### 6.1 Genetic Influences

Family and genetic studies suggest that vulnerability to mood disorders can be partly inherited.

Genetic vulnerability does not guarantee that an individual will develop a disorder.

### 6.2 Neurotransmitter Systems

Research examines neurotransmitters such as:
- Serotonin.
- Dopamine.
- Norepinephrine.

These systems contribute to mood, motivation and other brain functions.

Mood disorders cannot be explained simply as an imbalance of one neurotransmitter.

### 6.3 Brain Networks

Studies investigate differences in brain networks involved in:
- Emotional processing.
- Reward.
- Motivation.
- Cognitive control.
- Stress regulation.

### 6.4 Circadian Rhythms

Circadian rhythms regulate biological processes across approximately 24-hour cycles.

Sleep and circadian disruptions can be associated with mood symptoms and may influence the course of mood disorders.

## 7. Psychological Factors

### 7.1 Cognitive Model of Depression

Aaron Beck proposed that negative thinking patterns contribute to depression.

He described the cognitive triad:

1. Negative views of the self.
2. Negative views of the world.
3. Negative views of the future.

These patterns may influence how individuals interpret experiences.

### 7.2 Learned Helplessness

Learned helplessness refers to a pattern in which repeated experiences of uncontrollable negative events can reduce attempts to change later situations.

The concept was developed through research by Martin Seligman and colleagues.

### 7.3 Rumination

Rumination involves repetitive thinking about distressing experiences and their possible causes or consequences.

Persistent rumination may maintain negative mood and interfere with problem-solving.

## 8. Social and Environmental Influences

Mood disorders can be influenced by:
- Interpersonal conflict.
- Social isolation.
- Financial stress.
- Trauma.
- Loss.
- Discrimination.
- Chronic illness.
- Lack of social support.

Social and environmental factors interact with biological and psychological vulnerability.

## 9. Assessment of Mood Disorders

Assessment may involve:
- Clinical interviews.
- Symptom history.
- Evaluation of sleep and energy.
- Medical assessment.
- Family history.
- Functional evaluation.
- Risk assessment.

Clinicians must distinguish mood disorders from conditions that can produce similar symptoms.

Examples include thyroid disorders, medication effects and substance-related conditions.

## 10. Treatment Approaches

Treatment depends on diagnosis, severity and individual circumstances.

### 10.1 Cognitive Behavioural Therapy

CBT helps identify and modify unhelpful patterns of thought and behaviour.

It may support individuals in developing:
- More balanced interpretations.
- Behavioural activation.
- Problem-solving.
- Coping strategies.

### 10.2 Interpersonal Therapy

Interpersonal therapy focuses on relationships and social circumstances that may influence depressive symptoms.

### 10.3 Behavioural Activation

Behavioural activation encourages engagement in meaningful and rewarding activities.

It aims to reduce patterns of withdrawal and inactivity.

### 10.4 Medication

Medication may be used under professional supervision.

Antidepressants and mood-stabilising treatments have different uses and should be selected according to diagnosis and clinical needs.

### 10.5 Psychoeducation

Psychoeducation helps individuals and families understand symptoms, treatment and relapse prevention.

## 11. Suicide Risk and Support

Mood disorders may be associated with increased risk of suicidal thoughts or behaviour.

Assessment of suicide risk requires sensitive and direct professional evaluation.

Warning signs can include:
- Expressions of hopelessness.
- Persistent thoughts of death.
- Withdrawal.
- Significant behavioural changes.
- Seeking access to lethal means.

When someone may be in immediate danger, urgent support from emergency services or qualified healthcare professionals is necessary.

## 12. Relapse Prevention

Relapse prevention involves strategies that reduce the likelihood or impact of recurring episodes.

These may include:
- Following an agreed treatment plan.
- Monitoring early warning signs.
- Maintaining regular sleep routines.
- Managing stress.
- Attending follow-up appointments.
- Building social support.

## 13. Psychological Well-Being

Psychological well-being involves more than the absence of a mental disorder.

It includes:
- Positive relationships.
- Personal growth.
- Purpose.
- Autonomy.
- Self-acceptance.
- Environmental mastery.

Well-being can exist alongside psychological challenges, and individuals with mental health conditions can experience meaningful lives and relationships.

## 14. Summary

- Mood disorders involve clinically significant disturbances in mood and functioning.
- Depressive disorders include major depressive disorder and persistent depressive disorder.
- Bipolar disorders involve episodes of depression and elevated mood.
- Mania is more severe than hypomania.
- Bipolar I requires at least one manic episode.
- Bipolar II involves hypomanic and major depressive episodes without a history of mania.
- Biological, cognitive and social influences interact.
- Beck's cognitive triad describes negative views of the self, world and future.
- Treatment may include psychotherapy, medication and psychoeducation.
- Relapse prevention involves monitoring symptoms and maintaining appropriate support.
- Psychological well-being includes positive functioning and meaningful engagement with life.
