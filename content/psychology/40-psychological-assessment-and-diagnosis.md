# Lesson 40: Psychological Assessment and Diagnosis

Psychological assessment is the systematic process of gathering and interpreting information about an individual's thoughts, emotions, behaviour and cognitive abilities. It helps psychologists understand psychological functioning, identify difficulties and make informed decisions about diagnosis and treatment. This lesson examines major assessment methods, psychological testing, diagnostic principles and ethical considerations.

## 1. Understanding Psychological Assessment

### 1.1 Definition

Psychological assessment is a structured process used to evaluate psychological characteristics and functioning through multiple sources of information.

It may examine:

- Intelligence.
- Personality.
- Memory.
- Attention.
- Emotional functioning.
- Behaviour.
- Psychological symptoms.

### 1.2 Purposes of Assessment

Psychological assessment may be conducted to:

- Identify psychological difficulties.
- Support clinical diagnosis.
- Evaluate cognitive abilities.
- Understand personality characteristics.
- Guide treatment planning.
- Monitor progress.
- Support educational or occupational decisions.

### 1.3 Assessment and Diagnosis

Assessment is the broader process of collecting and interpreting information.

Diagnosis involves determining whether an individual's symptoms and experiences meet the criteria for a recognised psychological disorder.

An assessment does not always result in a diagnosis.

## 2. Clinical Interviews

### 2.1 Meaning of Clinical Interview

A clinical interview is a conversation conducted by a trained professional to collect information about an individual's psychological functioning.

### 2.2 Structured Interviews

Structured interviews follow a predetermined set of questions and procedures.

Advantages include:

- Greater consistency.
- Easier comparison between individuals.
- Reduced variation between interviewers.

### 2.3 Semi-Structured Interviews

Semi-structured interviews use planned questions while allowing additional questions when clarification is needed.

They combine consistency with flexibility.

### 2.4 Unstructured Interviews

Unstructured interviews allow the conversation to develop more freely.

They provide flexibility but may produce less standardised information.

## 3. Behavioural Observation

### 3.1 Meaning

Behavioural observation involves systematically recording and examining an individual's actions in a particular setting.

### 3.2 Naturalistic Observation

Naturalistic observation involves studying behaviour in its usual environment without deliberately manipulating the situation.

### 3.3 Controlled Observation

Controlled observation takes place in a structured environment where certain conditions are arranged to examine specific behaviours.

### 3.4 Limitations

Observation may be affected by:

- Observer bias.
- Reactivity to being observed.
- Environmental influences.
- Difficulty interpreting behaviour.

## 4. Psychological Testing

### 4.1 Meaning of Psychological Tests

Psychological tests are standardised instruments designed to measure particular psychological characteristics.

Examples include intelligence tests, personality inventories and symptom questionnaires.

### 4.2 Standardisation

Standardisation involves administering and scoring a test according to consistent procedures.

It helps ensure that results can be interpreted meaningfully.

### 4.3 Norms

Norms provide reference information based on the performance of a relevant comparison group.

An individual's score can be interpreted in relation to these reference values.

### 4.4 Objective and Projective Tests

Objective tests generally use standardised questions and scoring procedures.

Projective tests present ambiguous stimuli and examine the individual's responses.

Projective techniques require careful interpretation and have limitations in reliability and validity.

## 5. Reliability of Psychological Tests

### 5.1 Definition

Reliability refers to the consistency of a measurement.

A reliable test should produce reasonably consistent results when measurement conditions are comparable.

### 5.2 Test-Retest Reliability

Test-retest reliability examines whether a test produces consistent results when administered to the same individuals on separate occasions.

### 5.3 Inter-Rater Reliability

Inter-rater reliability measures the degree of agreement between different evaluators assessing the same behaviour or responses.

### 5.4 Internal Consistency

Internal consistency examines how consistently the items within a test measure the same underlying construct.

Cronbach's alpha is one commonly used measure.

## 6. Validity of Psychological Tests

### 6.1 Definition

Validity refers to the extent to which evidence supports the interpretation and intended use of test scores.

### 6.2 Content Validity

Content validity examines whether a test adequately represents the content or domain it is intended to measure.

### 6.3 Criterion-Related Validity

Criterion-related validity examines how well test scores relate to an external criterion.

It includes:

- Concurrent validity: comparison with a criterion measured at approximately the same time.
- Predictive validity: ability to predict a relevant future outcome.

### 6.4 Construct Validity

Construct validity examines whether a test measures the theoretical characteristic or psychological construct it claims to measure.

Examples include intelligence, anxiety and extraversion.

### 6.5 Reliability and Validity

A test may be reliable without being valid.

For example, a measuring instrument may consistently produce the same incorrect result.

However, strong reliability is generally necessary for meaningful test interpretation.

## 7. Intelligence Assessment

### 7.1 Intelligence Tests

Intelligence tests are designed to assess cognitive abilities such as reasoning, memory, problem-solving and verbal comprehension.

### 7.2 Intelligence Quotient

The historical ratio IQ formula is:

\[
IQ=\frac{\text{Mental Age}}{\text{Chronological Age}}\times100
\]

Modern intelligence tests generally use deviation IQ scores based on performance relative to an age-based reference group.

### 7.3 Interpretation

Intelligence test scores provide information about particular cognitive abilities but do not represent every aspect of an individual's potential or capabilities.

## 8. Personality Assessment

### 8.1 Personality Inventories

Personality inventories use standardised questions to assess personality characteristics.

Examples include inventories based on the Big Five personality traits.

### 8.2 Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory

The Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI) is a widely used psychological assessment instrument that includes scales designed to examine personality characteristics and psychological symptoms.

### 8.3 Projective Techniques

Projective techniques use ambiguous stimuli to explore patterns in how individuals interpret information.

Examples include:

- Rorschach Inkblot Test.
- Thematic Apperception Test.

These methods require trained interpretation and should not be treated as definitive evidence of a psychological disorder.

## 9. Symptom Assessment and Screening

### 9.1 Screening Instruments

Screening instruments help identify individuals who may benefit from more detailed assessment.

They do not independently establish a diagnosis.

### 9.2 Self-Report Questionnaires

Self-report questionnaires ask individuals to describe their thoughts, feelings and behaviours.

Advantages include efficiency and access to subjective experiences.

Limitations may include inaccurate recall, misunderstanding questions and socially desirable responding.

### 9.3 Multiple Sources of Information

A comprehensive assessment may combine:

- Clinical interviews.
- Standardised tests.
- Behavioural observations.
- Medical history.
- Developmental history.
- Information from relevant caregivers or family members, with appropriate consent.

## 10. Psychological Diagnosis

### 10.1 Diagnostic Classification

Diagnostic systems provide standardised criteria for identifying and describing psychological disorders.

The major systems include:

- DSM-5-TR.
- ICD-11.

### 10.2 Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnosis involves distinguishing between disorders that may produce similar symptoms.

For example, difficulties with concentration may occur in ADHD, depression, anxiety disorders and sleep-related conditions.

### 10.3 Comorbidity

Comorbidity refers to the presence of two or more disorders in the same individual.

It is important because conditions may influence one another and affect treatment planning.

### 10.4 Clinical Judgement

Diagnosis requires more than matching a list of symptoms.

Professionals must consider duration, severity, developmental history, cultural context and functional impairment.

## 11. Ethical Principles in Psychological Assessment

### 11.1 Informed Consent

Individuals should understand the purpose, procedures and possible uses of an assessment before participating.

### 11.2 Confidentiality

Assessment information must be protected and shared only under appropriate ethical and legal conditions.

### 11.3 Competence

Psychological tests should be administered and interpreted by professionals with appropriate training.

### 11.4 Fairness and Cultural Sensitivity

Assessment procedures should consider language, culture, disability and relevant social circumstances.

### 11.5 Avoiding Misuse

Test results should not be used to label, discriminate against or unfairly restrict individuals.

## 12. Summary

Psychological assessment uses interviews, observations and standardised tests to understand psychological functioning. Reliability concerns measurement consistency, while validity concerns the evidence supporting test-score interpretations. Assessment supports diagnosis, treatment planning and monitoring. Ethical practice requires informed consent, confidentiality, professional competence and cultural sensitivity.
