# Lesson 8: Learning and Conditioning

Learning is a relatively lasting change in knowledge or behaviour that occurs through experience, practice or observation. It is a fundamental psychological process that allows individuals to adapt to their environment, acquire skills and modify responses.

## 1. Meaning of Learning

Learning is a process through which experience produces a relatively lasting change in behaviour or knowledge.

Examples include:

- Learning to ride a bicycle.
- Acquiring a language.
- Remembering mathematical formulas.
- Developing problem-solving strategies.
- Learning to avoid dangerous situations.

Temporary changes caused only by fatigue, illness or immediate physiological conditions are not necessarily examples of learning.

## 2. Characteristics of Learning

Important characteristics include:

- Learning involves experience or practice.
- It produces relatively lasting changes.
- It can involve knowledge, skills, attitudes or behaviour.
- It may occur consciously or unconsciously.
- It can happen through direct experience or observation.
- Learning is influenced by motivation, attention and prior knowledge.

## 3. Types of Learning

Learning occurs through several processes.

### 3.1 Classical Conditioning

Classical conditioning is a form of associative learning in which an organism learns to associate two stimuli.

It was investigated extensively by Ivan Pavlov.

### 3.2 Pavlov's Experiment

Pavlov studied digestive responses in dogs.

Initially:

- Food naturally produced salivation.
- A bell produced no food-related salivation response.

During conditioning:

- A bell was repeatedly presented before food.
- The dog learned an association between the bell and food.

After conditioning:

- The bell alone could produce salivation.

### 3.3 Important Terms in Classical Conditioning

**Unconditioned stimulus (UCS):** A stimulus that naturally produces a response.

Example: Food.

**Unconditioned response (UCR):** The natural response to the unconditioned stimulus.

Example: Salivation in response to food.

**Neutral stimulus (NS):** A stimulus that initially does not produce the target conditioned response.

Example: A bell before conditioning.

**Conditioned stimulus (CS):** The previously neutral stimulus that produces a learned response after conditioning.

Example: The bell after conditioning.

**Conditioned response (CR):** The learned response to the conditioned stimulus.

Example: Salivation in response to the bell.

## 4. Processes in Classical Conditioning

### 4.1 Acquisition

Acquisition is the stage during which the association between the conditioned stimulus and unconditioned stimulus develops.

### 4.2 Extinction

Extinction occurs when the conditioned stimulus is repeatedly presented without the unconditioned stimulus, leading to a reduction in the conditioned response.

### 4.3 Spontaneous Recovery

Spontaneous recovery is the reappearance of an extinguished conditioned response after a period of rest.

### 4.4 Stimulus Generalisation

Stimulus generalisation occurs when stimuli similar to the conditioned stimulus produce a similar response.

Example: A dog conditioned to respond to one bell may respond to a similar bell.

### 4.5 Stimulus Discrimination

Stimulus discrimination occurs when an organism learns to respond differently to similar stimuli.

Example: A dog responds to one particular tone but not to other tones.

## 5. Operant Conditioning

Operant conditioning is a form of learning in which behaviour is influenced by its consequences.

It is strongly associated with B. F. Skinner.

### 5.1 Reinforcement

Reinforcement increases the likelihood of a behaviour occurring again.

There are two major types.

**Positive reinforcement:** Adding a desirable stimulus after a behaviour.

Example: Giving praise after completing a task.

**Negative reinforcement:** Removing an unpleasant stimulus after a behaviour.

Example: A loud warning sound stops when a person fastens a seat belt.

Both types increase the behaviour.

### 5.2 Punishment

Punishment decreases the likelihood of a behaviour occurring again.

**Positive punishment:** Adding an unpleasant consequence after a behaviour.

Example: Receiving an additional task after breaking an agreed rule.

**Negative punishment:** Removing a desirable stimulus after a behaviour.

Example: Losing access to a preferred activity after repeatedly violating a rule.

### 5.3 Reinforcement and Punishment Compared

| Process | What happens? | Effect on behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Positive reinforcement | Adds a desirable stimulus | Increases |
| Negative reinforcement | Removes an unpleasant stimulus | Increases |
| Positive punishment | Adds an unpleasant stimulus | Decreases |
| Negative punishment | Removes a desirable stimulus | Decreases |

The terms positive and negative refer to adding and removing something, not to whether an outcome is morally good or bad.

## 6. Schedules of Reinforcement

A reinforcement schedule determines when reinforcement is provided.

### 6.1 Continuous Reinforcement

Every desired response is reinforced.

It can help establish a new behaviour quickly.

### 6.2 Partial Reinforcement

Only some responses are reinforced.

Four common schedules are:

**Fixed Ratio (FR):** Reinforcement after a fixed number of responses.

Example: A reward after every five completed tasks.

**Variable Ratio (VR):** Reinforcement after an unpredictable number of responses.

Example: Rewards occurring after an unpredictable number of attempts.

**Fixed Interval (FI):** Reinforcement for the first appropriate response after a fixed period.

Example: A weekly assessment.

**Variable Interval (VI):** Reinforcement for the first appropriate response after varying periods.

Example: Unpredictable checks of task completion.

## 7. Observational Learning

Observational learning occurs when individuals learn by watching the behaviour of others and observing its consequences.

Albert Bandura contributed significantly to this field.

### 7.1 Bobo Doll Experiment

Bandura's famous experiment investigated whether children would imitate aggressive behaviour after observing adults.

Children who observed an aggressive model sometimes reproduced similar actions.

The findings demonstrated that behaviour can be acquired through observation without direct reinforcement.

### 7.2 Processes of Observational Learning

Four important processes are:

1. **Attention:** The individual notices the model's behaviour.
2. **Retention:** The individual remembers the behaviour.
3. **Reproduction:** The individual has the ability to perform it.
4. **Motivation:** The individual has a reason to reproduce the behaviour.

## 8. Cognitive Learning

Cognitive learning involves changes in knowledge, understanding and mental representations.

### 8.1 Latent Learning

Latent learning occurs when knowledge is acquired without immediately being demonstrated.

The learned information may become visible when an appropriate motivation or situation appears.

### 8.2 Insight Learning

Insight learning involves suddenly understanding a problem or discovering a solution through reorganising information.

Wolfgang Köhler's studies of chimpanzees are historically associated with this concept.

### 8.3 Cognitive Maps

A cognitive map is a mental representation of spatial relationships in an environment.

For example, an individual may remember the layout of a building without consciously memorising every route.

## 9. Factors Influencing Learning

Learning is influenced by several factors.

**Motivation:** Provides the willingness to engage in learning.

**Attention:** Helps individuals focus on relevant information.

**Practice:** Repeated engagement can strengthen skills and knowledge.

**Feedback:** Information about performance helps individuals identify errors.

**Prior knowledge:** Existing knowledge influences how new information is understood.

**Sleep:** Supports memory consolidation and learning.

**Environment:** Noise, distractions and available resources can influence performance.

## 10. Applications of Learning Principles

Learning theories have practical applications.

### Education

Teachers use reinforcement, practice, feedback and observation to support learning.

### Behaviour Modification

Behavioural principles can help develop desirable habits and reduce unwanted behaviours.

### Parenting

Consistent consequences and positive reinforcement can support behaviour development.

### Workplace Training

Demonstrations, feedback and repeated practice can improve job-related skills.

### Animal Training

Classical and operant conditioning principles are used in animal training.

## 11. Summary

- Learning produces relatively lasting changes through experience.
- Classical conditioning involves associations between stimuli.
- Pavlov demonstrated classical conditioning through experiments with dogs.
- Acquisition, extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalisation and discrimination are important conditioning processes.
- Operant conditioning involves learning through consequences.
- Reinforcement increases behaviour, while punishment decreases behaviour.
- Positive means adding, and negative means removing.
- Observational learning occurs through watching others.
- Bandura investigated observational learning through the Bobo doll experiment.
- Cognitive learning includes latent learning and insight learning.
