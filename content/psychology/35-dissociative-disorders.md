# Lesson 35: Dissociative Disorders

Dissociative disorders involve disruptions in the normal integration of consciousness, memory, identity, emotion, perception and behaviour. These disruptions may affect how individuals experience themselves and remember events. This lesson examines the major types of dissociative disorders, their possible causes and treatment approaches.

## 1. Understanding Dissociation

### 1.1 Meaning of Dissociation

Dissociation refers to a disruption in the integration of psychological processes that normally function together.

It may involve:

- Gaps in memory.
- Feeling disconnected from oneself.
- Experiencing surroundings as unreal.
- Disruptions in identity.
- Difficulty recalling personal experiences.

### 1.2 Ordinary Dissociation

Mild dissociative experiences can occur in everyday life.

Examples include becoming deeply absorbed in a book or temporarily losing awareness of one's surroundings while daydreaming.

These experiences do not necessarily indicate a disorder.

### 1.3 Dissociative Disorders

Dissociative disorders involve more persistent or severe disruptions that cause significant distress or impairment.

## 2. Dissociative Amnesia

### 2.1 Definition

Dissociative amnesia involves an inability to recall important autobiographical information, usually associated with stressful or traumatic experiences.

The memory loss is more extensive than ordinary forgetting and cannot be adequately explained by normal memory processes.

### 2.2 Characteristics

Common features include:

- Difficulty remembering personal events.
- Gaps in autobiographical memory.
- Confusion about aspects of personal history.
- Significant distress related to memory loss.

### 2.3 Dissociative Fugue

Dissociative fugue is a subtype of dissociative amnesia involving unexpected travel or wandering accompanied by confusion about identity or an inability to recall important personal information.

## 3. Dissociative Identity Disorder

### 3.1 Definition

Dissociative identity disorder (DID) involves a disruption of identity characterised by two or more distinct personality states.

These states may involve differences in patterns of behaviour, memory, perception and emotional experience.

### 3.2 Characteristics

Possible features include:

- Discontinuities in identity and sense of self.
- Recurrent gaps in memory.
- Changes in behaviour and emotional responses.
- Distress or impairment in everyday functioning.

### 3.3 Identity States

Different identity states may have distinct ways of interpreting experiences and responding to situations.

The individual may experience memory gaps between certain identity states.

### 3.4 Possible Developmental Factors

DID has been associated with severe and repeated childhood trauma in many clinical accounts.

However, its development is complex, and diagnosis requires careful professional assessment.

## 4. Depersonalisation/Derealisation Disorder

### 4.1 Depersonalisation

Depersonalisation refers to experiences of detachment from one's own body, thoughts, feelings or sense of self.

An individual may feel as though they are observing themselves from outside their body.

### 4.2 Derealisation

Derealisation involves experiences in which the external world feels unreal, distant or dreamlike.

People may describe familiar surroundings as artificial or visually altered.

### 4.3 Important Characteristics

Individuals experiencing these symptoms generally retain awareness that their experiences are subjective rather than literal changes in reality.

Persistent or recurrent symptoms that cause significant distress may indicate depersonalisation/derealisation disorder.

## 5. Causes and Explanations

### 5.1 Trauma and Stress

Severe or repeated traumatic experiences may contribute to dissociative symptoms.

Dissociation may function as a psychological response to overwhelming experiences.

### 5.2 Psychological Factors

Certain psychological processes may contribute to dissociation, including:

- Difficulty regulating intense emotions.
- Disrupted autobiographical memory.
- Avoidance of distressing experiences.
- Difficulties integrating conflicting experiences.

### 5.3 Biological Factors

Research has examined possible differences in brain systems involved in memory, emotional regulation and self-awareness.

However, no single biological explanation accounts for all dissociative disorders.

## 6. Diagnosis and Assessment

### 6.1 Clinical Interview

Professionals assess memory difficulties, identity disruptions, experiences of detachment and their impact on daily functioning.

### 6.2 Differential Diagnosis

Dissociative symptoms must be distinguished from conditions involving:

- Neurological disorders.
- Substance-related effects.
- Sleep-related disturbances.
- Other psychological disorders.
- Medical conditions affecting memory or consciousness.

### 6.3 Importance of Careful Assessment

Diagnosis should avoid assuming that every memory gap or unusual experience indicates a dissociative disorder.

## 7. Treatment Approaches

### 7.1 Psychotherapy

Psychotherapy is a major component of treatment.

It may focus on:

- Developing emotional regulation skills.
- Improving awareness of dissociative experiences.
- Building a sense of safety.
- Processing traumatic experiences when appropriate.
- Strengthening everyday functioning.

### 7.2 Trauma-Informed Treatment

Trauma-informed approaches recognise the possible effects of traumatic experiences and prioritise safety, trust and individual control.

### 7.3 Grounding Techniques

Grounding techniques help individuals reconnect with their present surroundings.

Examples include:

- Identifying objects in the environment.
- Focusing on physical sensations.
- Describing sounds in the surroundings.
- Practising controlled breathing.

### 7.4 Treatment Considerations

Treatment should be individualised and guided by qualified professionals. Medication may be used to address associated symptoms or co-occurring conditions, but there is no specific medication that directly cures dissociative disorders.

## 8. Summary

Dissociative disorders involve disruptions in memory, identity, consciousness or perception. Major conditions include dissociative amnesia, dissociative identity disorder and depersonalisation/derealisation disorder. Trauma and psychological stress may contribute to their development. Careful assessment and individualised psychotherapy are important components of treatment.
