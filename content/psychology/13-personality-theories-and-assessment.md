# Lesson 13: Personality Theories and Assessment

Personality refers to relatively enduring patterns of thoughts, emotions, motivations and behaviour that distinguish individuals. Personality psychology investigates how these patterns develop, how they differ between people and how they influence behaviour across situations.

## 1. Meaning of Personality

Personality is the relatively consistent organisation of psychological characteristics that influences how an individual thinks, feels and behaves.

Personality includes:
- Emotional tendencies.
- Behavioural patterns.
- Attitudes.
- Motivations.
- Social preferences.
- Characteristic ways of responding to situations.

Personality is relatively stable but can change through development, experience and environmental influences.

## 2. Characteristics of Personality

### 2.1 Individual Differences

Individuals differ in characteristics such as sociability, emotional stability and openness to experience.

### 2.2 Relative Stability

Many personality characteristics demonstrate consistency over time.

However, stability does not mean that personality never changes.

### 2.3 Organisation

Personality characteristics interact to produce patterns of behaviour.

### 2.4 Interaction with the Environment

Personality influences how individuals respond to situations, while environmental experiences can also influence personality development.

## 3. Freud's Psychoanalytic Theory

Sigmund Freud proposed that personality involves interactions between conscious and unconscious processes.

### 3.1 Levels of Awareness

Freud described three levels of mental activity.

**Conscious:** Information currently within awareness.

**Preconscious:** Information not currently in awareness but accessible through attention or recall.

**Unconscious:** Mental processes and contents that are not directly accessible to ordinary awareness in Freud's theoretical framework.

### 3.2 Structure of Personality

Freud proposed three interacting personality structures.

**Id:**
- Operates according to the pleasure principle.
- Seeks immediate satisfaction.
- Represents basic instinctual desires.

**Ego:**
- Operates according to the reality principle.
- Attempts to balance desires with external conditions.
- Helps with planning and decision-making.

**Superego:**
- Represents internalised moral standards and ideals.
- Influences guilt, pride and moral judgement.

### 3.3 Defence Mechanisms

Defence mechanisms are psychological processes that Freud and later psychoanalytic theorists described as ways of managing anxiety or internal conflict.

Important examples include:

| Defence mechanism | Description |
|---|---|
| Repression | Keeping distressing thoughts outside conscious awareness |
| Denial | Refusing to acknowledge an uncomfortable reality |
| Projection | Attributing one's own unacceptable feelings to another person |
| Displacement | Redirecting an emotional response towards a safer target |
| Rationalisation | Providing seemingly logical explanations for behaviour |
| Regression | Returning to earlier patterns of behaviour |
| Sublimation | Redirecting unacceptable impulses into socially acceptable activities |

These concepts are influential historically, but specific defence mechanisms can be difficult to measure scientifically.

## 4. Psychosexual Stages of Development

Freud proposed five stages of psychosexual development.

1. **Oral stage:** Associated with the mouth and feeding.
2. **Anal stage:** Associated with bowel control and toilet training.
3. **Phallic stage:** Associated with awareness of biological sex differences.
4. **Latency stage:** A period in which sexual impulses are considered less prominent.
5. **Genital stage:** Associated with mature sexuality and relationships.

Freud proposed that unresolved conflicts could influence later personality.

These stages are historically influential but are not accepted as established developmental facts in contemporary psychology.

## 5. Jung's Analytical Psychology

Carl Jung developed analytical psychology.

He introduced several concepts.

### 5.1 Personal Unconscious

The personal unconscious contains experiences and memories that are not currently conscious.

### 5.2 Collective Unconscious

Jung proposed a collective unconscious containing inherited symbolic patterns called archetypes.

This is a theoretical concept and is not established as a scientifically demonstrated inherited memory system.

### 5.3 Archetypes

Archetypes are recurring symbolic patterns that Jung believed influence human experiences and cultural representations.

Examples include:
- The hero.
- The shadow.
- The caregiver.
- The wise figure.

## 6. Trait Theories of Personality

Trait theories describe personality through relatively stable characteristics called traits.

A trait represents a tendency to think, feel or behave in a particular way.

### 6.1 Allport's Trait Theory

Gordon Allport classified traits into three broad categories.

**Cardinal traits:** Dominant characteristics that strongly influence much of an individual's behaviour.

**Central traits:** Important characteristics that describe an individual.

**Secondary traits:** More specific tendencies that appear in particular situations.

### 6.2 Cattell's Sixteen Personality Factors

Raymond Cattell used factor analysis to identify 16 personality factors.

His work contributed to the development of structured personality assessment.

## 7. The Big Five Personality Model

### 7.1 Openness to Experience

Involves imagination, curiosity, intellectual interests and willingness to explore new experiences.

### 7.2 Conscientiousness

Involves organisation, responsibility, discipline and goal-directed behaviour.

### 7.3 Extraversion

Involves sociability, assertiveness, energy and tendency towards positive social engagement.

### 7.4 Agreeableness

Involves cooperation, compassion, trust and consideration for others.

### 7.5 Neuroticism

Involves tendencies towards negative emotional experiences such as anxiety, insecurity and emotional instability.

The Big Five describes dimensions rather than fixed personality categories. Individuals can demonstrate different degrees of each trait.

## 8. Humanistic Theories of Personality

Humanistic psychologists emphasised personal experience, self-development and individual potential.

### 8.1 Carl Rogers

Rogers proposed that personality development is influenced by self-concept and experiences of acceptance.

Important concepts include:

**Self-concept:** An individual's perception of their own characteristics and identity.

**Ideal self:** The person an individual would like to become.

**Congruence:** A degree of consistency between self-concept and experience.

**Unconditional positive regard:** Acceptance and respect without making personal worth entirely dependent on meeting particular conditions.

### 8.2 Abraham Maslow

Maslow's approach emphasised growth, personal potential and self-actualisation.

He proposed that individuals seek to develop their abilities and pursue meaningful goals.

## 9. Social-Cognitive Theory

Social-cognitive theories explain personality through interactions between cognitive processes, behaviour and environmental conditions.

### 9.1 Reciprocal Determinism

Albert Bandura proposed reciprocal determinism.

It describes three interacting factors:

- Personal factors.
- Behaviour.
- Environment.

Each factor can influence the others.

Example:

An individual's confidence influences whether they participate in a group. Their participation changes how others respond, which may then influence their confidence.

### 9.2 Self-Efficacy

Self-efficacy refers to beliefs about one's ability to perform particular tasks.

It influences motivation, persistence and behavioural choices.

## 10. Biological and Environmental Influences

Personality development involves both biological and environmental factors.

### Biological Influences

These include:
- Genetic variation.
- Temperamental characteristics.
- Nervous system functioning.
- Physiological processes.

### Environmental Influences

These include:
- Family relationships.
- Education.
- Culture.
- Peer groups.
- Life experiences.

Neither biological inheritance nor environmental experience alone explains the full development of personality.

## 11. Personality Assessment

Personality assessment involves evaluating characteristic patterns of thoughts, feelings and behaviour.

### 11.1 Self-Report Inventories

Individuals answer standardised questions about their own characteristics.

Examples include:
- Big Five personality questionnaires.
- Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI).

Advantages include standardisation and efficient administration.

Limitations include response bias and inaccurate self-perception.

### 11.2 Interviews

Structured or semi-structured interviews collect information through questions and discussion.

### 11.3 Behavioural Observation

Researchers or clinicians observe behaviour in relevant settings.

### 11.4 Projective Techniques

Projective techniques use ambiguous stimuli to encourage individuals to produce responses.

Examples include:
- Rorschach Inkblot Test.
- Thematic Apperception Test (TAT).

Their interpretation and psychometric properties vary, and they require careful professional evaluation.

## 12. Personality and Situation

Personality traits influence behaviour, but situations also matter.

For example, an individual who is usually outgoing may remain quiet during an unfamiliar or stressful situation.

Modern personality psychology therefore considers interactions between personal characteristics and environmental conditions.

## 13. Summary

- Personality consists of relatively enduring patterns of thought, emotion and behaviour.
- Freud proposed the id, ego and superego.
- Psychoanalytic theory introduced the concept of defence mechanisms.
- Jung developed analytical psychology and the concept of archetypes.
- Trait theories describe stable personality characteristics.
- The Big Five includes openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism.
- Humanistic psychology emphasises personal growth and self-concept.
- Social-cognitive theory examines interactions between personal factors, behaviour and environment.
- Personality develops through biological and environmental influences.
- Personality assessment uses inventories, interviews, observation and other techniques.
