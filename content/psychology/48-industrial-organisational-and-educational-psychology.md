# Lesson 48: Industrial-Organisational and Educational Psychology

Industrial-organisational psychology applies psychological principles to workplaces, organisations and employment. Educational psychology studies how individuals learn and how teaching environments can support development. This lesson examines the major theories, methods and applications of both fields.

## 1. Introduction to Industrial-Organisational Psychology

### 1.1 Definition

Industrial-organisational psychology is the scientific study of human behaviour in workplaces and organisations.

It applies psychological knowledge to improve employee performance, satisfaction, well-being and organisational effectiveness.

### 1.2 Major Areas

Industrial-organisational psychology includes:

- Personnel selection.
- Employee training.
- Performance evaluation.
- Leadership.
- Workplace motivation.
- Organisational culture.
- Occupational health.
- Job satisfaction.

## 2. Personnel Selection and Recruitment

### 2.1 Job Analysis

Job analysis is the systematic process of identifying the tasks, responsibilities and requirements of a particular job.

It provides information for recruitment, training and performance assessment.

### 2.2 Recruitment

Recruitment involves attracting individuals who may be suitable for a position.

### 2.3 Selection Methods

Common selection methods include:

- Interviews.
- Aptitude tests.
- Personality assessments.
- Work-sample tests.
- Assessment centres.
- Reference checks.

### 2.4 Reliability and Validity

Selection procedures should demonstrate reliability and validity.

A selection test should consistently measure relevant characteristics and provide useful information about job performance.

## 3. Employee Training and Development

### 3.1 Training

Training involves developing the knowledge and skills required to perform job responsibilities.

### 3.2 Training Needs Analysis

Training needs analysis identifies gaps between current employee capabilities and the requirements of a role.

### 3.3 Training Methods

Common methods include:

- Demonstrations.
- Simulations.
- Workshops.
- Mentoring.
- On-the-job training.
- Online learning.

### 3.4 Evaluation

Training effectiveness can be evaluated through changes in knowledge, behaviour, performance and organisational outcomes.

## 4. Motivation in the Workplace

### 4.1 Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

Maslow proposed that human motivation involves different categories of needs:

1. Physiological needs.
2. Safety needs.
3. Love and belonging.
4. Esteem.
5. Self-actualisation.

The hierarchy is influential, although human motivation does not always follow a rigid sequence.

### 4.2 Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory

Frederick Herzberg distinguished between:

**Hygiene factors:** Conditions such as salary, job security and workplace policies that can reduce dissatisfaction.

**Motivators:** Factors such as achievement, recognition and responsibility that can increase job satisfaction.

### 4.3 Expectancy Theory

Victor Vroom's expectancy theory explains motivation through three relationships:

- Expectancy: belief that effort can produce performance.
- Instrumentality: belief that performance will lead to outcomes.
- Valence: value placed on those outcomes.

The theory is often represented as:

\[
M=E\times I\times V
\]

Where:

- \(M\) = motivational force.
- \(E\) = expectancy.
- \(I\) = instrumentality.
- \(V\) = valence.

### 4.4 Equity Theory

Equity theory proposes that employees evaluate fairness by comparing their contributions and outcomes with those of others.

Perceived unfairness may influence motivation and job satisfaction.

## 5. Leadership and Management

### 5.1 Definition of Leadership

Leadership involves influencing and guiding individuals or groups towards shared goals.

### 5.2 Trait Theory

Trait theories examine personal characteristics associated with leadership.

### 5.3 Behavioural Approaches

Behavioural approaches focus on what leaders do rather than only on their personal characteristics.

### 5.4 Transformational Leadership

Transformational leadership involves inspiring employees through a shared vision, intellectual stimulation and individual consideration.

### 5.5 Transactional Leadership

Transactional leadership uses structured expectations, rewards and corrective actions to guide performance.

### 5.6 Situational Leadership

Situational approaches recognise that effective leadership depends on the task, environment and characteristics of the people being led.

## 6. Job Satisfaction and Organisational Behaviour

### 6.1 Job Satisfaction

Job satisfaction refers to an individual's overall evaluation of their job and work experiences.

### 6.2 Organisational Commitment

Organisational commitment refers to an employee's psychological attachment to an organisation.

### 6.3 Organisational Culture

Organisational culture includes shared values, beliefs, assumptions and practices that influence behaviour within an organisation.

### 6.4 Workplace Conflict

Conflict may arise from differences in goals, communication, expectations or resource allocation.

Constructive conflict management can improve understanding and problem-solving.

## 7. Occupational Stress and Employee Well-Being

### 7.1 Workplace Stressors

Common workplace stressors include:

- Excessive workload.
- Unclear responsibilities.
- Limited control.
- Poor communication.
- Job insecurity.
- Interpersonal conflict.

### 7.2 Burnout

Burnout is an occupational phenomenon associated with chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed.

It is characterised by:

- Energy depletion or exhaustion.
- Increased mental distance from work or cynicism.
- Reduced professional efficacy.

### 7.3 Workplace Well-Being

Organisations can support employee well-being through manageable workloads, fair treatment, supportive leadership and access to appropriate resources.

## 8. Introduction to Educational Psychology

### 8.1 Definition

Educational psychology studies the psychological processes involved in teaching, learning, motivation, assessment and educational development.

### 8.2 Major Areas

Educational psychology examines:

- Learning theories.
- Cognitive development.
- Student motivation.
- Classroom behaviour.
- Individual differences.
- Assessment.
- Learning environments.

## 9. Learning Theories in Education

### 9.1 Behaviourism

Behaviourism explains learning through observable behaviour and environmental consequences.

Classical and operant conditioning are important foundations.

### 9.2 Cognitivism

Cognitive approaches examine internal mental processes such as memory, attention and problem-solving.

### 9.3 Constructivism

Constructivism proposes that learners actively construct knowledge through experience and interaction with information.

### 9.4 Social Learning Theory

Albert Bandura's social learning theory emphasises learning through observation, imitation and modelling.

### 9.5 Social Constructivism

Social constructivist approaches emphasise the role of social interaction and cultural tools in learning.

## 10. Motivation in Education

### 10.1 Intrinsic Motivation

Intrinsic motivation involves engaging in an activity because it is personally interesting or satisfying.

### 10.2 Extrinsic Motivation

Extrinsic motivation involves engaging in an activity to obtain external rewards or avoid negative consequences.

### 10.3 Self-Efficacy

Self-efficacy refers to an individual's belief in their ability to perform a particular task.

Higher self-efficacy may encourage persistence and effort when facing challenges.

### 10.4 Growth Mindset

A growth mindset involves the belief that abilities can develop through learning, practice and effective strategies.

It contrasts with a fixed mindset, which treats abilities as largely unchangeable.

## 11. Individual Differences in Learning

### 11.1 Cognitive Differences

Learners differ in memory, attention, reasoning and prior knowledge.

### 11.2 Learning Difficulties

Some learners experience difficulties that require additional educational support.

These may include dyslexia, dyscalculia and ADHD-related learning challenges.

### 11.3 Inclusive Education

Inclusive education aims to provide meaningful learning opportunities for individuals with different abilities, backgrounds and support needs.

### 11.4 Differentiated Instruction

Differentiated instruction involves adapting teaching methods, materials and assessment to address learner differences.

## 12. Educational Assessment

### 12.1 Formative Assessment

Formative assessment monitors learning during instruction and provides feedback that can guide improvement.

### 12.2 Summative Assessment

Summative assessment evaluates learning at the end of a defined instructional period.

### 12.3 Diagnostic Assessment

Diagnostic assessment identifies specific areas of difficulty and informs instructional planning.

### 12.4 Feedback

Effective feedback identifies what a learner has done well, where improvement is needed and how to make progress.

## 13. Applications

Industrial-organisational psychology contributes to employee selection, leadership, workplace well-being and organisational development.

Educational psychology contributes to instructional design, student motivation, assessment, inclusive education and learning support.

## 14. Summary

Industrial-organisational psychology applies psychological principles to workplace performance, motivation, leadership and employee well-being. Educational psychology examines learning, teaching, cognitive development and educational assessment. Both fields use scientific evidence to improve individual functioning and the environments in which people work and learn.
