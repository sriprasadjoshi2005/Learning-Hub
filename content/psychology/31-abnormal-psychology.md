# Lesson 31: Abnormal Psychology: Understanding Psychological Disorders

Abnormal psychology is the scientific study of unusual patterns of thought, emotion and behaviour that may cause psychological distress or interfere with everyday functioning. It examines how psychological disorders develop, how they affect individuals and how they can be understood and treated.

## 1. Understanding Psychological Abnormality

### 1.1 What Is Abnormal Behaviour?

Abnormal behaviour refers to patterns of thinking, feeling or acting that significantly differ from expected functioning and may cause distress or impairment.

Not every unusual behaviour is a psychological disorder. Human behaviour varies across cultures, situations and individuals.

### 1.2 Four Common Criteria

Psychologists commonly consider four dimensions when evaluating abnormal behaviour:

1. **Deviance:** Behaviour differs substantially from social or cultural expectations.
2. **Distress:** The individual experiences significant emotional suffering.
3. **Dysfunction:** Behaviour interferes with work, relationships or daily activities.
4. **Danger:** Behaviour creates a risk of harm to oneself or others.

These criteria are considered together rather than treated as a strict checklist.

## 2. Historical Explanations of Psychological Disorders

### 2.1 Supernatural Explanations

Early societies sometimes attributed unusual behaviour to spirits, possession or supernatural forces.

### 2.2 Biological Explanations

The biological perspective connects psychological disorders with brain function, genetics, hormones and nervous-system activity.

### 2.3 Psychological Explanations

Psychological explanations examine experiences, learning, thought patterns, emotional conflicts and environmental influences.

### 2.4 Modern Biopsychosocial Perspective

The biopsychosocial model explains psychological disorders through the interaction of:

- Biological factors: genes, brain chemistry and physical health.
- Psychological factors: beliefs, emotions, personality and coping strategies.
- Social factors: relationships, culture, family environment and socioeconomic conditions.

No single factor explains every disorder.

## 3. Classification of Psychological Disorders

### 3.1 Purpose of Classification

Classification systems help psychologists and psychiatrists:

- Describe patterns of symptoms.
- Communicate diagnostic information.
- Organise research.
- Plan appropriate treatment.
- Study the causes and development of disorders.

### 3.2 Diagnostic Systems

Two widely recognised classification systems are:

- **DSM-5-TR:** Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, published by the American Psychiatric Association.
- **ICD-11:** International Classification of Diseases, developed by the World Health Organization.

These systems provide diagnostic criteria, but diagnosis requires professional judgement and consideration of individual circumstances.

## 4. Major Categories of Psychological Disorders

### 4.1 Anxiety Disorders

Anxiety disorders involve excessive fear, worry or avoidance that interferes with functioning.

Examples include:

- Generalised anxiety disorder.
- Panic disorder.
- Specific phobias.
- Social anxiety disorder.

### 4.2 Mood Disorders

Mood disorders involve significant disturbances in emotional states.

Examples include:

- Major depressive disorder.
- Bipolar disorders.

### 4.3 Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders

These disorders involve persistent intrusive thoughts, repetitive behaviours or related difficulties.

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) may involve unwanted thoughts and repetitive actions intended to reduce distress.

### 4.4 Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders

These disorders may develop following traumatic or highly stressful experiences.

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is one example.

### 4.5 Psychotic Disorders

Psychotic disorders can involve disturbances in perception, thinking and the interpretation of reality.

Schizophrenia is one example and may involve hallucinations, delusions and disorganised thinking.

## 5. Causes and Risk Factors

Psychological disorders can develop through interacting influences.

### 5.1 Genetic Vulnerability

Some disorders have a genetic component. However, genetic vulnerability does not guarantee that a disorder will develop.

### 5.2 Brain and Neurochemical Factors

Differences in neural circuits, neurotransmitter systems and brain development may contribute to psychological difficulties.

### 5.3 Environmental Experiences

Childhood adversity, chronic stress, trauma, isolation and difficult living conditions may increase vulnerability.

### 5.4 Cognitive and Behavioural Factors

Unhelpful beliefs, learned avoidance and persistent negative thinking can contribute to certain disorders.

## 6. Stigma and Mental Health

### 6.1 Meaning of Stigma

Stigma refers to negative stereotypes, prejudice and discrimination directed towards people because of their psychological difficulties.

### 6.2 Consequences of Stigma

Stigma may discourage people from seeking professional support and can contribute to social isolation.

### 6.3 Reducing Stigma

- Use respectful and non-judgemental language.
- Recognise that psychological disorders are not character weaknesses.
- Encourage evidence-based treatment.
- Promote mental health education.
- Respect privacy and individual dignity.

## 7. Summary

Abnormal psychology studies patterns of behaviour and experience associated with significant distress or impairment. Modern explanations emphasise biological, psychological and social influences. Classification systems support diagnosis and research, while understanding stigma encourages respectful treatment of individuals experiencing psychological difficulties.
