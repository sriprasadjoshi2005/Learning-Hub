# Lesson 36: Personality Disorders

Personality disorders are enduring patterns of inner experience and behaviour that differ significantly from cultural expectations and create difficulties in relationships, emotional functioning and everyday life. This lesson examines their characteristics, major categories, possible causes and treatment approaches.

## 1. Understanding Personality Disorders

### 1.1 Meaning of Personality

Personality refers to relatively consistent patterns of thinking, feeling and behaving that distinguish individuals.

Personality develops through interactions between biological influences, experiences and social environments.

### 1.2 Definition of Personality Disorders

A personality disorder involves an enduring and inflexible pattern of inner experience and behaviour that:

- Deviates significantly from cultural expectations.
- Appears across multiple situations.
- Begins by adolescence or early adulthood.
- Remains relatively stable over time.
- Causes distress or impairment.

### 1.3 Personality Traits and Personality Disorders

Having a particular personality trait does not automatically indicate a disorder.

For example, being reserved does not mean a person has avoidant personality disorder.

A clinical diagnosis requires a persistent pattern that significantly affects functioning.

## 2. Classification of Personality Disorders

Personality disorders are traditionally grouped into three clusters in the DSM-5-TR.

### 2.1 Cluster A: Odd or Eccentric Patterns

This cluster includes:

- Paranoid personality disorder.
- Schizoid personality disorder.
- Schizotypal personality disorder.

### 2.2 Cluster B: Dramatic, Emotional or Erratic Patterns

This cluster includes:

- Antisocial personality disorder.
- Borderline personality disorder.
- Histrionic personality disorder.
- Narcissistic personality disorder.

### 2.3 Cluster C: Anxious or Fearful Patterns

This cluster includes:

- Avoidant personality disorder.
- Dependent personality disorder.
- Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder.

## 3. Cluster A Personality Disorders

### 3.1 Paranoid Personality Disorder

This disorder involves a pervasive pattern of distrust and suspiciousness.

Individuals may frequently interpret other people's actions as threatening or harmful.

### 3.2 Schizoid Personality Disorder

This disorder involves a persistent pattern of detachment from social relationships and limited emotional expression.

Individuals may prefer solitary activities and show little interest in close relationships.

### 3.3 Schizotypal Personality Disorder

This disorder involves social and interpersonal difficulties accompanied by unusual patterns of thinking, perception and behaviour.

Possible features include:

- Unusual beliefs.
- Eccentric behaviour.
- Unusual perceptual experiences.
- Social anxiety.
- Suspiciousness.

## 4. Cluster B Personality Disorders

### 4.1 Antisocial Personality Disorder

Antisocial personality disorder involves a persistent pattern of disregard for and violation of the rights of others.

Possible features include:

- Repeated disregard for social rules.
- Deceitfulness.
- Impulsivity.
- Irritability and aggression.
- Lack of consistent responsibility.
- Lack of remorse.

Diagnosis requires a history of conduct disorder symptoms before age 15, along with other diagnostic requirements.

### 4.2 Borderline Personality Disorder

Borderline personality disorder involves instability in emotions, relationships, self-image and behaviour.

Possible features include:

- Intense emotional changes.
- Fear of abandonment.
- Unstable relationships.
- Impulsive behaviour.
- Disturbances in identity.
- Recurrent self-harming behaviour or suicidal thoughts.

### 4.3 Histrionic Personality Disorder

This disorder involves a pervasive pattern of excessive emotionality and attention-seeking behaviour.

Individuals may feel uncomfortable when they are not the centre of attention.

### 4.4 Narcissistic Personality Disorder

Narcissistic personality disorder involves a persistent pattern of grandiosity, need for admiration and difficulties with empathy.

Possible features include:

- Exaggerated sense of self-importance.
- Strong need for recognition.
- Sense of entitlement.
- Difficulty recognising others' feelings.
- Sensitivity to criticism.

## 5. Cluster C Personality Disorders

### 5.1 Avoidant Personality Disorder

This disorder involves social inhibition, feelings of inadequacy and sensitivity to negative evaluation.

Individuals may avoid relationships or activities because of fear of rejection or criticism.

### 5.2 Dependent Personality Disorder

Dependent personality disorder involves an excessive need to be cared for, leading to submissive and dependent behaviour.

Individuals may struggle to make everyday decisions without reassurance.

### 5.3 Obsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder

Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (OCPD) involves a persistent pattern of perfectionism, orderliness and control.

Possible features include:

- Excessive attention to details.
- Rigid rules and routines.
- Difficulty delegating tasks.
- Excessive devotion to work.
- Perfectionism that interferes with completing tasks.

OCPD is different from obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), which involves obsessions, compulsions or both.

## 6. Possible Causes and Risk Factors

### 6.1 Genetic Influences

Inherited characteristics may contribute to personality traits and vulnerability to certain personality disorders.

### 6.2 Early Developmental Experiences

Childhood experiences, family relationships and early social environments may influence personality development.

### 6.3 Environmental Influences

Experiences such as chronic stress, neglect, trauma and unstable relationships may contribute to difficulties in personality development.

### 6.4 Biological Factors

Differences in temperament, emotional regulation and brain functioning may contribute to certain personality patterns.

Personality disorders generally arise through complex interactions rather than one isolated cause.

## 7. Diagnosis and Assessment

### 7.1 Clinical Evaluation

Assessment involves examining long-term patterns of behaviour, emotional responses, relationships and self-perception.

### 7.2 Diagnostic Considerations

Professionals consider:

- Duration and stability of symptoms.
- Cultural and social context.
- Effects on everyday functioning.
- Developmental history.
- Other possible psychological or medical explanations.

### 7.3 Avoiding Misdiagnosis

Temporary emotional difficulties, unusual personality traits and isolated behaviours should not be treated as sufficient evidence of a personality disorder.

## 8. Treatment Approaches

### 8.1 Psychotherapy

Psychotherapy is a major treatment approach for personality disorders.

It may help individuals develop:

- Emotional regulation.
- Healthier relationship patterns.
- Better communication.
- More flexible thinking.
- Improved coping strategies.

### 8.2 Dialectical Behaviour Therapy

Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) was originally developed to help individuals with borderline personality disorder.

It focuses on four major skill areas:

1. Mindfulness.
2. Distress tolerance.
3. Emotion regulation.
4. Interpersonal effectiveness.

### 8.3 Cognitive Behavioural Therapy

CBT may help individuals identify unhelpful beliefs and behavioural patterns and develop healthier alternatives.

### 8.4 Medication

Medication may be prescribed to address specific symptoms or co-occurring conditions.

There is no single medication that cures personality disorders.

## 9. Social Understanding and Stigma

Personality disorders are frequently misunderstood and can be associated with harmful stereotypes.

A diagnosis does not define an individual's entire identity or determine their ability to develop healthier patterns.

Respectful communication, professional support and individualised treatment are important.

## 10. Summary

Personality disorders involve enduring and inflexible patterns of behaviour, emotion and thinking that cause significant difficulties. The DSM-5-TR groups ten traditional personality disorders into Clusters A, B and C. Their development may involve biological, psychological and environmental factors. Psychotherapy, including approaches such as DBT and CBT, can help improve functioning.
