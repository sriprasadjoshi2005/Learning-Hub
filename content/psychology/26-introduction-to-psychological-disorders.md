# Lesson 26: Introduction to Psychological Disorders

Psychological disorders are patterns of thoughts, emotions or behaviours associated with significant distress, impairment or disruption in important areas of functioning. Abnormal psychology studies these conditions, their causes, classifications, development and treatment. Understanding psychological disorders requires scientific evidence, cultural awareness and careful consideration of individual experiences.

## 1. Meaning of Psychological Disorders

A psychological disorder is a clinically significant disturbance in cognition, emotional regulation or behaviour that reflects dysfunction in psychological, biological or developmental processes.

The presence of unusual behaviour alone does not establish a disorder.

Assessment considers:
- Distress.
- Functional impairment.
- Duration.
- Severity.
- Context.
- Developmental expectations.
- Cultural background.

## 2. Normality and Abnormality

Distinguishing normal psychological variation from a disorder can be difficult.

Several approaches are used to understand abnormality.

### 2.1 Statistical Rarity

A characteristic may be considered unusual because it occurs infrequently.

However, rarity alone does not establish psychological disorder.

### 2.2 Violation of Social Norms

Behaviour may be considered abnormal when it strongly conflicts with social expectations.

However, social norms vary across cultures and historical periods.

### 2.3 Personal Distress

Significant emotional suffering can be an important indicator of psychological difficulty.

However, not every disorder produces obvious subjective distress.

### 2.4 Functional Impairment

A condition may interfere with relationships, education, employment or everyday responsibilities.

### 2.5 Harm and Risk

Potential harm to oneself or others may require urgent assessment.

No single criterion is sufficient in every situation.

## 3. Historical Approaches to Mental Illness

Explanations of mental illness have changed over time.

### 3.1 Supernatural Explanations

Historically, some societies interpreted mental illness through supernatural or spiritual explanations.

These approaches often lacked scientific understanding and sometimes resulted in harmful treatment.

### 3.2 Biological Approaches

Biological explanations examine the roles of genetics, brain functioning, hormones and physiological processes.

### 3.3 Psychological Approaches

Psychological approaches examine learning, cognition, emotion, personality and developmental experiences.

### 3.4 Social and Cultural Approaches

These approaches examine relationships, social conditions, cultural expectations and environmental stressors.

Modern approaches recognise that multiple influences may interact.

## 4. Models of Psychological Disorders

### 4.1 Biological Model

The biological model examines factors such as:
- Genetic vulnerability.
- Brain structure and functioning.
- Neurotransmitter systems.
- Hormonal regulation.
- Medical conditions.

Biological explanations can contribute to understanding disorders but do not explain every individual experience.

### 4.2 Psychodynamic Model

The psychodynamic model examines unconscious processes, internal conflicts and early relationships.

It is historically influential, although many classical claims are difficult to test scientifically.

### 4.3 Behavioural Model

The behavioural model explains certain maladaptive patterns through learning processes.

These may include:
- Classical conditioning.
- Operant conditioning.
- Observational learning.

### 4.4 Cognitive Model

The cognitive model examines how patterns of thinking, interpretation and belief influence emotional and behavioural difficulties.

### 4.5 Humanistic Model

The humanistic model emphasises personal experience, self-concept, growth and the need for acceptance.

### 4.6 Sociocultural Model

The sociocultural model examines how social relationships, culture, discrimination, poverty and other environmental factors influence psychological well-being.

## 5. The Diathesis-Stress Model

The diathesis-stress model explains psychological disorders through interactions between vulnerability and stressful experiences.

**Diathesis:** A predisposition or vulnerability.

**Stress:** Environmental or psychological demands that may contribute to the development of difficulties.

Example:

An individual with a genetic vulnerability may experience increased risk of a disorder following prolonged stress.

Vulnerability does not make the development of a disorder inevitable.

## 6. Biopsychosocial Understanding

The biopsychosocial perspective combines biological, psychological and social influences.

### Biological Influences

- Genetic factors.
- Nervous system functioning.
- Physical health.
- Hormonal processes.

### Psychological Influences

- Thinking patterns.
- Emotional regulation.
- Coping.
- Learning history.
- Personality.

### Social Influences

- Family relationships.
- Social support.
- Cultural expectations.
- Economic conditions.
- Trauma and adversity.

This integrated perspective recognises that psychological disorders rarely have one simple cause.

## 7. Classification of Psychological Disorders

Classification systems organise psychological conditions into categories based on clinical features.

Two major classification systems are:

### 7.1 DSM-5-TR

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision, is published by the American Psychiatric Association.

It provides diagnostic criteria and descriptions for mental disorders.

### 7.2 ICD-11

The International Classification of Diseases, Eleventh Revision, is published by the World Health Organization.

It includes mental, behavioural and neurodevelopmental disorders within a broader international health classification system.

These systems support communication, research and clinical practice, but diagnosis still requires professional judgement.

## 8. Assessment and Diagnosis

Psychological assessment gathers information to understand an individual's difficulties.

Methods may include:

- Clinical interviews.
- Behavioural observation.
- Psychological tests.
- Medical evaluation.
- Developmental history.
- Information about daily functioning.

### 8.1 Clinical Interview

A clinical interview explores symptoms, history, circumstances and relevant experiences.

### 8.2 Mental Status Examination

A mental status examination evaluates current psychological functioning.

It commonly examines:
- Appearance and behaviour.
- Speech.
- Mood and affect.
- Thought processes.
- Thought content.
- Perception.
- Cognition.
- Insight and judgement.

### 8.3 Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnosis involves distinguishing between conditions that may produce similar symptoms.

Physical illnesses, medications and substance effects may also contribute to psychological symptoms.

## 9. Stigma and Mental Health

Stigma involves negative attitudes, stereotypes and discriminatory treatment associated with mental health conditions.

It may lead to:
- Social exclusion.
- Delayed help-seeking.
- Reduced employment opportunities.
- Shame.
- Isolation.

Mental health education, respectful communication and accessible services can help reduce stigma.

## 10. Ethical Principles in Clinical Psychology

Ethical practice requires:

**Confidentiality:** Protecting personal information.

**Informed consent:** Explaining procedures and obtaining voluntary agreement.

**Beneficence:** Promoting the welfare of the client.

**Non-maleficence:** Avoiding unnecessary harm.

**Professional competence:** Working within appropriate training and qualifications.

**Respect for autonomy:** Recognising the individual's right to participate in decisions.

## 11. Prevention and Early Intervention

Psychological difficulties may be addressed at different stages.

### 11.1 Primary Prevention

Aims to reduce risk before a disorder develops.

Examples include mental health education and supportive community environments.

### 11.2 Secondary Prevention

Focuses on early identification and intervention.

### 11.3 Tertiary Prevention

Aims to reduce the long-term impact of an established disorder and support recovery.

## 12. Summary

- Psychological disorders involve clinically significant disturbances in psychological functioning.
- Abnormality cannot be determined through statistical rarity alone.
- Biological, psychological and social approaches explain different aspects of disorders.
- The diathesis-stress model examines vulnerability interacting with stress.
- The biopsychosocial perspective integrates multiple influences.
- DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 are major classification systems.
- Diagnosis involves interviews, observation and appropriate assessment.
- Differential diagnosis helps distinguish conditions with similar symptoms.
- Stigma can discourage help-seeking and contribute to discrimination.
- Ethical practice requires confidentiality, informed consent and professional competence.
- Prevention includes primary, secondary and tertiary approaches.
