# Lesson 46: Consciousness and Advanced Cognitive Processes

Consciousness is one of the most complex subjects in psychology and cognitive science. It concerns subjective experience, awareness and the ability to process information about oneself and the surrounding world. This lesson examines major theories of consciousness, metacognition, decision-making and advanced cognitive processes.

## 1. Understanding Consciousness

### 1.1 Definition

Consciousness refers broadly to subjective awareness of internal experiences and external surroundings.

It includes awareness of:

- Thoughts.
- Emotions.
- Sensations.
- Memories.
- Perceptions.
- The sense of self.

### 1.2 Subjective Experience

Subjective experience refers to the personal, first-person quality of conscious experience.

For example, seeing a colour involves more than processing light. It also involves the subjective experience of seeing that colour.

### 1.3 Levels of Consciousness

Conscious states vary in their degree and type of awareness.

Examples include:

- Ordinary waking consciousness.
- Focused attention.
- Daydreaming.
- Sleep.
- Dreaming.
- Meditative states.

## 2. Conscious and Unconscious Processing

### 2.1 Conscious Processing

Conscious processing involves information that is available to awareness.

It often requires attention and may support deliberate reasoning.

### 2.2 Unconscious Processing

Unconscious processing refers to mental processes that occur without direct awareness.

Examples include certain automatic perceptual processes and learned motor responses.

### 2.3 Automatic and Controlled Processing

Automatic processing is relatively fast and requires limited conscious effort.

Controlled processing requires greater attention and deliberate effort.

### 2.4 Dual-Process Theories

Dual-process theories distinguish between two broad styles of thinking.

**Type 1 processing:** Fast, automatic and often intuitive.

**Type 2 processing:** Slower, deliberate and more analytical.

These categories describe broad patterns rather than two completely separate brain systems.

## 3. Theories of Consciousness

### 3.1 Global Workspace Theory

Global workspace theory proposes that information becomes consciously accessible when it is widely distributed across multiple cognitive systems.

Information selected for this global availability can influence memory, reasoning and decision-making.

### 3.2 Global Neuronal Workspace Theory

The global neuronal workspace theory develops this idea through neural mechanisms.

It proposes that conscious access involves widespread communication across distributed brain networks.

### 3.3 Integrated Information Theory

Integrated information theory attempts to explain consciousness through the integration and differentiation of information within a system.

It proposes that consciousness is related to the degree and structure of integrated information.

The theory remains scientifically debated.

### 3.4 Higher-Order Theories

Higher-order theories propose that a mental state becomes conscious when the individual has an appropriate higher-order representation or awareness of that state.

### 3.5 Recurrent Processing Theory

Recurrent processing theories emphasise feedback interactions between different levels of neural processing as important for conscious perception.

## 4. Attention and Conscious Awareness

### 4.1 Selective Attention

Selective attention involves focusing on particular information while ignoring competing information.

### 4.2 Divided Attention

Divided attention involves attempting to process multiple tasks or information sources simultaneously.

Performance may decline when tasks compete for limited cognitive resources.

### 4.3 Inattentional Blindness

Inattentional blindness occurs when an individual fails to notice an unexpected stimulus because attention is focused elsewhere.

### 4.4 Change Blindness

Change blindness occurs when an individual fails to detect changes in a visual scene.

It demonstrates that visual awareness does not involve complete and continuous representation of every environmental detail.

## 5. Metacognition

### 5.1 Definition

Metacognition refers to thinking about and monitoring one's own cognitive processes.

It involves awareness and regulation of learning, memory, reasoning and problem-solving.

### 5.2 Metacognitive Knowledge

Metacognitive knowledge involves understanding one's cognitive abilities and strategies.

It includes:

- Knowledge about oneself as a learner.
- Knowledge about tasks.
- Knowledge about strategies.

### 5.3 Metacognitive Regulation

Metacognitive regulation involves controlling cognitive activities.

Major processes include:

1. Planning.
2. Monitoring.
3. Evaluating.

### 5.4 Example

A person preparing for an examination may recognise that they do not understand a concept, change their study strategy and later test their understanding.

This is an example of metacognitive regulation.

## 6. Decision-Making and Judgement

### 6.1 Meaning of Decision-Making

Decision-making is the process of selecting an option from available alternatives.

It may involve evaluating risks, benefits, probabilities and personal goals.

### 6.2 Rational Decision-Making

Rational decision-making attempts to compare alternatives systematically and select an option that best satisfies defined objectives.

### 6.3 Bounded Rationality

Herbert Simon proposed bounded rationality.

It recognises that human decisions are limited by:

- Available information.
- Time constraints.
- Cognitive capacity.
- Environmental uncertainty.

### 6.4 Satisficing

Satisficing involves selecting an option that meets acceptable requirements rather than searching indefinitely for the theoretically best option.

### 6.5 Decision Biases

Decision-making may be influenced by:

- Confirmation bias.
- Anchoring bias.
- Availability heuristic.
- Overconfidence.
- Loss aversion.

## 7. Problem-Solving and Creative Thinking

### 7.1 Problem Representation

Problem-solving begins with understanding and representing the problem.

An incorrect representation can lead to ineffective solutions.

### 7.2 Algorithms

An algorithm is a systematic procedure that guarantees a solution when correctly applied to a problem within its defined conditions.

### 7.3 Heuristics

Heuristics are practical strategies that simplify problem-solving.

They can save time but do not always guarantee correct solutions.

### 7.4 Insight

Insight refers to a sudden understanding of a problem or its solution.

It may occur after a period of unsuccessful attempts or restructuring of the problem.

### 7.5 Creative Thinking

Creative thinking involves producing ideas that are both original and appropriate or useful.

It may involve:

- Divergent thinking.
- Associative thinking.
- Reframing.
- Conceptual combination.

## 8. Self-Awareness and the Sense of Self

### 8.1 Self-Awareness

Self-awareness involves recognising oneself as an individual with internal experiences, characteristics and relationships.

### 8.2 Self-Concept

Self-concept refers to the beliefs and perceptions an individual has about themselves.

### 8.3 Self-Reflection

Self-reflection involves examining personal thoughts, feelings, motivations and behaviour.

### 8.4 Metacognition and Self-Awareness

Metacognition focuses on monitoring cognitive processes, while self-awareness is broader and includes awareness of emotions, identity and personal experiences.

## 9. Consciousness and Artificial Intelligence

### 9.1 Information Processing

Artificial intelligence systems can process information, recognise patterns and generate responses.

### 9.2 Functional Behaviour and Subjective Experience

The ability to perform intelligent tasks does not by itself establish that a system has subjective conscious experience.

### 9.3 The Hard Problem of Consciousness

The hard problem of consciousness, a term associated with philosopher David Chalmers, concerns why and how physical processes are accompanied by subjective experience.

### 9.4 Scientific Challenges

Research into consciousness faces challenges because subjective experience cannot be directly observed from the outside.

Researchers therefore use behavioural reports, neural measurements and theoretical models.

## 10. Applications

Understanding consciousness and advanced cognition contributes to:

- Education and learning strategies.
- Human-computer interaction.
- Decision-making research.
- Cognitive rehabilitation.
- Artificial intelligence research.
- Attention and performance studies.

## 11. Summary

Consciousness involves subjective awareness of internal and external experiences. Theories such as global workspace theory, integrated information theory and higher-order theories attempt to explain how conscious experience arises. Metacognition supports monitoring and regulation of thinking, while decision-making and problem-solving demonstrate how cognitive systems operate under limitations.
