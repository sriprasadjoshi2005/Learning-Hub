# Lesson 11: Motivation and Human Needs

Motivation is the psychological process that initiates, directs and sustains goal-oriented behaviour. It explains why individuals act, how they choose between alternatives and what keeps them working towards particular objectives. Motivation is influenced by biological needs, psychological desires, personal goals and environmental conditions.

## 1. Meaning and Characteristics of Motivation

Motivation refers to the internal and external processes that activate, direct and maintain behaviour.

Examples include:
- Studying to achieve a particular academic goal.
- Eating in response to hunger.
- Practising a musical instrument to improve performance.
- Helping another person because of empathy.
- Working to obtain financial security.

Important characteristics include:

- **Activation:** Initiating behaviour.
- **Direction:** Selecting a particular goal or activity.
- **Intensity:** Determining the amount of effort invested.
- **Persistence:** Maintaining effort over time.

## 2. Types of Motivation

### 2.1 Intrinsic Motivation

Intrinsic motivation occurs when an individual performs an activity because it is personally interesting, enjoyable or satisfying.

Examples:
- Reading about astronomy out of curiosity.
- Solving mathematical problems for enjoyment.
- Learning a language because of personal interest.

Intrinsic motivation is associated with personal satisfaction and engagement.

### 2.2 Extrinsic Motivation

Extrinsic motivation occurs when behaviour is influenced by external outcomes or consequences.

Examples:
- Studying to receive a scholarship.
- Working to earn money.
- Completing an assignment to avoid a penalty.

Extrinsic motivation does not necessarily reduce interest. External rewards can sometimes support engagement, depending on how they are introduced.

### 2.3 Primary and Secondary Motives

**Primary motives:** Biologically based motives such as hunger, thirst and the need for sleep.

**Secondary motives:** Motives developed through learning and social experience, such as achievement, recognition, financial success and social approval.

## 3. Biological and Psychological Needs

Motivation can arise from biological requirements and psychological needs.

### 3.1 Biological Needs

Biological needs are connected to survival and physiological functioning.

Examples include:
- Food.
- Water.
- Sleep.
- Temperature regulation.
- Avoidance of physical danger.

### 3.2 Psychological Needs

Psychological needs involve mental and social well-being.

Examples include:
- Autonomy.
- Competence.
- Belonging.
- Recognition.
- Personal growth.

These needs can influence behaviour even when basic biological requirements are satisfied.

## 4. Instinct and Drive Theories

### 4.1 Instinct Theory

Instinct theory historically explained some behaviours as biologically inherited patterns of action.

Examples of instinct-like behaviour include certain defensive responses and species-specific behaviours.

However, complex human motivation cannot generally be explained through instinct alone.

### 4.2 Drive-Reduction Theory

Drive-reduction theory, associated with Clark Hull, proposes that physiological needs create internal states of tension called drives.

Individuals are motivated to perform behaviours that reduce these drives.

Example:

1. The body requires water.
2. Thirst develops.
3. The individual searches for water.
4. Drinking reduces thirst.

The theory helps explain biological motivation but does not fully explain curiosity, exploration or behaviours that increase stimulation.

## 5. Arousal Theory

Arousal theory proposes that individuals are motivated to maintain or achieve an appropriate level of physiological and psychological alertness.

People may seek stimulation when under-stimulated and reduce stimulation when overwhelmed.

Example:

An individual who feels bored may seek an interesting activity, while someone experiencing excessive stress may seek a quiet environment.

The optimal level of arousal varies depending on the individual and task.

## 6. Incentive Theory

Incentive theory explains motivation through external rewards and consequences.

An incentive is an external object or outcome that attracts or discourages behaviour.

Examples:
- Money.
- Recognition.
- Awards.
- Social approval.
- Avoidance of penalties.

Incentives interact with internal needs and personal expectations.

## 7. Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

Abraham Maslow proposed a theory in which human needs are arranged in a hierarchy.

### 7.1 Physiological Needs

These are basic biological requirements.

Examples include food, water, sleep and shelter.

### 7.2 Safety Needs

These involve security, stability and protection from danger.

### 7.3 Love and Belonging

These include friendship, family relationships, affection and social connection.

### 7.4 Esteem Needs

These involve self-respect, competence, achievement and recognition.

### 7.5 Self-Actualisation

Self-actualisation refers to developing one's abilities and pursuing personal potential.

Maslow's hierarchy is influential, but human needs do not always follow a rigid sequence. Individuals may pursue higher-level goals even when some basic needs remain unmet.

## 8. Self-Determination Theory

Self-determination theory, developed by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, examines how psychological needs influence motivation and well-being.

It identifies three fundamental psychological needs.

### 8.1 Autonomy

Autonomy refers to experiencing a sense of choice and personal control over one's actions.

### 8.2 Competence

Competence refers to feeling capable of successfully performing activities and developing skills.

### 8.3 Relatedness

Relatedness refers to feeling connected to and accepted by other people.

When these needs are supported, intrinsic motivation and psychological well-being may improve.

## 9. Achievement Motivation

Achievement motivation refers to the desire to accomplish challenging goals and meet standards of excellence.

Individuals with strong achievement motivation may:

- Prefer challenging but achievable tasks.
- Seek feedback.
- Take responsibility for outcomes.
- Persist after difficulties.
- Evaluate their progress.

Achievement motivation can be influenced by personality, previous experiences, expectations and social environments.

## 10. Motivation and Goal Setting

Goals provide direction for behaviour.

Effective goals are often:

- Specific.
- Measurable.
- Achievable.
- Relevant.
- Time-bound.

### 10.1 Goal-Setting Theory

Edwin Locke and Gary Latham developed goal-setting theory.

The theory proposes that specific and challenging goals can improve performance when individuals are committed to them and receive appropriate feedback.

### 10.2 Self-Efficacy

Self-efficacy, a concept developed by Albert Bandura, refers to an individual's belief in their ability to perform a particular task.

Higher self-efficacy can influence:
- Willingness to attempt difficult tasks.
- Persistence.
- Effort.
- Response to failure.

Self-efficacy is task-specific and is not identical to general self-confidence.

## 11. Motivation and Emotion

Motivation and emotion influence one another.

For example:
- Fear may motivate an individual to avoid danger.
- Curiosity may encourage exploration.
- Frustration may motivate problem-solving.
- Pride may encourage continued effort.

Emotions can influence the direction and intensity of motivated behaviour.

## 12. Applications of Motivation

### Education

Teachers can support motivation through meaningful goals, constructive feedback and opportunities for independent learning.

### Workplace

Organisations can encourage motivation through recognition, autonomy, suitable responsibilities and fair rewards.

### Sports

Athletes use goal-setting, self-efficacy and achievement motivation to improve performance.

### Personal Development

Understanding motivation can help individuals establish habits and maintain progress towards long-term goals.

## 13. Summary

- Motivation activates, directs and sustains behaviour.
- Intrinsic motivation arises from internal interest, while extrinsic motivation involves external outcomes.
- Biological and psychological needs influence motivation.
- Drive-reduction theory explains behaviour through the reduction of physiological tension.
- Arousal and incentive theories explain additional aspects of motivation.
- Maslow proposed a hierarchy of human needs.
- Self-determination theory identifies autonomy, competence and relatedness.
- Achievement motivation concerns the pursuit of challenging goals.
- Self-efficacy influences effort and persistence.
- Goal-setting can improve performance when goals are appropriately designed.
