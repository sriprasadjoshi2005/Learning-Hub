# Lesson 14: Human Development Across the Lifespan

Developmental psychology studies how individuals change throughout life. It investigates physical growth, cognitive abilities, emotional experiences, personality and social relationships from conception through old age. Development is influenced by biological maturation, learning, family, culture and environmental experiences.

## 1. Meaning of Human Development

Human development refers to systematic changes in physical, cognitive, emotional and social functioning across the lifespan.

It includes:
- Physical development.
- Cognitive development.
- Language development.
- Emotional development.
- Social development.
- Moral development.

Development does not stop at childhood. Individuals continue to change throughout adulthood and old age.

## 2. Principles of Development

### 2.1 Development is Lifelong

Development occurs from conception through old age.

### 2.2 Development is Multidimensional

It involves physical, cognitive, emotional and social changes.

### 2.3 Development is Multidirectional

Some abilities improve while others may decline.

For example, vocabulary may increase with age while certain aspects of processing speed may decrease.

### 2.4 Development Shows Individual Differences

People develop at different rates because of biological and environmental influences.

### 2.5 Development Involves Continuity and Change

Some developmental characteristics change gradually, while others involve more noticeable transitions.

## 3. Major Issues in Developmental Psychology

### 3.1 Nature and Nurture

Nature refers to genetic and biological influences.

Nurture refers to environmental experiences, including family, education and culture.

Development results from interactions between these influences.

### 3.2 Continuity and Discontinuity

Continuity describes gradual changes in development.

Discontinuity describes development through distinct stages or transitions.

Different developmental processes may show different patterns.

### 3.3 Stability and Change

Some individual characteristics remain relatively stable, while others change through experience and maturation.

## 4. Prenatal Development

Prenatal development occurs before birth.

It is commonly divided into three stages.

### 4.1 Germinal Stage

This stage begins at fertilisation and continues through the early development of the fertilised egg.

Important events include:
- Cell division.
- Formation of the blastocyst.
- Implantation in the uterine wall.

### 4.2 Embryonic Stage

This stage involves the development of major organs and basic body structures.

It is a particularly sensitive period for certain developmental disruptions.

### 4.3 Fetal Stage

The fetal stage involves continued growth and maturation of organs and body systems until birth.

### 4.4 Prenatal Influences

Development may be influenced by:
- Maternal nutrition.
- Maternal health.
- Infections.
- Certain medications and substances.
- Environmental toxins.
- Genetic conditions.

Teratogens are environmental agents that can interfere with prenatal development.

Their effects depend on the substance, timing, dose and other biological factors.

## 5. Infancy and Early Childhood

Infancy and early childhood involve rapid physical, cognitive and social development.

Important developments include:
- Motor coordination.
- Language acquisition.
- Attachment.
- Recognition of familiar individuals.
- Exploration.
- Development of basic self-regulation.

### 5.1 Attachment

Attachment refers to an enduring emotional bond between an infant and a caregiver.

John Bowlby proposed that attachment has important biological and developmental functions.

Mary Ainsworth's research contributed to the study of attachment patterns.

Commonly described patterns include:

- Secure attachment.
- Insecure-avoidant attachment.
- Insecure-resistant or ambivalent attachment.
- Disorganised attachment.

Attachment patterns are influenced by caregiving and other factors. They should not be treated as permanent labels for a child or caregiver.

## 6. Piaget's Theory of Cognitive Development

Jean Piaget proposed that children actively construct knowledge through interaction with their environment.

He described four major stages.

### 6.1 Sensorimotor Stage

Approximate age: Birth to 2 years.

Characteristics:
- Learning through sensory and motor experiences.
- Development of object permanence.
- Increasing coordination of actions.

**Object permanence** refers to understanding that objects continue to exist even when they cannot be seen.

### 6.2 Preoperational Stage

Approximate age: 2 to 7 years.

Characteristics:
- Development of symbolic thought.
- Increasing language ability.
- Egocentrism.
- Difficulty with certain logical operations.

Egocentrism refers to difficulty understanding perspectives different from one's own.

### 6.3 Concrete Operational Stage

Approximate age: 7 to 11 years.

Characteristics:
- Development of logical thinking about concrete situations.
- Understanding conservation.
- Classification.
- Seriation.

Conservation refers to understanding that certain quantities remain unchanged despite changes in appearance.

### 6.4 Formal Operational Stage

Approximate age: 11 years onwards.

Characteristics:
- Abstract reasoning.
- Hypothetical thinking.
- Systematic problem-solving.
- Consideration of possibilities.

Not every individual demonstrates all formal reasoning abilities consistently across every context.

## 7. Vygotsky's Sociocultural Theory

Lev Vygotsky emphasised the importance of social interaction and culture in cognitive development.

### 7.1 Zone of Proximal Development

The zone of proximal development (ZPD) is the range between what a learner can accomplish independently and what they can accomplish with suitable assistance.

### 7.2 Scaffolding

Scaffolding involves providing temporary support that helps a learner perform a task they cannot yet complete independently.

The support is gradually reduced as competence develops.

### 7.3 Language and Thought

Vygotsky considered language an important tool for cognitive development.

Social communication can gradually become internalised as inner speech and self-guided thinking.

## 8. Erikson's Psychosocial Development Theory

Erik Erikson proposed eight psychosocial stages across the lifespan.

Each stage involves a developmental challenge.

| Stage | Approximate period | Central conflict |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Infancy | Trust vs. mistrust |
| 2 | Early childhood | Autonomy vs. shame and doubt |
| 3 | Preschool years | Initiative vs. guilt |
| 4 | School age | Industry vs. inferiority |
| 5 | Adolescence | Identity vs. role confusion |
| 6 | Young adulthood | Intimacy vs. isolation |
| 7 | Middle adulthood | Generativity vs. stagnation |
| 8 | Late adulthood | Integrity vs. despair |

These stages provide a framework for understanding psychosocial development, but real-life development does not always follow a fixed sequence or age boundary.

## 9. Adolescence

Adolescence is a developmental period involving transitions between childhood and adulthood.

Major changes include:

- Puberty.
- Physical growth.
- Development of abstract thinking.
- Identity exploration.
- Increasing independence.
- Changes in peer relationships.
- Emotional development.

### 9.1 Identity Development

Adolescents explore personal values, beliefs, interests and social roles.

Erikson associated this period with identity versus role confusion.

## 10. Adulthood

Adulthood involves continued psychological, social and biological development.

### 10.1 Early Adulthood

Common developmental concerns include:
- Building relationships.
- Career development.
- Independence.
- Personal identity.
- Long-term commitments.

### 10.2 Middle Adulthood

Development may involve:
- Family responsibilities.
- Career changes.
- Community participation.
- Reflection on personal goals.
- Supporting younger and older generations.

### 10.3 Late Adulthood

Older adulthood may involve:
- Retirement.
- Changes in physical functioning.
- Adjustment to changing social roles.
- Reflection on life experiences.
- Maintaining relationships and independence.

Ageing experiences differ greatly among individuals.

## 11. Cognitive Development in Adulthood and Ageing

Cognitive abilities do not all change in the same way.

Some aspects of cognition, such as accumulated knowledge and vocabulary, may remain stable or improve through adulthood.

Other abilities, including certain aspects of processing speed and working memory, may decline with age.

Cognitive health is influenced by:
- Physical health.
- Education.
- Social engagement.
- Sleep.
- Physical activity.
- Environmental conditions.

Normal ageing should be distinguished from neurocognitive disorders.

## 12. Summary

- Developmental psychology examines changes across the lifespan.
- Development is lifelong, multidimensional and influenced by biological and environmental factors.
- Prenatal development includes germinal, embryonic and fetal stages.
- Attachment contributes to early social and emotional development.
- Piaget proposed four stages of cognitive development.
- Vygotsky emphasised social interaction and the zone of proximal development.
- Erikson proposed eight psychosocial stages.
- Adolescence involves physical, cognitive and identity-related changes.
- Adulthood includes continued psychological and social development.
- Cognitive abilities may change differently during ageing.
