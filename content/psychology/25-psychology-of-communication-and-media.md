# Lesson 25: Psychology of Communication and Media

The psychology of communication and media examines how information is created, transmitted, interpreted and influenced by social and technological environments. It investigates persuasion, media effects, advertising, digital communication, misinformation and the psychological processes involved in consuming information.

## 1. Meaning of Communication Psychology

Communication psychology studies the mental and behavioural processes involved in exchanging information.

It examines:
- Message interpretation.
- Persuasion.
- Social interaction.
- Media consumption.
- Emotional communication.
- Digital behaviour.
- Information processing.

## 2. Communication Process

Communication generally involves several components.

### 2.1 Sender

The individual or organisation that creates and transmits a message.

### 2.2 Message

The information, idea or feeling being communicated.

### 2.3 Channel

The medium through which the message is transmitted.

Examples include speech, writing, television and digital platforms.

### 2.4 Receiver

The individual or audience that receives and interprets the message.

### 2.5 Feedback

The receiver's response to the message.

### 2.6 Noise

Anything that interferes with accurate communication.

Noise may be:
- Physical.
- Psychological.
- Semantic.
- Technological.

## 3. Communication Barriers

Communication can become ineffective because of several barriers.

**Physical barriers:** Distance, background noise or poor equipment.

**Psychological barriers:** Anxiety, prejudice or lack of attention.

**Semantic barriers:** Differences in language or interpretation.

**Cultural barriers:** Differences in social expectations and communication norms.

**Technological barriers:** Network failures or limitations of digital systems.

## 4. Mass Media and Human Behaviour

Mass media distributes information to large audiences.

Examples include:
- Television.
- Newspapers.
- Radio.
- Films.
- Social media.
- Online news platforms.

Media can influence attitudes, beliefs, emotional responses and social expectations.

However, media effects depend on content, audience characteristics, context and previous beliefs.

## 5. Agenda-Setting Theory

Agenda-setting theory examines how media attention can influence which issues people consider important.

The theory suggests that media may influence the perceived importance of topics through the frequency and prominence of coverage.

It does not necessarily mean that media directly determines what individuals think about an issue.

## 6. Framing Theory

Framing concerns how information is presented and organised.

Different presentations of the same issue can influence interpretation.

Example:

A news report may describe an economic change through its potential benefits or through the costs it creates.

The underlying event may remain the same, but the framing influences which aspects receive attention.

## 7. Cultivation Theory

George Gerbner developed cultivation theory to examine the long-term influence of television exposure.

The theory proposes that repeated exposure to particular media representations may influence perceptions of social reality.

For example, frequent exposure to violent television content may contribute to exaggerated perceptions of how common violence is.

Media effects are influenced by many other factors and are not identical for every viewer.

## 8. Social Learning and Media

Albert Bandura's social learning perspective helps explain how media can influence behaviour through observation.

Individuals may learn by observing:
- Actions.
- Consequences.
- Social rewards.
- Models of behaviour.

Media characters and public figures can become behavioural models.

The influence depends on factors such as identification, attention and perceived consequences.

## 9. Persuasion and Advertising

Advertising attempts to influence consumer attitudes and behaviour.

It may use:
- Emotional appeals.
- Social proof.
- Authority.
- Repetition.
- Attractive presentation.
- Product comparisons.
- Scarcity messages.

### 9.1 Emotional Appeals

Advertisements may associate products with happiness, security, excitement or belonging.

### 9.2 Social Proof

Social proof involves using the behaviour or opinions of others as evidence of what is desirable or appropriate.

### 9.3 Authority

Messages may become more persuasive when associated with perceived expertise or credibility.

### 9.4 Repetition

Repeated exposure can increase familiarity and recognition, although excessive repetition may produce irritation.

## 10. Digital Media Psychology

Digital media creates new forms of social interaction and information consumption.

Psychological processes involved include:
- Social comparison.
- Online identity.
- Social reinforcement.
- Attention management.
- Information seeking.
- Emotional expression.

Digital environments can provide social connection and access to information while also creating challenges.

## 11. Social Comparison on Social Media

Social comparison involves evaluating oneself in relation to others.

Online platforms often display selected aspects of people's lives.

This can influence:
- Self-esteem.
- Body image.
- Perceived success.
- Social expectations.
- Emotional well-being.

The effects vary according to the content, individual characteristics and patterns of use.

## 12. Attention and Media Multitasking

Media multitasking involves engaging with multiple information sources or activities within a limited period.

Frequent switching between tasks can increase cognitive demands and interfere with sustained attention.

Examples include:
- Studying while checking messages.
- Watching videos while browsing social media.
- Switching repeatedly between unrelated tasks.

Task switching is not necessarily the same as performing multiple demanding tasks simultaneously.

## 13. Misinformation and Cognitive Bias

Misinformation refers to inaccurate or misleading information.

People may be influenced by:

**Confirmation bias:** Preference for information that supports existing beliefs.

**Availability heuristic:** Judging likelihood based on how easily examples come to mind.

**Illusory truth effect:** Increased perceived truth of information through repeated exposure.

**Source credibility bias:** Accepting information because it comes from a trusted or authoritative source without sufficient evaluation.

Critical evaluation can reduce the influence of these biases.

## 14. Media Literacy

Media literacy involves the ability to access, analyse, evaluate and create media messages.

Important practices include:
- Checking the original source.
- Comparing multiple reliable sources.
- Distinguishing facts from opinions.
- Examining evidence.
- Identifying emotional manipulation.
- Recognising misleading headlines.
- Checking publication dates and context.

## 15. Psychological Effects of Media Consumption

Media use can influence:
- Mood.
- Attention.
- Knowledge.
- Social attitudes.
- Sleep routines.
- Emotional responses.

The effects depend on content, timing, duration, personal vulnerability and the surrounding environment.

It is therefore inaccurate to assume that all media use is harmful or beneficial.

## 16. Ethical Issues in Media

Important ethical concerns include:
- Privacy.
- Manipulative advertising.
- Data collection.
- Misrepresentation.
- Cyberbullying.
- Harmful stereotypes.
- Misinformation.
- Inappropriate targeting.

Psychological knowledge should be used responsibly in media design and communication.

## 17. Summary

- Communication involves senders, messages, channels, receivers, feedback and noise.
- Physical, psychological, semantic and cultural barriers can interfere with communication.
- Agenda-setting concerns which issues receive public attention.
- Framing influences how issues are presented and interpreted.
- Cultivation theory examines long-term media influences on perceptions of reality.
- Social learning explains how people learn through observation.
- Advertising uses emotional appeals, repetition and social influence.
- Digital media can influence identity, attention and social comparison.
- Cognitive biases contribute to the spread of misinformation.
- Media literacy supports critical evaluation of information.
