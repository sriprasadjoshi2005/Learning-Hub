# Lesson 17: Introduction to Social Psychology

Social psychology studies how individuals' thoughts, feelings and behaviours are influenced by the actual, imagined or implied presence of other people. It investigates social perception, attitudes, relationships, group membership and the processes through which individuals interpret and respond to their social environment.

## 1. Meaning of Social Psychology

Social psychology is the scientific study of how individuals think about, influence and relate to other people.

It examines questions such as:
- Why do people conform to group expectations?
- How are attitudes formed?
- Why do stereotypes develop?
- How do relationships influence behaviour?
- What factors contribute to cooperation or conflict?

Social psychology focuses on the interaction between individuals and their social environments.

## 2. Individual and Social Influences

Human behaviour is influenced by both personal characteristics and social conditions.

### 2.1 Individual Factors

These include:
- Personality.
- Motivation.
- Beliefs.
- Emotions.
- Previous experiences.
- Cognitive processes.

### 2.2 Social Factors

These include:
- Family.
- Friends.
- Social groups.
- Cultural expectations.
- Social norms.
- Institutions.
- Media.

The same individual may behave differently across different social situations.

## 3. Social Cognition

Social cognition refers to the mental processes involved in understanding other people and social situations.

It includes:
- Forming impressions.
- Interpreting behaviour.
- Making social judgements.
- Understanding intentions.
- Remembering social information.

## 4. Social Perception

Social perception involves interpreting information about other individuals.

People form impressions using:
- Facial expressions.
- Body language.
- Speech.
- Behaviour.
- Appearance.
- Social context.

These impressions may be useful but can also be inaccurate.

## 5. Attribution Theory

Attribution theory examines how people explain the causes of behaviour.

People often distinguish between internal and external explanations.

### 5.1 Internal Attribution

Internal attribution explains behaviour through personal characteristics.

Examples:
- Ability.
- Personality.
- Motivation.
- Attitudes.

### 5.2 External Attribution

External attribution explains behaviour through situational factors.

Examples:
- Environmental conditions.
- Social pressure.
- Difficulty of a task.
- Unexpected events.

### 5.3 Fundamental Attribution Error

The fundamental attribution error refers to the tendency to overemphasise personal characteristics and underestimate situational influences when explaining other people's behaviour.

Example:

A person may interpret another individual's lateness as irresponsibility without considering traffic or an emergency.

### 5.4 Actor-Observer Difference

People may explain their own behaviour through situational circumstances while explaining another person's behaviour through personal characteristics.

This tendency is not universal and varies across situations and cultures.

### 5.5 Self-Serving Bias

Self-serving bias involves attributing personal successes to internal factors and failures to external factors.

Example:

A person may attribute a good examination result to ability but a poor result to an unfair examination.

## 6. Attitudes

An attitude is a relatively enduring evaluation of an object, person, group or issue.

Attitudes can be positive, negative or mixed.

### 6.1 Components of Attitudes

A commonly used model identifies three components.

**Cognitive component:** Beliefs and thoughts about an object.

**Affective component:** Feelings or emotional responses.

**Behavioural component:** Behavioural intentions or action tendencies.

Example:

A person who values environmental protection may:
- Believe pollution harms ecosystems.
- Feel concerned about environmental damage.
- Support recycling initiatives.

Actual behaviour may still differ from stated attitudes.

## 7. Formation of Attitudes

Attitudes can develop through several processes.

### 7.1 Direct Experience

Personal experiences can shape evaluations.

### 7.2 Classical Conditioning

An attitude may develop through associations between an object and positive or negative experiences.

### 7.3 Operant Conditioning

Attitudes may be influenced by rewards and consequences associated with expressing particular views.

### 7.4 Observational Learning

Individuals may develop attitudes by observing family members, peers and other influential people.

### 7.5 Social and Cultural Influences

Cultural values, education and social institutions contribute to attitude formation.

## 8. Attitude Change and Persuasion

Persuasion is an attempt to influence attitudes through communication.

Factors influencing persuasion include:

- Credibility of the communicator.
- Strength of the message.
- Emotional appeal.
- Relevance to the audience.
- Existing beliefs.
- Quality of supporting evidence.

### 8.1 Elaboration Likelihood Model

The elaboration likelihood model, developed by Richard Petty and John Cacioppo, describes two major routes of persuasion.

**Central route:**

Individuals carefully examine arguments and supporting evidence.

Attitude change through this route may be relatively durable when people are motivated and able to think deeply.

**Peripheral route:**

Individuals rely more on cues such as attractiveness, reputation or emotional presentation.

This route involves less detailed evaluation of the message itself.

## 9. Cognitive Dissonance

Cognitive dissonance theory was developed by Leon Festinger.

Cognitive dissonance is psychological discomfort that may occur when an individual experiences inconsistency between beliefs, attitudes or behaviour.

Example:

A person who values physical health but regularly avoids exercise may experience dissonance.

Possible responses include:
- Changing behaviour.
- Changing beliefs.
- Justifying the inconsistency.
- Reducing the importance of the conflict.

## 10. Stereotypes, Prejudice and Discrimination

These concepts are related but distinct.

### 10.1 Stereotypes

Stereotypes are generalised beliefs about members of a social group.

They may be positive, negative or mixed, but can oversimplify individual differences.

### 10.2 Prejudice

Prejudice refers to an attitude or evaluation towards individuals based on their perceived group membership.

It often involves unfavourable judgements.

### 10.3 Discrimination

Discrimination refers to behaviour or actions that treat individuals differently because of group membership.

### 10.4 Comparison

| Concept | Main component |
|---|---|
| Stereotype | Belief |
| Prejudice | Attitude |
| Discrimination | Behaviour |

## 11. Reducing Prejudice

Prejudice can be reduced through several approaches.

### Education

Providing accurate information can challenge misconceptions.

### Intergroup Contact

Contact between members of different groups can reduce prejudice under suitable conditions.

### Cooperation

Shared goals can encourage positive intergroup relationships.

### Institutional Support

Fair policies and social norms can discourage discriminatory behaviour.

## 12. Social Identity Theory

Social identity theory, developed by Henri Tajfel and John Turner, explains how group membership contributes to an individual's sense of identity.

Important concepts include:

**Social categorisation:** Dividing people into social groups.

**Social identification:** Incorporating group membership into one's identity.

**Social comparison:** Evaluating one's group in relation to other groups.

In-group favouritism can occur when individuals show preference towards members of their own group.

## 13. Summary

- Social psychology studies how people think, feel and behave in social contexts.
- Social cognition involves understanding and interpreting social information.
- Attribution theory explains how people interpret the causes of behaviour.
- The fundamental attribution error involves overemphasising personal causes.
- Attitudes include cognitive, affective and behavioural components.
- Persuasion can occur through central and peripheral routes.
- Cognitive dissonance involves discomfort arising from inconsistency.
- Stereotypes are beliefs, prejudice involves attitudes and discrimination involves behaviour.
- Social identity theory explains the psychological importance of group membership.
