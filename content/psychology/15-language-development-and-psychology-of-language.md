# Lesson 15: Language Development and Psychology of Language

Language is a structured system of symbols used to communicate information, thoughts, emotions and intentions. Psychology investigates how language develops, how the brain processes it and how language interacts with thinking, memory and social behaviour.

## 1. Meaning and Characteristics of Language

Language is a system of spoken, written or signed symbols governed by rules that allow meaningful communication.

Major characteristics include:

- Symbolic representation.
- Rule-governed structure.
- Communication of meaning.
- Productivity.
- Social transmission.
- Development through learning and biological capacities.

Language is not limited to speech. Sign languages are fully developed linguistic systems.

## 2. Components of Language

### 2.1 Phonology

Phonology concerns the sound system and patterns of a language.

A phoneme is the smallest sound unit capable of distinguishing meaning in a particular language.

Example: The sounds /p/ and /b/ distinguish the words "pat" and "bat".

### 2.2 Morphology

Morphology studies the structure of words and how meaningful units combine.

A morpheme is the smallest unit of language that carries meaning or grammatical function.

Examples:
- "Un" in "unhappy".
- "-ed" in "walked".
- "Book" as an independent word.

### 2.3 Syntax

Syntax concerns the rules governing how words are arranged into sentences.

Example:

"The child reads the book" follows a different structure from "The book reads the child".

### 2.4 Semantics

Semantics concerns meaning in language.

It includes the meanings of words, phrases and sentences.

### 2.5 Pragmatics

Pragmatics concerns how language is used appropriately in social contexts.

Example:

The sentence "Could you open the window?" is grammatically a question but commonly functions as a polite request.

## 3. Language Acquisition

Language acquisition is the process through which individuals develop the ability to understand and use language.

Children acquire language through interactions involving biological development, cognitive abilities and social experience.

### 3.1 Early Language Development

Common developmental patterns include:

**Cooing:** Early vocalisations, often consisting of vowel-like sounds.

**Babbling:** Repeated combinations of speech-like sounds.

**One-word stage:** Children begin using single words to communicate meaningful ideas.

**Two-word stage:** Children combine two words to express simple relationships.

**Early sentence development:** Children gradually develop more complex grammatical structures.

The timing and sequence of language development vary across individuals and languages.

## 4. Theories of Language Development

### 4.1 Behaviourist Theory

B. F. Skinner proposed that language learning could be explained partly through imitation, reinforcement and environmental interaction.

For example, children may repeat words that receive positive responses from caregivers.

**Limitation:** Reinforcement and imitation alone do not fully explain the complexity and creativity of language.

### 4.2 Nativist Theory

Noam Chomsky argued that humans possess biological capacities that support language acquisition.

He proposed that children are biologically prepared to acquire the structural rules of language.

His ideas challenged explanations based entirely on imitation and reinforcement.

### 4.3 Interactionist Theory

Interactionist approaches emphasise the combined influence of biological capacities, cognitive development and social interaction.

Children acquire language through active participation in communication with others.

## 5. Critical Period and Sensitive Period

A critical period refers to a limited developmental window during which particular experiences are especially necessary for typical development.

A sensitive period refers to a period when learning a particular ability may be easier or more efficient, although learning may remain possible later.

Language research suggests that early experience is especially important for language development, but the precise effects vary across linguistic abilities and circumstances.

## 6. Brain and Language

Language processing involves interconnected brain regions.

### 6.1 Broca's Area

Broca's area, commonly associated with the left frontal lobe, contributes to speech production and language processing.

Damage to relevant regions may produce expressive language difficulties.

### 6.2 Wernicke's Area

Wernicke's area, traditionally associated with the left temporal region, contributes to language comprehension.

Damage to relevant regions may produce difficulties understanding language.

Modern neuroscience recognises that language depends on distributed networks rather than two isolated brain centres.

## 7. Aphasia

Aphasia is an acquired language disorder caused by damage to brain regions involved in language.

### 7.1 Broca's Aphasia

Common characteristics include:
- Slow and effortful speech.
- Reduced sentence complexity.
- Difficulty producing grammatical speech.
- Relatively stronger comprehension than expression in many cases.

### 7.2 Wernicke's Aphasia

Common characteristics include:
- Fluent but sometimes meaningless speech.
- Difficulty understanding spoken language.
- Word substitutions.
- Reduced awareness of language errors in some cases.

The exact presentation depends on the location and extent of brain damage.

## 8. Language and Thought

The relationship between language and thought has been investigated through several theoretical approaches.

### 8.1 Linguistic Relativity

The linguistic relativity hypothesis proposes that the language a person uses can influence aspects of perception, categorisation and thinking.

### 8.2 Linguistic Determinism

A stronger claim, linguistic determinism, proposes that language determines the limits of thought.

This stronger version is not generally accepted as a complete explanation of human cognition.

### 8.3 Contemporary Perspective

Research suggests that language can influence certain cognitive processes without completely determining what individuals are capable of thinking.

Humans can also reason through visual imagery, spatial representations, numerical systems and other non-linguistic processes.

## 9. Bilingualism and Multilingualism

Bilingualism involves the use of two languages, while multilingualism involves the use of multiple languages.

Possible cognitive and social advantages include:
- Communication across linguistic communities.
- Greater opportunities for cultural interaction.
- Flexible language use.
- Development of metalinguistic awareness.

Cognitive effects vary depending on language experience, proficiency, context and research methods.

## 10. Nonverbal Communication

Communication does not depend entirely on words.

Nonverbal communication includes:

- Facial expressions.
- Gestures.
- Posture.
- Eye contact.
- Personal distance.
- Tone of voice.
- Body movements.

Nonverbal signals may reinforce, modify or contradict spoken messages.

Their interpretation depends on social and cultural context.

## 11. Language, Memory and Learning

Language supports several cognitive processes.

It can help individuals:
- Organise information.
- Categorise experiences.
- Communicate knowledge.
- Develop abstract concepts.
- Use inner speech to guide behaviour.
- Remember and explain complex information.

Language is therefore an important tool for both communication and cognition.

## 12. Summary

- Language is a structured system of symbols used for communication.
- Phonology concerns sound patterns.
- Morphology concerns word structure.
- Syntax concerns sentence structure.
- Semantics concerns meaning.
- Pragmatics concerns language use in social contexts.
- Language development involves biological, cognitive and social influences.
- Behaviourist, nativist and interactionist theories provide different explanations.
- Broca's and Wernicke's areas are associated with different language functions.
- Aphasia involves acquired language difficulties.
- Language can influence thought without completely determining it.
- Nonverbal communication contributes significantly to social interaction.
