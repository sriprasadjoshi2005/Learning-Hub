# Lesson 34: Schizophrenia and Psychotic Disorders

Psychotic disorders involve disturbances in the way individuals perceive, interpret and understand reality. Schizophrenia is one of the most widely studied psychotic disorders. This lesson examines its symptoms, possible causes, effects on functioning and treatment approaches.

## 1. Understanding Psychosis

### 1.1 Meaning of Psychosis

Psychosis is a condition involving significant disturbances in reality testing.

An individual experiencing psychosis may have difficulty distinguishing personal perceptions or beliefs from external reality.

### 1.2 Common Features

Psychosis may involve:

- Hallucinations.
- Delusions.
- Disorganised thinking.
- Disorganised behaviour.
- Reduced awareness of symptoms.

Psychosis can occur in several conditions and does not automatically indicate schizophrenia.

## 2. Understanding Schizophrenia

### 2.1 Definition

Schizophrenia is a serious mental disorder involving disturbances in perception, thought, emotional expression and behaviour.

It can affect relationships, education, employment and independent living.

### 2.2 Onset and Development

Schizophrenia commonly begins in late adolescence or early adulthood, although its onset varies.

Its development may involve a combination of genetic vulnerability, brain development and environmental influences.

## 3. Positive Symptoms

Positive symptoms refer to experiences or behaviours that are added to ordinary functioning.

### 3.1 Hallucinations

Hallucinations are sensory experiences that occur without an external stimulus corresponding to the experience.

Types include:

- Auditory hallucinations: hearing sounds or voices.
- Visual hallucinations: seeing things that are not present.
- Tactile hallucinations: experiencing sensations of touch without an external cause.
- Olfactory hallucinations: smelling odours that are not present.
- Gustatory hallucinations: experiencing tastes without an external source.

Auditory hallucinations are particularly associated with schizophrenia.

### 3.2 Delusions

Delusions are strongly held beliefs that persist despite clear contradictory evidence and are not adequately explained by the person's cultural or social context.

Examples include:

- Persecutory delusions: believing that others intend to harm or threaten the individual.
- Grandiose delusions: believing oneself to possess exceptional power or importance.
- Referential delusions: believing that ordinary events or messages are specifically directed towards oneself.

### 3.3 Disorganised Thinking

Disorganised thinking may become noticeable through speech.

An individual may:

- Move rapidly between unrelated ideas.
- Give answers that are difficult to follow.
- Produce speech with weak logical connections.
- Struggle to organise thoughts coherently.

## 4. Negative Symptoms

Negative symptoms involve reductions or losses in ordinary psychological functioning.

### 4.1 Affective Flattening

Affective flattening refers to reduced outward expression of emotion.

### 4.2 Avolition

Avolition involves reduced motivation and difficulty initiating or maintaining purposeful activities.

### 4.3 Alogia

Alogia refers to reduced speech output or limited verbal expression.

### 4.4 Anhedonia

Anhedonia involves reduced ability to experience pleasure or interest in activities.

### 4.5 Asociality

Asociality refers to reduced interest in social interactions and relationships.

Negative symptoms can significantly interfere with daily functioning.

## 5. Cognitive Symptoms

Cognitive symptoms involve difficulties with mental processes.

They may include:

- Reduced working memory.
- Difficulty sustaining attention.
- Problems with planning.
- Slower information processing.
- Difficulties with decision-making.

These difficulties can affect learning, employment and independent functioning.

## 6. Possible Causes and Explanations

### 6.1 Genetic Factors

Schizophrenia has a substantial genetic component.

Having a close relative with schizophrenia increases vulnerability, but genetics alone do not determine whether someone develops the disorder.

### 6.2 Dopamine Hypothesis

The dopamine hypothesis proposes that abnormal dopamine signalling contributes to certain symptoms of schizophrenia.

Dopamine-related mechanisms are particularly relevant to positive symptoms.

Modern research recognises that schizophrenia involves more complex neural systems than dopamine alone.

### 6.3 Brain Structure and Function

Research has identified differences in brain structure and activity among some individuals with schizophrenia.

Areas and networks involved in cognition, emotional processing and perception may be affected.

### 6.4 Environmental Influences

Possible environmental risk factors include:

- Prenatal complications.
- Early developmental adversity.
- Exposure to severe stress.
- Certain substance-related risks.
- Complications affecting early brain development.

These factors interact with biological vulnerability.

## 7. Diagnosis and Assessment

### 7.1 Clinical Evaluation

Diagnosis involves a detailed assessment of symptoms, their duration, severity and impact on functioning.

### 7.2 Diagnostic Criteria

Under DSM-5-TR criteria, schizophrenia requires at least two characteristic symptoms during a significant portion of a one-month period, with at least one being delusions, hallucinations or disorganised speech.

The overall disturbance must persist for at least six months, including active symptoms and other periods of disturbance.

### 7.3 Differential Diagnosis

Professionals must distinguish schizophrenia from other conditions that may involve psychosis, including:

- Bipolar disorder with psychotic features.
- Major depressive disorder with psychotic features.
- Schizoaffective disorder.
- Substance-induced psychotic disorders.
- Certain neurological or medical conditions.

## 8. Treatment and Management

### 8.1 Antipsychotic Medication

Antipsychotic medications are commonly used to manage psychotic symptoms.

They influence neurotransmitter systems, particularly dopamine pathways.

### 8.2 Psychotherapy

Psychological interventions may help individuals:

- Understand their experiences.
- Manage distress.
- Improve coping skills.
- Develop strategies for everyday challenges.

Cognitive behavioural therapy for psychosis may be used as part of treatment.

### 8.3 Family Intervention

Family interventions provide education and support to relatives.

They can improve communication, reduce misunderstandings and support treatment participation.

### 8.4 Rehabilitation and Social Support

Psychosocial rehabilitation may focus on:

- Social skills.
- Independent living.
- Educational participation.
- Employment support.
- Community integration.

### 8.5 Early Intervention

Recognising symptoms and obtaining professional assessment early can improve opportunities for effective treatment and support.

## 9. Stigma and Recovery

Schizophrenia is frequently misunderstood and associated with harmful stereotypes.

People with schizophrenia are not inherently violent. Most people with the condition are not violent, and they may themselves experience increased vulnerability to victimisation and social exclusion.

Recovery is possible, although its course differs between individuals. Appropriate treatment, social support and access to services can improve quality of life.

## 10. Summary

Psychosis involves disturbances in reality testing and may include hallucinations, delusions and disorganised thinking. Schizophrenia is a complex disorder involving positive, negative and cognitive symptoms. Its development is associated with interacting genetic, biological and environmental factors. Treatment may combine medication, psychotherapy, family interventions and rehabilitation.
