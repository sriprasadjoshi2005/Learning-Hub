# Lesson 29: Psychotic Disorders and Schizophrenia Spectrum

Psychotic disorders involve disturbances in reality testing, perception, thought and behaviour. They may include hallucinations, delusions and disorganised thinking. Schizophrenia is one condition within the broader schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders. Understanding these conditions requires distinguishing symptoms, examining possible causes and considering appropriate treatment.

## 1. Meaning of Psychosis

Psychosis is a clinical state involving significant disturbances in the interpretation of reality.

It may involve:
- Hallucinations.
- Delusions.
- Disorganised thinking.
- Disorganised behaviour.
- Reduced insight into unusual experiences.

Psychosis is not a diagnosis by itself. It can occur in several psychiatric, neurological and medical conditions.

## 2. Major Symptoms of Psychosis

### 2.1 Hallucinations

Hallucinations are perception-like experiences that occur without an external stimulus producing the corresponding perception.

They may involve:
- Auditory experiences.
- Visual experiences.
- Tactile experiences.
- Olfactory experiences.
- Gustatory experiences.

Auditory hallucinations are commonly reported in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders.

### 2.2 Delusions

Delusions are strongly held beliefs that remain fixed despite substantial contradictory evidence and cannot be adequately explained by the person's cultural context.

Examples include:
- Persecutory delusions.
- Grandiose delusions.
- Referential delusions.
- Somatic delusions.
- Delusions of control.

A strongly held unusual belief is not automatically a delusion. Clinical interpretation requires cultural and contextual understanding.

### 2.3 Disorganised Thinking

Disorganised thinking can affect the organisation and logical connection of ideas.

It may become noticeable through speech.

Examples include:
- Derailment.
- Tangential responses.
- Incoherent speech.
- Disorganised associations.

### 2.4 Disorganised Behaviour

Behaviour may become unpredictable, poorly organised or inappropriate to the situation.

### 2.5 Negative Symptoms

Negative symptoms involve reductions or absences of typical psychological functions.

Examples include:

**Avolition:** Reduced motivation for goal-directed activities.

**Alogia:** Reduced speech output.

**Anhedonia:** Reduced capacity for pleasure.

**Asociality:** Reduced interest in social interaction.

**Blunted affect:** Reduced outward expression of emotion.

Negative symptoms can interfere significantly with everyday functioning.

## 3. Schizophrenia

Schizophrenia is a complex psychiatric disorder involving disturbances in perception, thought, emotion and behaviour.

It can affect:
- Social functioning.
- Employment.
- Education.
- Self-care.
- Relationships.
- Cognitive performance.

Schizophrenia is not the same as having multiple personalities.

## 4. Positive, Negative and Cognitive Symptoms

Symptoms of schizophrenia are often discussed in three broad categories.

### 4.1 Positive Symptoms

Positive symptoms involve experiences or behaviours added to ordinary functioning.

Examples:
- Hallucinations.
- Delusions.
- Disorganised speech.
- Disorganised behaviour.

### 4.2 Negative Symptoms

Negative symptoms involve reductions in typical functions.

Examples:
- Reduced motivation.
- Limited emotional expression.
- Reduced speech.
- Social withdrawal.

### 4.3 Cognitive Symptoms

Cognitive difficulties may involve:
- Attention.
- Working memory.
- Processing speed.
- Executive functioning.
- Learning.

These difficulties can contribute to challenges in everyday functioning.

## 5. Course and Development

Schizophrenia often emerges during late adolescence or early adulthood, although onset can occur at different ages.

Its course varies considerably.

Some individuals experience:
- A gradual development of symptoms.
- Episodes with periods of improvement.
- Persistent symptoms.
- Significant recovery with appropriate treatment and support.

There is no single developmental pattern that applies to every person.

## 6. Biological Influences

### 6.1 Genetic Vulnerability

Genetic research suggests that schizophrenia has a substantial inherited component.

However, no single gene determines whether a person will develop the condition.

### 6.2 Dopamine Hypothesis

The dopamine hypothesis proposes that altered dopamine signalling contributes to some symptoms of schizophrenia.

Dopamine-related mechanisms are complex and vary across brain pathways.

Modern explanations include additional neurotransmitter systems and broader neural network differences.

### 6.3 Brain Structure and Function

Research has identified group-level differences in certain brain structures and functional networks.

These findings do not provide a single diagnostic brain scan for schizophrenia.

## 7. Psychological and Environmental Influences

Possible contributing factors include:
- Prenatal and developmental complications.
- Severe stress.
- Childhood adversity.
- Substance exposure.
- Social isolation.
- Urban environmental conditions.

These factors may influence vulnerability, but none alone explains every case.

## 8. Schizophrenia Spectrum and Related Disorders

Several conditions are classified within the schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders.

### 8.1 Schizophreniform Disorder

Schizophreniform disorder involves symptoms resembling schizophrenia, but the duration is between one and six months.

### 8.2 Brief Psychotic Disorder

Brief psychotic disorder involves sudden psychotic symptoms lasting at least one day but less than one month, followed by a return to the previous level of functioning.

### 8.3 Schizoaffective Disorder

Schizoaffective disorder involves an illness in which schizophrenia-spectrum symptoms occur alongside significant mood episodes.

### 8.4 Delusional Disorder

Delusional disorder involves one or more persistent delusions, with functioning outside the direct effects of the delusions often being relatively preserved.

These conditions require careful differential diagnosis.

## 9. Differential Diagnosis

Psychotic symptoms may occur in conditions other than schizophrenia.

Important possibilities include:
- Bipolar disorder with psychotic features.
- Severe depressive episodes with psychotic features.
- Substance-induced psychosis.
- Neurological disorders.
- Medical conditions.
- Medication-related effects.

Assessment must examine symptom history, timing, physical health and substance exposure.

## 10. Assessment

Assessment may include:
- Clinical interviews.
- Mental status examination.
- Developmental history.
- Functional assessment.
- Medical evaluation.
- Substance-use assessment.
- Information from family or caregivers when appropriate and with suitable consent.

Diagnosis is based on clinical criteria and the overall pattern of symptoms.

## 11. Treatment Approaches

Treatment often combines several forms of support.

### 11.1 Antipsychotic Medication

Antipsychotic medication may help reduce psychotic symptoms.

Treatment decisions require professional evaluation of benefits, side effects and individual needs.

### 11.2 Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Psychosis

CBT for psychosis can help individuals understand distressing experiences and develop strategies for managing their impact.

### 11.3 Family Intervention

Family-based interventions can improve communication, understanding and support.

### 11.4 Psychosocial Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation may support:
- Social skills.
- Independent living.
- Employment.
- Education.
- Daily functioning.

### 11.5 Coordinated Specialty Care

Early-intervention programmes may combine medication management, psychotherapy, family support and educational or vocational assistance.

## 12. Stigma and Misconceptions

Schizophrenia is frequently misunderstood.

Common misconceptions include:
- It means having multiple personalities.
- Everyone with schizophrenia is violent.
- Recovery is impossible.
- The condition results from personal weakness.

These claims are inaccurate.

Most people with schizophrenia are not violent, and many can achieve meaningful improvements with appropriate treatment and support.

## 13. Summary

- Psychosis involves disturbances in reality testing.
- Hallucinations are perception-like experiences without corresponding external stimuli.
- Delusions are fixed beliefs that persist despite strong contradictory evidence.
- Schizophrenia involves positive, negative and cognitive symptoms.
- Positive symptoms include hallucinations and delusions.
- Negative symptoms involve reductions in typical functioning.
- Schizophrenia has complex biological, psychological and environmental influences.
- Related conditions include schizophreniform disorder and brief psychotic disorder.
- Differential diagnosis is necessary because psychosis can occur in multiple conditions.
- Treatment may include medication, psychotherapy and psychosocial rehabilitation.
- Stigma and inaccurate assumptions can create additional difficulties for affected individuals.
