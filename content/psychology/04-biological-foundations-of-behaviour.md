# Lesson 4: Biological Foundations of Behaviour

Human behaviour and mental processes are closely connected to biological activity. The brain, nervous system, hormones and genetic factors influence how individuals perceive information, experience emotions, learn, remember and respond to their environment.

## 1. Relationship Between Biology and Behaviour

Biological psychology investigates how biological systems contribute to behaviour and mental processes.

Examples include:

- Brain activity during decision-making.
- Nervous system responses to danger.
- Hormonal changes associated with stress.
- Genetic influences on certain behavioural characteristics.
- Biological processes involved in sleep and memory.

Biological influences interact with psychological experiences and environmental conditions. Behaviour cannot generally be explained by biology alone.

## 2. The Nervous System

The nervous system is the body's communication and coordination network.

It receives information, processes signals and coordinates responses.

It is broadly divided into:

1. Central nervous system.
2. Peripheral nervous system.

### 2.1 Central Nervous System

The central nervous system consists of the brain and spinal cord.

**Brain:** Processes sensory information, coordinates movement and supports complex mental functions.

**Spinal cord:** Transmits information between the brain and the body and participates in certain reflex actions.

### 2.2 Peripheral Nervous System

The peripheral nervous system includes nerves outside the brain and spinal cord.

It connects the central nervous system with the rest of the body.

It is divided into the somatic and autonomic nervous systems.

#### Somatic Nervous System

The somatic nervous system carries sensory information and controls voluntary skeletal muscle movements.

Examples include walking, writing and lifting an object.

#### Autonomic Nervous System

The autonomic nervous system regulates many involuntary functions.

Examples include heart rate, digestion and glandular activity.

It includes two major divisions.

**Sympathetic division:**

Prepares the body for demanding or threatening situations.

Common effects include:

- Increased heart rate.
- Pupil dilation.
- Increased alertness.
- Reduced digestive activity.

**Parasympathetic division:**

Supports resting and restorative functions.

Common effects include:

- Reduced heart rate.
- Increased digestive activity.
- Energy conservation.
- Recovery following stress.

The two divisions work together to maintain physiological balance.

## 3. Neurons: The Basic Units of the Nervous System

Neurons are specialised cells that receive and transmit information through electrical and chemical signals.

### 3.1 Structure of a Neuron

Important components include:

- **Dendrites:** Receive signals from other cells.
- **Cell body (soma):** Contains the nucleus and maintains the cell.
- **Axon:** Carries electrical signals away from the cell body.
- **Myelin sheath:** Insulates many axons and increases signal transmission speed.
- **Axon terminals:** Release chemical messengers to communicate with other cells.

### 3.2 Types of Neurons

**Sensory neurons:** Carry information from sensory receptors towards the central nervous system.

**Motor neurons:** Carry signals from the central nervous system towards muscles or glands.

**Interneurons:** Connect neurons and process information within the central nervous system.

## 4. Neural Communication

Neurons communicate through electrical and chemical processes.

### 4.1 Resting Potential

A resting neuron maintains an electrical potential difference across its cell membrane.

The inside of the neuron is generally more negatively charged relative to the outside.

This state is called the resting potential.

### 4.2 Action Potential

An action potential is a rapid change in the electrical potential across a neuron's membrane.

When sufficient stimulation reaches the threshold, an action potential is generated.

It involves changes in the movement of charged ions, particularly sodium and potassium.

The action potential travels along the axon.

### 4.3 Synapse

A synapse is a specialised junction through which one neuron communicates with another neuron or target cell.

At a chemical synapse:

1. An electrical signal reaches the axon terminal.
2. Neurotransmitters are released.
3. They cross the synaptic gap.
4. They bind to receptors on the receiving cell.
5. The receiving cell responds.

### 4.4 Neurotransmitters

Neurotransmitters are chemical messengers involved in communication between neurons.

| Neurotransmitter | Some associated functions |
|---|---|
| Acetylcholine | Muscle activation, attention and memory |
| Dopamine | Motivation, movement and reward-related processes |
| Serotonin | Mood, sleep and other regulatory processes |
| GABA | Major inhibitory neurotransmission in the brain |
| Glutamate | Major excitatory neurotransmission and learning |
| Noradrenaline | Alertness and stress-related responses |

Neurotransmitters do not have only one function. Their effects depend on receptor types, brain regions and physiological conditions.

## 5. Structure and Functions of the Brain

<AsyncImage query="human brain lateral view labelled cerebral cortex frontal parietal temporal occipital lobes cerebellum brainstem educational anatomy diagram" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%" />

The brain contains interconnected regions responsible for different functions.

### 5.1 Cerebrum

The cerebrum is the largest part of the human brain.

It is involved in perception, voluntary movement, reasoning, language, memory and other complex functions.

### 5.2 Cerebral Cortex

The cerebral cortex is the outer layer of the cerebrum.

It is involved in complex cognitive processes and is divided into four major lobes.

**Frontal lobe:**

- Planning.
- Decision-making.
- Voluntary movement.
- Behaviour regulation.
- Speech production.

**Parietal lobe:**

- Touch processing.
- Spatial awareness.
- Integration of sensory information.

**Temporal lobe:**

- Auditory processing.
- Language comprehension.
- Memory-related processes.

**Occipital lobe:**

- Visual processing.

These lobes interact rather than functioning as completely independent units.

### 5.3 Cerebellum

The cerebellum contributes to:

- Balance.
- Coordination.
- Posture.
- Motor learning.
- Fine adjustment of movement.

### 5.4 Brainstem

The brainstem connects the brain with the spinal cord.

It contributes to essential functions such as breathing, heart rate and alertness.

### 5.5 Limbic System

The limbic system refers to interconnected structures involved in emotion, motivation, memory and other functions.

Important structures include:

**Amygdala:** Involved in emotional processing, particularly fear-related learning and responses.

**Hippocampus:** Important for forming and organising new memories.

**Hypothalamus:** Helps regulate hunger, thirst, body temperature and hormonal activity.

## 6. The Endocrine System

The endocrine system consists of glands that release hormones into the bloodstream.

Hormones are chemical messengers that influence physiological processes and behaviour.

Important glands include:

| Gland | Major functions |
|---|---|
| Pituitary | Regulates several other endocrine glands |
| Thyroid | Influences metabolism |
| Adrenal glands | Produce hormones involved in stress responses |
| Pancreas | Regulates blood glucose |
| Pineal gland | Produces melatonin, involved in sleep timing |
| Gonads | Produce sex hormones involved in reproduction and development |

### 6.1 Hormones and Behaviour

Hormones can influence:

- Mood and emotional responses.
- Stress reactions.
- Sleep patterns.
- Appetite.
- Reproductive behaviour.
- Growth and development.

Hormonal effects are complex and depend on interactions with the nervous system and environment.

## 7. Genetics and Behaviour

Genetics investigates how hereditary information influences biological characteristics.

Genes are segments of DNA that contribute to inherited traits.

### 7.1 Genetic Influence

Genetic factors can influence characteristics such as:

- Certain aspects of temperament.
- Susceptibility to some psychological conditions.
- Cognitive abilities.
- Physiological responses.

However, genetic influence does not mean that a trait is completely predetermined.

### 7.2 Nature and Nurture

The nature-nurture discussion concerns the relative contributions of genetic inheritance and environmental experience to human development and behaviour.

**Nature:** Biological and hereditary influences.

**Nurture:** Environmental influences, including upbringing, education, culture and personal experiences.

Modern psychology recognises that nature and nurture interact.

For example, a genetic predisposition may influence an individual's potential, while environmental experiences influence how that potential develops.

## 8. Neuroplasticity

Neuroplasticity refers to the nervous system's ability to change its structure and functioning in response to experience, learning or injury.

Examples include:

- Changes in neural connections during learning.
- Development of skills through repeated practice.
- Functional reorganisation following certain brain injuries.

Neuroplasticity demonstrates that the brain is not completely fixed after development.

## 9. Summary

- The nervous system coordinates communication and responses throughout the body.
- The central nervous system consists of the brain and spinal cord.
- Neurons transmit information through electrical and chemical signals.
- Neurotransmitters enable communication across many synapses.
- Different brain regions contribute to perception, movement, memory and reasoning.
- The endocrine system influences behaviour through hormones.
- Genetic and environmental factors interact in human development.
- Neuroplasticity allows the nervous system to adapt through experience.
