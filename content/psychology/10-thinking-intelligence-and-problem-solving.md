# Lesson 10: Thinking, Intelligence and Problem-Solving

Thinking is a cognitive process through which individuals manipulate information, form ideas, make judgments and solve problems. Intelligence involves abilities related to learning, reasoning, adaptation and understanding. Together, these processes help individuals interpret situations and make decisions.

## 1. Meaning of Thinking

Thinking is a mental process involving the manipulation and organisation of information.

It includes:

- Reasoning.
- Judgement.
- Planning.
- Decision-making.
- Problem-solving.
- Imagination.
- Concept formation.

Thinking allows individuals to go beyond immediate sensory experiences and mentally examine possibilities.

## 2. Concepts and Categories

A concept is a mental representation of a category, object, event or idea.

Examples include:

- Animal.
- Vehicle.
- Justice.
- Intelligence.
- Mathematics.

Concepts help organise information and simplify understanding.

### 2.1 Prototypes

A prototype is a typical or representative example of a category.

For example, some people may consider a sparrow a more typical bird than a penguin.

### 2.2 Schemas

A schema is an organised mental framework that helps individuals interpret and understand information.

Examples include:

- A restaurant schema.
- A classroom schema.
- A social interaction schema.

Schemas allow efficient processing but may also create expectations that influence interpretation.

## 3. Reasoning

Reasoning involves using information, evidence and logical relationships to reach conclusions.

Two major forms are deductive and inductive reasoning.

### 3.1 Deductive Reasoning

Deductive reasoning moves from general principles to specific conclusions.

Example:

1. All mammals are warm-blooded.
2. A whale is a mammal.
3. Therefore, a whale is warm-blooded.

If the premises are true and the argument is logically valid, the conclusion must be true.

### 3.2 Inductive Reasoning

Inductive reasoning moves from specific observations towards general conclusions.

Example:

1. Several observed metals expand when heated.
2. Therefore, metals generally expand when heated.

Inductive conclusions are probable rather than logically guaranteed.

New evidence may require a generalisation to be revised.

## 4. Problem-Solving

Problem-solving is the process of identifying a problem and developing a suitable solution.

A common problem-solving sequence includes:

1. Identifying the problem.
2. Defining the goal.
3. Gathering relevant information.
4. Generating possible solutions.
5. Evaluating alternatives.
6. Selecting a solution.
7. Applying the solution.
8. Evaluating the result.

### 4.1 Algorithms

An algorithm is a systematic procedure that follows defined steps to solve a problem.

Algorithms can produce reliable solutions when the problem is clearly specified and the procedure is appropriate.

Example: Using a standard mathematical method to solve a quadratic equation.

### 4.2 Heuristics

A heuristic is a mental shortcut or general strategy that helps solve problems quickly.

Examples include:

- Working backwards.
- Breaking a problem into smaller parts.
- Eliminating unlikely alternatives.
- Using previous experience.

Heuristics are efficient but do not always produce correct answers.

## 5. Obstacles to Problem-Solving

### 5.1 Functional Fixedness

Functional fixedness is the tendency to view an object only in terms of its familiar use.

Example: Failing to recognise that a container can be used as a tool because it is normally associated with storing objects.

### 5.2 Mental Set

A mental set is the tendency to use previously successful strategies even when a different approach would be more effective.

### 5.3 Confirmation Bias

Confirmation bias is the tendency to favour information that supports existing beliefs while neglecting contradictory evidence.

### 5.4 Fixation

Fixation occurs when an individual becomes mentally stuck on a particular approach and struggles to consider alternatives.

## 6. Decision-Making

Decision-making involves selecting an option from available alternatives.

It may involve:

- Identifying choices.
- Evaluating consequences.
- Estimating risks.
- Considering preferences.
- Comparing possible outcomes.

### 6.1 Rational Decision-Making

A rational approach attempts to evaluate alternatives logically using relevant evidence and clearly defined objectives.

### 6.2 Bounded Rationality

Bounded rationality, associated with Herbert Simon, recognises that decision-making is limited by available information, time and cognitive resources.

Individuals may select an option that is sufficiently satisfactory rather than identifying the theoretically perfect solution.

### 6.3 Intuitive Decision-Making

Intuitive decisions are made through rapid judgements influenced by experience and automatic processing.

Intuition can be useful in familiar situations but may be unreliable when important information is missing or misleading.

## 7. Cognitive Biases and Heuristics

### 7.1 Availability Heuristic

The availability heuristic involves judging the likelihood of an event based on how easily examples come to mind.

Example: After hearing about several aircraft accidents, an individual may overestimate how frequently they occur.

### 7.2 Representativeness Heuristic

The representativeness heuristic involves judging probability based on similarity to a typical example.

This can cause people to ignore important statistical information.

### 7.3 Anchoring Bias

Anchoring occurs when individuals rely too heavily on an initial piece of information while making a judgement.

Example: An initial price influences how reasonable a later price appears.

### 7.4 Overconfidence Bias

Overconfidence occurs when individuals have greater confidence in their judgements or abilities than the available evidence justifies.

## 8. Meaning of Intelligence

Intelligence is a complex psychological construct involving abilities such as learning, reasoning, understanding, adaptation and problem-solving.

There is no single definition that captures every aspect of intelligence.

Psychologists have developed several theories to explain its structure.

## 9. Major Theories of Intelligence

### 9.1 Spearman's Two-Factor Theory

Charles Spearman proposed that intellectual performance involves two factors.

**General intelligence (g):** A broad ability contributing to performance across different cognitive tasks.

**Specific ability (s):** An ability associated with a particular task.

For example, performance in mathematics may involve general reasoning ability and specific mathematical skills.

### 9.2 Thurstone's Primary Mental Abilities

Louis Thurstone proposed several relatively distinct mental abilities.

Important abilities include:

- Verbal comprehension.
- Word fluency.
- Numerical ability.
- Spatial ability.
- Associative memory.
- Perceptual speed.
- Reasoning.

### 9.3 Gardner's Multiple Intelligences

Howard Gardner proposed that intelligence involves multiple relatively distinct capacities.

His proposed categories include:

1. Linguistic.
2. Logical-mathematical.
3. Spatial.
4. Musical.
5. Bodily-kinaesthetic.
6. Interpersonal.
7. Intrapersonal.
8. Naturalistic.

This framework is influential in educational discussions, although its status as a scientifically validated measurement model remains debated.

### 9.4 Sternberg's Triarchic Theory

Robert Sternberg proposed three broad aspects of intelligence.

**Analytical intelligence:** Analysing, evaluating and solving problems.

**Creative intelligence:** Generating new ideas and adapting to unfamiliar situations.

**Practical intelligence:** Applying knowledge effectively in everyday situations.

## 10. Emotional Intelligence

Emotional intelligence refers broadly to abilities involved in recognising, understanding and managing emotions.

Important components often include:

- Recognising one's emotions.
- Understanding emotional experiences.
- Regulating emotional responses.
- Recognising emotions in others.
- Using emotional information in social interactions.

The term has been developed through several theoretical models, and different models define and measure it differently.

## 11. Intelligence Testing

Intelligence tests are standardised instruments designed to assess selected cognitive abilities.

### 11.1 Intelligence Quotient

The traditional ratio IQ formula is:

\[
IQ=\frac{\text{Mental Age}}{\text{Chronological Age}}\times100
\]

Example:

If a child has a mental age of 10 years and a chronological age of 10 years:

\[
IQ=\frac{10}{10}\times100=100
\]

This is a historical formula and is not the standard method used in most modern intelligence tests.

### 11.2 Deviation IQ

Modern intelligence tests commonly use deviation IQ scores.

These compare an individual's performance with that of a relevant age-based reference group.

A commonly used scale has:

- Mean: 100.
- Standard deviation: 15.

A score of 100 therefore represents the average performance of the reference group.

### 11.3 Characteristics of Good Intelligence Tests

**Standardisation:** Administration and scoring follow consistent procedures.

**Reliability:** Results demonstrate suitable consistency.

**Validity:** The test measures the abilities it is intended to assess.

**Norms:** Scores are interpreted using relevant reference groups.

### 11.4 Limitations of Intelligence Testing

Intelligence tests do not measure every form of human ability.

Performance may be influenced by:

- Education.
- Language.
- Culture.
- Motivation.
- Health.
- Testing conditions.
- Previous experience.

Test scores should not be treated as complete measures of an individual's potential or worth.

## 12. Heredity, Environment and Intelligence

Intellectual development is influenced by both genetic and environmental factors.

**Genetic factors:** Hereditary differences can contribute to variation in cognitive abilities.

**Environmental factors:** Education, nutrition, health, family environment and opportunities influence development.

The interaction between these influences is complex.

Heritability is a population-level statistical concept. It does not indicate the proportion of an individual's intelligence that is genetically determined.

## 13. Creativity

Creativity involves generating ideas or products that are both novel and useful within a particular context.

### 13.1 Divergent Thinking

Divergent thinking involves generating multiple possible answers or approaches.

Example: Finding several different uses for an ordinary object.

### 13.2 Convergent Thinking

Convergent thinking involves narrowing alternatives towards a single appropriate answer.

Example: Solving a mathematical problem with a definite result.

Both forms of thinking contribute to creative problem-solving.

## 14. Summary

- Thinking involves reasoning, planning, judgement and problem-solving.
- Concepts and schemas help organise knowledge.
- Deductive reasoning moves from general principles to specific conclusions.
- Inductive reasoning develops general conclusions from observations.
- Algorithms provide systematic procedures, while heuristics provide mental shortcuts.
- Functional fixedness and mental sets can obstruct problem-solving.
- Decision-making is influenced by cognitive limitations and biases.
- Intelligence includes several cognitive abilities.
- Spearman proposed general and specific intelligence factors.
- Thurstone proposed primary mental abilities.
- Gardner proposed multiple intelligences.
- Sternberg proposed analytical, creative and practical intelligence.
- Modern IQ tests commonly use deviation scores.
- Intelligence is influenced by interactions between heredity and environment.
- Creativity involves both divergent and convergent thinking.
