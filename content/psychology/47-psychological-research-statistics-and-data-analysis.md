# Lesson 47: Psychological Research, Statistics and Data Analysis

Psychology relies on scientific research and statistical analysis to investigate human behaviour and mental processes. Researchers use systematic methods to collect data, test hypotheses, identify relationships and evaluate explanations. This lesson examines research designs, measurement, descriptive statistics, inferential statistics and the interpretation of psychological findings.

## 1. Scientific Research in Psychology

### 1.1 Meaning of Scientific Research

Scientific research is a systematic process of investigating questions through observation, measurement, analysis and evaluation.

### 1.2 Characteristics of Scientific Research

Scientific research aims to be:

- Systematic.
- Empirical.
- Objective.
- Replicable.
- Evidence-based.
- Open to revision.

### 1.3 Research Questions

A research question identifies the specific issue that an investigation aims to examine.

Example:

Does sleep duration influence memory performance?

### 1.4 Hypotheses

A hypothesis is a testable prediction about a relationship or difference between variables.

A research hypothesis might predict that individuals who receive sufficient sleep will perform better on a memory test.

## 2. Variables in Psychological Research

### 2.1 Independent Variable

The independent variable is the variable manipulated or used as a predictor in a study.

### 2.2 Dependent Variable

The dependent variable is the outcome measured in response to the independent variable or predictor.

### 2.3 Control Variables

Control variables are factors that researchers hold constant or account for to reduce alternative explanations.

### 2.4 Confounding Variables

A confounding variable is associated with both the predictor and outcome in a way that can distort the interpretation of their relationship.

### 2.5 Operational Definitions

An operational definition explains precisely how a variable is measured or manipulated.

For example, memory performance may be operationally defined as the number of words correctly recalled from a list of 20 words.

## 3. Research Designs

### 3.1 Experimental Research

Experimental research involves manipulating an independent variable and measuring its effects on an outcome.

Random assignment can help balance participant characteristics across experimental conditions.

### 3.2 Correlational Research

Correlational research examines the relationship between variables without necessarily manipulating them.

It can identify associations but does not independently establish causation.

### 3.3 Descriptive Research

Descriptive research systematically records and describes characteristics of individuals, groups or situations.

Methods include surveys, observations and case studies.

### 3.4 Longitudinal Research

Longitudinal research collects data from the same individuals or groups across multiple time points.

It can help examine developmental changes and long-term patterns.

### 3.5 Cross-Sectional Research

Cross-sectional research collects information from different individuals or groups at a particular point in time.

It is generally faster than longitudinal research but may not reveal individual developmental changes.

## 4. Sampling Methods

### 4.1 Population and Sample

A population is the complete group that a researcher wants to study.

A sample is the subset of individuals selected from that population.

### 4.2 Simple Random Sampling

Every member of the population has an equal probability of selection.

### 4.3 Stratified Sampling

The population is divided into relevant subgroups, and participants are sampled from each subgroup.

### 4.4 Cluster Sampling

The population is divided into clusters, and selected clusters are sampled.

### 4.5 Convenience Sampling

Participants are selected based on accessibility or availability.

It is relatively easy to conduct but may produce sampling bias.

### 4.6 Sampling Bias

Sampling bias occurs when certain members of the population are more likely to be included than others.

It can reduce the representativeness of a sample.

## 5. Measurement Scales

### 5.1 Nominal Scale

Nominal measurement classifies data into categories without a meaningful numerical order.

Example: types of psychological treatment.

### 5.2 Ordinal Scale

Ordinal measurement places observations in an ordered arrangement, but differences between ranks are not necessarily equal.

Example: satisfaction ratings from low to high.

### 5.3 Interval Scale

Interval measurement has equal intervals between values but no absolute zero.

Example: temperature measured in degrees Celsius.

### 5.4 Ratio Scale

Ratio measurement has equal intervals and a meaningful absolute zero.

Example: reaction time measured in milliseconds.

## 6. Descriptive Statistics

### 6.1 Meaning

Descriptive statistics summarise and organise collected data.

They help researchers understand the main characteristics of a dataset.

### 6.2 Mean

The mean is the arithmetic average.

\[
\bar{x}=\frac{\sum x_i}{n}
\]

Where:

- \(\bar{x}\) = mean.
- \(x_i\) = individual observations.
- \(n\) = number of observations.

### 6.3 Median

The median is the middle value when observations are arranged in ascending or descending order.

For an even number of observations, it is the average of the two middle values.

### 6.4 Mode

The mode is the most frequently occurring value.

### 6.5 Measures of Variability

Variability describes how spread out the observations are.

Important measures include:

- Range.
- Variance.
- Standard deviation.
- Interquartile range.

### 6.6 Standard Deviation

Standard deviation measures the typical spread of values around the mean.

For a population:

\[
\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{\sum(x_i-\mu)^2}{N}}
\]

For a sample:

\[
s=\sqrt{\frac{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2}{n-1}}
\]

## 7. Probability and Statistical Inference

### 7.1 Probability

Probability measures the likelihood of an event.

Its value ranges from 0 to 1.

### 7.2 Population and Sample Statistics

A parameter describes a population characteristic.

A statistic describes a characteristic calculated from a sample.

Researchers use sample statistics to make inferences about populations.

### 7.3 Sampling Distribution

A sampling distribution describes the probability distribution of a statistic across repeated samples drawn under specified conditions.

### 7.4 Standard Error

The standard error measures the variability of a sample statistic across repeated samples.

For the sample mean:

\[
SE_{\bar{x}}=\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
\]

### 7.5 Confidence Interval

A confidence interval provides a range of plausible values for a population parameter under a specified statistical procedure.

A 95% confidence interval is constructed by a method that captures the true parameter in approximately 95% of repeated samples, under the method's assumptions.

## 8. Hypothesis Testing

### 8.1 Null Hypothesis

The null hypothesis, represented by \(H_0\), generally states that there is no effect, difference or relationship of the specified kind.

### 8.2 Alternative Hypothesis

The alternative hypothesis, represented by \(H_1\) or \(H_a\), states that an effect, difference or relationship exists.

### 8.3 Significance Level

The significance level, represented by \(\alpha\), is the chosen threshold for controlling the probability of a Type I error.

A common value is:

\[
\alpha=0.05
\]

### 8.4 P-Value

The p-value is the probability, assuming the null hypothesis and statistical model are correct, of obtaining results at least as extreme as those observed.

A small p-value indicates that the observed data would be relatively unusual under the null hypothesis.

### 8.5 Type I Error

A Type I error occurs when a true null hypothesis is rejected.

### 8.6 Type II Error

A Type II error occurs when a false null hypothesis is not rejected.

### 8.7 Statistical Significance

A statistically significant result indicates that the observed data meet a chosen statistical decision threshold.

It does not automatically imply that the effect is large, important or practically meaningful.

## 9. Common Statistical Tests

### 9.1 Independent-Samples t-Test

An independent-samples t-test compares the means of two independent groups.

Example: comparing average memory scores between two separate groups.

### 9.2 Paired-Samples t-Test

A paired-samples t-test compares two related measurements.

Example: comparing participants' scores before and after an intervention.

### 9.3 Analysis of Variance

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) compares means across two or more groups.

A significant result indicates evidence that not all group means are equal, but additional analysis may be needed to identify which groups differ.

### 9.4 Chi-Square Test

A chi-square test can examine associations between categorical variables.

Example: examining whether treatment preference differs across age groups.

### 9.5 Correlation Analysis

Correlation measures the strength and direction of association between variables.

The Pearson correlation coefficient is commonly represented by \(r\).

\[
-1\leq r\leq1
\]

A positive value indicates a positive linear association, while a negative value indicates a negative linear association.

A value near zero indicates little or no linear association.

## 10. Effect Size

### 10.1 Meaning

Effect size describes the magnitude of a relationship, difference or effect.

### 10.2 Cohen's d

Cohen's d is a standardised measure of the difference between two means.

For independent groups with a pooled standard deviation:

\[
d=\frac{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}{s_p}
\]

### 10.3 Importance

Effect sizes help researchers understand practical importance beyond statistical significance.

A very small effect may become statistically significant in a sufficiently large sample.

## 11. Data Interpretation and Visualisation

### 11.1 Frequency Distribution

A frequency distribution shows how often different values or categories occur.

### 11.2 Histograms

Histograms display the distribution of numerical data using adjacent bars.

### 11.3 Bar Charts

Bar charts display frequencies or values for separate categories.

### 11.4 Scatterplots

Scatterplots show the relationship between two numerical variables.

### 11.5 Box Plots

Box plots summarise a distribution using the median, quartiles and potential outliers.

## 12. Research Ethics

### 12.1 Informed Consent

Participants should understand the purpose, procedures and potential risks of a study before agreeing to participate.

### 12.2 Confidentiality

Personal information should be protected and handled responsibly.

### 12.3 Protection from Harm

Researchers must minimise unnecessary physical, psychological and social risks.

### 12.4 Right to Withdraw

Participants should generally be free to withdraw from research without inappropriate consequences.

### 12.5 Debriefing

Debriefing involves explaining relevant aspects of the study after participation, particularly when information was withheld or deception was used under approved conditions.

## 13. Summary

Psychological research uses systematic designs and statistical methods to investigate human behaviour. Variables, sampling, measurement scales and research designs determine how data are collected. Descriptive statistics summarise observations, while inferential statistics support conclusions about populations. Reliability, validity, effect size, ethical standards and careful interpretation are essential for scientific psychological research.
