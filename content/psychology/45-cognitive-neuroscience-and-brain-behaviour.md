# Lesson 45: Cognitive Neuroscience and Brain-Behaviour Relationships

Cognitive neuroscience studies the relationship between brain activity and mental processes such as attention, memory, language, emotion and decision-making. It combines psychological theories with neuroscience methods to understand how the nervous system supports cognition and behaviour.

## 1. Introduction to Cognitive Neuroscience

### 1.1 Definition

Cognitive neuroscience is an interdisciplinary field that investigates the neural mechanisms underlying cognitive processes.

It combines knowledge from:

- Psychology.
- Neuroscience.
- Biology.
- Medicine.
- Computer science.
- Cognitive science.

### 1.2 Central Questions

Cognitive neuroscience examines questions such as:

- How does the brain produce conscious experiences?
- How are memories formed and retrieved?
- How does the brain recognise objects?
- How are decisions made?
- How do emotions influence reasoning?

## 2. Organisation of the Nervous System

### 2.1 Central Nervous System

The central nervous system consists of the brain and spinal cord.

It processes information and coordinates many bodily and psychological functions.

### 2.2 Peripheral Nervous System

The peripheral nervous system connects the central nervous system with the rest of the body.

It includes:

- Somatic nervous system.
- Autonomic nervous system.

### 2.3 Autonomic Nervous System

The autonomic nervous system regulates many involuntary processes.

Its major divisions include:

**Sympathetic division:** Mobilises the body during demanding situations.

**Parasympathetic division:** Supports restorative functions and energy conservation.

## 3. Major Brain Structures

### 3.1 Cerebral Cortex

The cerebral cortex is the outer layer of the cerebrum and is involved in complex cognitive processes.

### 3.2 Frontal Lobe

The frontal lobe contributes to:

- Planning.
- Decision-making.
- Voluntary movement.
- Working memory.
- Inhibitory control.
- Social behaviour.

### 3.3 Parietal Lobe

The parietal lobe contributes to:

- Touch processing.
- Spatial awareness.
- Body representation.
- Integration of sensory information.

### 3.4 Temporal Lobe

The temporal lobe is involved in:

- Auditory processing.
- Language comprehension.
- Memory.
- Recognition of objects and faces.

### 3.5 Occipital Lobe

The occipital lobe is primarily involved in visual processing.

## 4. Important Subcortical Structures

### 4.1 Thalamus

The thalamus acts as an important relay and processing centre for much sensory information travelling to the cerebral cortex.

### 4.2 Hypothalamus

The hypothalamus contributes to:

- Hunger.
- Thirst.
- Temperature regulation.
- Hormonal control.
- Sleep-wake regulation.

### 4.3 Hippocampus

The hippocampus is essential for the formation and organisation of new declarative memories.

It is particularly important in spatial and episodic memory.

### 4.4 Amygdala

The amygdala contributes to emotional processing, threat detection and emotional learning.

### 4.5 Basal Ganglia

The basal ganglia contribute to movement regulation, habit learning and action selection.

### 4.6 Cerebellum

The cerebellum is important for coordination, balance, motor learning and aspects of cognitive functioning.

## 5. Neurons and Neural Communication

### 5.1 Structure of a Neuron

A typical neuron consists of:

- Dendrites.
- Cell body.
- Axon.
- Axon terminals.

### 5.2 Action Potential

An action potential is a rapid electrical signal that travels along the axon.

It involves changes in the electrical potential across the neuronal membrane.

### 5.3 Synaptic Transmission

At chemical synapses, neurotransmitters are released from the presynaptic neuron and interact with receptors on the postsynaptic cell.

### 5.4 Major Neurotransmitters

| Neurotransmitter | Important Functions |
|---|---|
| Dopamine | Motivation, movement and reward learning |
| Serotonin | Mood regulation and other physiological processes |
| Acetylcholine | Attention, memory and muscle activation |
| GABA | Major inhibitory neurotransmission |
| Glutamate | Major excitatory neurotransmission |
| Noradrenaline | Alertness and stress-related responses |

## 6. Neural Basis of Cognitive Processes

### 6.1 Attention

Attention involves selecting relevant information for further processing.

It depends on coordinated activity across multiple brain regions, including frontal and parietal networks.

### 6.2 Memory

Memory depends on interactions among several brain structures.

The hippocampus plays an important role in forming new declarative memories, while other systems contribute to procedural and emotional learning.

### 6.3 Language

Language depends on distributed neural networks.

Classically, two important regions are:

- Broca's area, associated with speech production and language expression.
- Wernicke's area, associated with language comprehension.

Modern research shows that language depends on broader networks rather than two isolated regions.

### 6.4 Executive Functions

Executive functions include:

- Planning.
- Working memory.
- Cognitive flexibility.
- Inhibitory control.
- Goal-directed behaviour.

The prefrontal cortex plays a major role in these processes.

### 6.5 Emotion

Emotional processing involves interactions among the amygdala, prefrontal cortex, hypothalamus and other neural systems.

Emotion is not controlled by a single brain structure.

## 7. Brain Plasticity

### 7.1 Definition

Neuroplasticity refers to the nervous system's ability to change its structure and functioning in response to experience, learning or injury.

### 7.2 Synaptic Plasticity

Synaptic plasticity involves changes in the strength or effectiveness of communication between neurons.

### 7.3 Long-Term Potentiation

Long-term potentiation (LTP) is a lasting increase in synaptic strength following certain patterns of neural activity.

It is an important mechanism studied in learning and memory.

### 7.4 Long-Term Depression

Long-term depression (LTD) involves a lasting reduction in synaptic strength.

Both LTP and LTD contribute to the modification of neural networks.

### 7.5 Experience-Dependent Plasticity

Learning, practice and environmental experiences can modify neural connections.

This helps explain how experience influences brain functioning.

## 8. Research Methods in Cognitive Neuroscience

### 8.1 Electroencephalography

Electroencephalography (EEG) measures electrical activity from the scalp using electrodes.

It provides excellent temporal resolution.

### 8.2 Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging

Functional MRI (fMRI) measures changes associated with blood oxygenation and blood flow.

It provides information about brain activity indirectly through the blood-oxygen-level-dependent signal.

### 8.3 Positron Emission Tomography

Positron emission tomography (PET) uses radioactive tracers to study metabolic activity or particular biological processes.

### 8.4 Event-Related Potentials

Event-related potentials (ERPs) are changes in EEG activity associated with specific sensory, cognitive or motor events.

### 8.5 Lesion Studies

Lesion studies examine how damage to particular brain areas affects functioning.

They provide evidence about the relationship between brain structures and psychological processes.

## 9. Interpreting Brain Research

### 9.1 Temporal Resolution

Temporal resolution refers to how precisely a method measures changes over time.

EEG has high temporal resolution.

### 9.2 Spatial Resolution

Spatial resolution refers to how precisely a method identifies the location of activity.

fMRI generally provides better spatial resolution than EEG.

### 9.3 Correlation and Causation

Observing activity in a brain region during a task does not automatically prove that the region causes the behaviour.

Researchers must consider alternative explanations and use multiple methods.

## 10. Applications

Cognitive neuroscience contributes to:

- Understanding neurological disorders.
- Improving rehabilitation.
- Studying learning and memory.
- Developing brain-computer interfaces.
- Investigating attention and perception.
- Understanding decision-making.
- Designing educational interventions.

## 11. Summary

Cognitive neuroscience examines the neural foundations of cognition and behaviour. Different brain structures contribute to memory, attention, language, emotion and decision-making through interconnected networks. Methods such as EEG, fMRI, PET and lesion studies provide complementary information about brain functioning. Neuroplasticity explains how experience can modify neural connections and support learning.
