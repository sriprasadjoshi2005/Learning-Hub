# Lesson 3: Research Methods in Psychology

Psychology uses scientific research methods to investigate behaviour and mental processes. Researchers formulate questions, collect evidence, analyse observations and evaluate explanations. Understanding research methods helps distinguish scientific findings from assumptions and unsupported claims.

## 1. Scientific Research in Psychology

Scientific research is a systematic process used to investigate questions and develop evidence-based explanations.

A typical research process involves:

1. Identifying a research problem.
2. Reviewing existing knowledge.
3. Formulating a research question.
4. Developing a hypothesis.
5. Selecting an appropriate research method.
6. Collecting data.
7. Analysing results.
8. Drawing conclusions.
9. Reporting findings.

Research is often iterative. New findings may lead researchers to revise existing explanations or conduct further investigations.

## 2. Hypotheses and Variables

### 2.1 Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a testable prediction about a relationship between variables or an expected research outcome.

Example:

Students who sleep for longer periods may perform better on certain memory tasks than students who sleep for shorter periods.

A scientific hypothesis must be capable of being tested through observation or experimentation.

### 2.2 Variables

A variable is any characteristic or condition that can take different values.

Common types include:

**Independent variable:** The variable manipulated or categorised by a researcher to examine its influence.

**Dependent variable:** The outcome measured in a study.

**Controlled variables:** Conditions kept constant to reduce unwanted influences.

**Confounding variable:** An additional factor that may influence the observed relationship between variables.

Example:

A researcher investigates whether background music affects memory performance.

- Independent variable: Background music condition.
- Dependent variable: Memory test score.
- Controlled variables: Testing duration and instructions.
- Possible confounding variable: Differences in participants' prior memory ability.

## 3. Methods of Psychological Research

### 3.1 Experimental Method

An experiment investigates cause-and-effect relationships by manipulating an independent variable and measuring its effect on a dependent variable.

Participants may be assigned to different conditions.

- **Experimental group:** Receives the treatment or condition being investigated.
- **Control group:** Provides a comparison condition.

Random assignment can help distribute individual differences across groups.

**Example:** Investigating whether a particular study technique improves test performance.

**Advantage:** Carefully controlled experiments can provide evidence about causal relationships.

**Limitation:** Some psychological questions cannot be ethically or practically investigated through experiments.

### 3.2 Observational Method

Observation involves systematically recording behaviour.

Types include:

- **Naturalistic observation:** Behaviour is observed in its usual environment.
- **Controlled observation:** Behaviour is observed under arranged conditions.
- **Participant observation:** The researcher participates in the group being studied.
- **Non-participant observation:** The researcher observes without participating.

**Advantage:** Provides information about actual behaviour in different settings.

**Limitation:** Observers may influence behaviour or interpret observations subjectively.

### 3.3 Survey Method

Surveys collect information through questionnaires or interviews.

They can investigate:

- Attitudes.
- Opinions.
- Habits.
- Experiences.
- Self-reported behaviour.

**Advantage:** Information can be collected from many participants relatively efficiently.

**Limitation:** Responses may be influenced by memory errors, misunderstanding or social desirability bias.

### 3.4 Case Study Method

A case study is an intensive investigation of an individual, group, organisation or specific situation.

Researchers may use interviews, observations, records and psychological assessments.

**Advantage:** Provides detailed information about complex or unusual cases.

**Limitation:** Findings from one case may not apply to the wider population.

### 3.5 Correlational Method

Correlation measures the strength and direction of an association between variables.

The correlation coefficient, represented by \(r\), ranges from -1 to +1.

\[
-1 \leq r \leq +1
\]

- \(r=+1\): Perfect positive correlation.
- \(r=-1\): Perfect negative correlation.
- \(r=0\): No linear correlation.

**Positive correlation:** As one variable increases, the other tends to increase.

**Negative correlation:** As one variable increases, the other tends to decrease.

**Zero correlation:** No linear relationship is observed.

A correlation does not automatically establish causation.

For example, ice cream sales and swimming activity may both increase during warmer weather. This does not mean that ice cream consumption causes swimming.

Temperature may influence both variables.

## 4. Sampling Methods

A population is the entire group a researcher wants to investigate.

A sample is a smaller group selected from that population.

### 4.1 Random Sampling

Every member of the population has a known chance of selection, commonly an equal chance in simple random sampling.

### 4.2 Stratified Sampling

The population is divided into relevant subgroups, and participants are selected from each subgroup.

Example: Selecting participants from different age groups.

### 4.3 Systematic Sampling

Participants are selected according to a fixed interval from an ordered list.

### 4.4 Convenience Sampling

Participants are selected because they are easily accessible.

It is convenient but may introduce selection bias.

## 5. Data Collection and Measurement

### 5.1 Quantitative Data

Quantitative data are expressed numerically.

Examples include:

- Reaction time.
- Test scores.
- Age.
- Number of errors.

### 5.2 Qualitative Data

Qualitative data describe experiences, meanings or characteristics.

Examples include interview responses, personal narratives and descriptions of emotional experiences.

### 5.3 Operational Definition

An operational definition explains exactly how a variable is measured or manipulated in a particular study.

For example, instead of vaguely defining attention, a researcher may define it as the number of correctly identified target symbols during a timed task.

Operational definitions make research procedures clearer and more reproducible.

## 6. Reliability and Validity

### 6.1 Reliability

Reliability refers to the consistency of a measurement or research instrument.

A reliable test should produce reasonably consistent results under comparable conditions.

Types include:

- Test-retest reliability.
- Inter-rater reliability.
- Internal consistency.

### 6.2 Validity

Validity refers to whether a measurement or study actually measures or investigates what it claims to measure.

Important types include:

- **Content validity:** Whether the instrument adequately covers the relevant subject.
- **Construct validity:** Whether it measures the intended theoretical concept.
- **Criterion validity:** Whether results relate appropriately to an external criterion.
- **Internal validity:** Whether the study supports the proposed causal conclusion.
- **External validity:** Whether findings can be generalised to other populations or settings.

A measurement can be reliable without being valid. Consistency alone does not guarantee accuracy.

## 7. Research Ethics

Psychological research must protect participants and respect their rights.

Important principles include:

### 7.1 Informed Consent

Participants should receive sufficient information to make an informed decision about participation.

### 7.2 Voluntary Participation

Individuals should not be forced or improperly pressured to participate.

### 7.3 Right to Withdraw

Participants should generally be allowed to leave a study without unfair consequences.

### 7.4 Confidentiality

Personal information should be protected and handled responsibly.

### 7.5 Protection from Harm

Researchers should minimise physical, psychological and social risks.

### 7.6 Debriefing

After a study, researchers may explain its purpose and procedures, particularly when some information had to be withheld temporarily.

Deception, when permitted, must be justified, carefully limited and followed by appropriate safeguards.

## 8. Common Research Errors and Biases

- **Confirmation bias:** Favouring evidence that supports existing beliefs.
- **Sampling bias:** Selecting participants in a way that produces an unrepresentative sample.
- **Observer bias:** Personal expectations influencing observations.
- **Social desirability bias:** Participants providing answers they believe are socially acceptable.
- **Recall bias:** Inaccuracies in remembering past experiences.
- **Experimenter expectancy effect:** Researcher expectations influencing study outcomes.

Researchers use standardised procedures, blinding, appropriate sampling and replication to reduce these problems.

## 9. Summary

- Psychological research follows systematic scientific procedures.
- A hypothesis is a testable prediction.
- Independent variables are manipulated or categorised, while dependent variables are measured.
- Experiments investigate causal relationships.
- Observation, surveys, case studies and correlational studies provide different forms of evidence.
- Sampling determines which members of a population participate.
- Reliability concerns consistency, while validity concerns whether a measurement is appropriate.
- Ethical research protects participants and their rights.
- Correlation alone does not establish causation.
