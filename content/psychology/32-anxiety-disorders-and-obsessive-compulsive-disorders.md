# Lesson 32: Anxiety Disorders and Obsessive-Compulsive Disorders

Anxiety is a normal response to perceived threats and uncertainty. It becomes a psychological concern when fear, worry or repetitive behaviours become excessive, persistent and disruptive. This lesson examines anxiety disorders and obsessive-compulsive and related disorders.

## 1. Understanding Anxiety

### 1.1 Meaning of Anxiety

Anxiety is a state of apprehension or worry about a possible future threat.

It may involve:

- Increased heart rate.
- Muscle tension.
- Restlessness.
- Difficulty concentrating.
- Excessive worrying.
- Avoidance of feared situations.

### 1.2 Fear and Anxiety

Fear is generally a response to an immediate threat, whereas anxiety involves anticipation of possible future danger.

### 1.3 The Fight-or-Flight Response

The fight-or-flight response prepares the body to respond to danger.

The sympathetic nervous system becomes active, producing changes such as increased heart rate, faster breathing and heightened alertness.

## 2. Major Anxiety Disorders

### 2.1 Generalised Anxiety Disorder

Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) involves persistent and excessive worry about several areas of life.

Common features include:

- Difficulty controlling worry.
- Restlessness.
- Fatigue.
- Muscle tension.
- Sleep difficulties.
- Irritability.

### 2.2 Panic Disorder

Panic disorder involves recurrent unexpected panic attacks and persistent concern about experiencing additional attacks or their consequences.

A panic attack may involve intense fear, sweating, trembling, chest discomfort and a feeling of losing control.

### 2.3 Specific Phobias

A specific phobia involves intense and persistent fear of a particular object or situation.

Examples include:

- Fear of heights.
- Fear of spiders.
- Fear of enclosed spaces.
- Fear of flying.

### 2.4 Social Anxiety Disorder

Social anxiety disorder involves intense fear of social situations in which an individual may be judged, embarrassed or negatively evaluated.

Common situations include:

- Speaking in front of an audience.
- Meeting unfamiliar people.
- Eating in public.
- Participating in group discussions.

### 2.5 Agoraphobia

Agoraphobia involves significant fear or anxiety about situations where escape might be difficult or help might not be available if panic-like symptoms occur.

Examples include using public transportation, being in crowded places and being outside alone.

## 3. Understanding Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder

### 3.1 Obsessions

Obsessions are recurrent and persistent thoughts, urges or mental images that are experienced as intrusive and unwanted.

Examples include:

- Repeated fears of contamination.
- Persistent doubts about whether a door is locked.
- Unwanted thoughts about causing harm.
- Excessive concern about symmetry or order.

### 3.2 Compulsions

Compulsions are repetitive behaviours or mental acts that an individual feels driven to perform in response to an obsession or according to rigid rules.

Examples include:

- Repeated handwashing.
- Checking locks repeatedly.
- Counting objects.
- Arranging items in a particular order.
- Repeating certain phrases mentally.

### 3.3 Obsession-Compulsion Cycle

The relationship between obsessions and compulsions can be understood through a repeating cycle:

1. An intrusive thought or obsession appears.
2. The individual experiences anxiety or discomfort.
3. A compulsion is performed to reduce the discomfort.
4. Temporary relief occurs.
5. The cycle may repeat when the obsession returns.

The temporary relief can reinforce compulsive behaviour.

## 4. Related Disorders

### 4.1 Body Dysmorphic Disorder

Body dysmorphic disorder involves persistent preoccupation with perceived defects in physical appearance that are not noticeable or appear minor to others.

It may involve repeated mirror checking, excessive grooming or comparing one's appearance with others.

### 4.2 Hoarding Disorder

Hoarding disorder involves persistent difficulty discarding possessions, regardless of their actual value.

The accumulation of belongings may make living spaces difficult to use safely.

### 4.3 Trichotillomania

Trichotillomania, also called hair-pulling disorder, involves repeated pulling out of one's hair, resulting in hair loss or significant distress.

### 4.4 Excoriation Disorder

Excoriation disorder involves recurrent skin picking that causes skin lesions and significant distress or impairment.

## 5. Psychological Explanations

### 5.1 Behavioural Perspective

The behavioural perspective explains some anxiety responses through learning.

For example, a person who experiences a frightening event in an enclosed space may develop a fear of similar environments.

Avoidance may be reinforced because it temporarily reduces anxiety.

### 5.2 Cognitive Perspective

The cognitive perspective emphasises how individuals interpret situations.

An individual with anxiety may:

- Overestimate the likelihood of danger.
- Underestimate their ability to cope.
- Interpret uncertain situations as threatening.
- Focus excessively on possible negative outcomes.

### 5.3 Biological Perspective

Biological explanations examine:

- Genetic vulnerability.
- Brain circuits involved in fear and threat processing.
- Neurotransmitter systems.
- Stress-response mechanisms.

The amygdala is particularly important in processing emotional significance and detecting potential threats.

## 6. Treatment Approaches

### 6.1 Cognitive Behavioural Therapy

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) helps individuals identify unhelpful thinking patterns and develop healthier behavioural responses.

It may involve:

- Identifying distorted thoughts.
- Examining evidence for feared predictions.
- Developing realistic interpretations.
- Gradually changing avoidance behaviours.

### 6.2 Exposure Therapy

Exposure therapy involves gradually and systematically facing feared situations in a controlled and supportive manner.

It can help individuals develop new learning about feared situations and reduce avoidance.

### 6.3 Exposure and Response Prevention

Exposure and response prevention (ERP) is an important treatment approach for OCD.

It involves exposure to triggers while resisting the urge to perform compulsive behaviours.

With appropriate guidance, individuals learn that anxiety can decrease without completing the compulsion.

### 6.4 Medication

Certain medications, including selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), may be prescribed for anxiety disorders and OCD.

Medication decisions require assessment and supervision by qualified healthcare professionals.

## 7. Anxiety and Everyday Functioning

### 7.1 Academic and Occupational Effects

Severe anxiety may interfere with concentration, decision-making, performance and attendance.

### 7.2 Social Effects

Avoidance of social situations may affect friendships, relationships and participation in everyday activities.

### 7.3 Importance of Professional Support

Persistent anxiety and compulsive behaviours that interfere with everyday life should be assessed by qualified mental health professionals.

Treatment can help individuals manage symptoms and improve functioning.

## 8. Summary

Anxiety disorders involve excessive fear, worry or avoidance, while OCD involves obsessions, compulsions or both. Related disorders include body dysmorphic disorder, hoarding disorder, trichotillomania and excoriation disorder. Psychological, behavioural and biological factors contribute to these conditions. Treatments such as CBT, exposure therapy and ERP can help individuals manage symptoms.
