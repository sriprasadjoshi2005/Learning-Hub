# Lesson 5: Sensation and Sensory Processes

Sensation is the process through which sensory receptors detect information from the environment and convert it into signals that can be processed by the nervous system. It provides the biological foundation for experiencing light, sound, temperature, pressure, taste, smell and other forms of stimulation.

## 1. Meaning of Sensation

Sensation is the initial process of detecting physical or chemical stimulation through sensory receptors.

Examples include:

- Detecting light through the eyes.
- Detecting sound waves through the ears.
- Detecting pressure through the skin.
- Detecting chemicals through taste and smell receptors.

Sensation is closely related to perception, but the two terms describe different stages of information processing.

**Sensation:** Detection of stimulation.

**Perception:** Organisation and interpretation of sensory information.

## 2. Sensory Receptors

Sensory receptors are specialised cells or structures that detect particular forms of stimulation.

| Receptor type | Detects |
|---|---|
| Photoreceptors | Light |
| Mechanoreceptors | Mechanical pressure, vibration and movement |
| Chemoreceptors | Chemical substances |
| Thermoreceptors | Temperature |
| Nociceptors | Potentially harmful stimuli associated with pain |

### 2.1 Transduction

Sensory transduction is the process through which sensory receptors convert physical or chemical energy into electrical signals.

For example, photoreceptors in the retina convert light-related stimulation into neural signals.

These signals are then transmitted through sensory pathways for further processing.

## 3. Vision

Vision is the sensory system responsible for detecting and processing light.

### 3.1 Structure of the Eye

Important structures include:

- **Cornea:** Transparent outer surface that helps refract incoming light.
- **Iris:** Coloured structure that controls pupil size.
- **Pupil:** Opening through which light enters the eye.
- **Lens:** Changes shape to help focus light on the retina.
- **Retina:** Light-sensitive layer containing photoreceptors.
- **Optic nerve:** Carries visual information towards the brain.

### 3.2 Photoreceptors

The retina contains two major types of photoreceptors.

**Rods:**

- Highly sensitive to low light.
- Important for night vision.
- Do not provide detailed colour vision.

**Cones:**

- Function best in brighter conditions.
- Important for colour vision.
- Support detailed visual perception.

### 3.3 Colour Vision

Human colour vision is primarily based on three types of cones with different sensitivities to wavelengths of light.

The brain combines information from these receptors to produce colour experiences.

The trichromatic theory explains colour vision through the activity of three cone types.

The opponent-process theory explains colour processing through opposing channels, such as red-green and blue-yellow.

These theories describe complementary aspects of colour vision.

## 4. Hearing

Hearing is the process of detecting sound waves and interpreting them as auditory experiences.

### 4.1 Structure of the Ear

The ear has three main divisions.

**Outer ear:**

- Collects sound waves.
- Directs them towards the eardrum.

**Middle ear:**

Contains three small bones:

- Malleus.
- Incus.
- Stapes.

These bones transmit and amplify vibrations.

**Inner ear:**

The cochlea contains specialised structures that convert mechanical vibrations into neural signals.

The auditory nerve carries information towards the brain.

### 4.2 Properties of Sound

**Frequency:** Number of sound-wave cycles per second, measured in hertz (Hz). It is associated with pitch.

**Amplitude:** Magnitude of sound-wave variation. It is associated with loudness.

**Timbre:** Quality that helps distinguish sounds with similar pitch and loudness.

## 5. Taste

Taste is a chemical sense that detects substances dissolved in saliva.

Taste receptors are located mainly in taste buds.

The five commonly recognised basic tastes are:

1. Sweet.
2. Sour.
3. Salty.
4. Bitter.
5. Umami.

Umami is associated with the taste of certain amino acids, particularly glutamate.

Taste contributes to food selection and can help detect potentially harmful substances.

## 6. Smell

Smell, or olfaction, detects airborne chemical molecules.

Odour molecules enter the nasal cavity and interact with olfactory receptors.

These receptors transmit information to the olfactory bulb and other brain regions.

Smell contributes to:

- Identifying food.
- Detecting environmental hazards.
- Emotional experiences.
- Memory associations.

Smell and taste interact to produce the overall experience of flavour.

## 7. Touch and Skin Sensation

The skin contains different receptors that detect several types of stimulation.

These include:

- Pressure.
- Vibration.
- Temperature.
- Texture.
- Pain.

Touch information travels through sensory nerves to the spinal cord and brain.

Different regions of the skin have different levels of sensitivity.

For example, fingertips can detect fine differences in texture because they contain a high density of sensory receptors.

## 8. Pain Sensation

Pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage.

Nociceptors detect potentially harmful stimulation.

Pain perception is influenced by both biological and psychological processes.

Factors influencing pain include:

- Tissue damage.
- Nervous system activity.
- Attention.
- Emotion.
- Previous experience.
- Social and cultural context.

Pain is therefore not simply a direct measurement of physical injury.

## 9. Absolute and Difference Thresholds

### 9.1 Absolute Threshold

The absolute threshold is the minimum intensity of a stimulus that can be detected approximately 50% of the time under specified conditions.

Example: The faintest sound a person can detect under particular testing conditions.

### 9.2 Difference Threshold

The difference threshold is the smallest difference between two stimuli that can be detected approximately 50% of the time.

It is also called the just noticeable difference (JND).

### 9.3 Weber's Law

Weber's law states that the detectable difference between two stimuli is approximately proportional to the original stimulus intensity under suitable conditions.

\[
\frac{\Delta I}{I}=k
\]

Where:

- \(\Delta I\) = Minimum detectable change.
- \(I\) = Original stimulus intensity.
- \(k\) = Weber fraction.

Example:

If a 100 g weight requires an additional 2 g to produce a noticeable difference:

\[
k=\frac{2}{100}=0.02
\]

For a 200 g weight, the approximate noticeable difference would be:

\[
\Delta I=0.02(200)=4\text{ g}
\]

This is an approximation and applies within particular sensory ranges.

## 10. Signal Detection Theory

Signal detection theory examines how individuals distinguish meaningful signals from background noise.

Detection depends on both sensory sensitivity and decision-making factors.

For example, a person may detect a faint sound differently depending on whether they expect an important announcement.

Four possible outcomes are:

| Actual condition | Response | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Signal present | Signal detected | Hit |
| Signal present | Signal not detected | Miss |
| Signal absent | Signal detected | False alarm |
| Signal absent | Signal not detected | Correct rejection |

Signal detection theory is useful in medical diagnosis, security screening and sensory research.

## 11. Sensory Adaptation

Sensory adaptation refers to reduced sensitivity to a constant or unchanging stimulus over time.

Examples include:

- Becoming less aware of a perfume smell after entering a room.
- Gradually ignoring the feeling of a watch on the wrist.
- Becoming less aware of continuous background noise.

Adaptation allows attention to become more responsive to changes in the environment.

## 12. Summary

- Sensation involves detecting stimulation through sensory receptors.
- Transduction converts sensory energy into neural signals.
- Vision depends on rods, cones and other structures of the eye.
- Hearing involves the transmission of vibrations through the ear.
- Taste and smell detect chemical stimulation.
- Touch receptors detect pressure, temperature and other forms of stimulation.
- Absolute threshold concerns minimum detectable intensity.
- Difference threshold concerns the smallest detectable change.
- Weber's law relates detectable differences to original stimulus intensity.
- Signal detection theory examines the detection of signals in noise.
- Sensory adaptation reduces sensitivity to constant stimulation.
