Adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions, and interjections perform vital connective, conditional, structural, and modifying roles in English grammar.

## Main Concept 1: Adverbs

An **adverb** is a word that modifies a verb, an adjective, or another adverb. Adverbs express details such as *How? When? Where? How often? To what extent?*

- **Definitions and terminology:** Adverbs of degree alter intensity, while conjunctive adverbs connect independent clauses logically.
- **Important principles and rules:** Many adverbs are formed by adding *-ly* to adjectives (*rapid $\rightarrow$ rapidly*), but many common adverbs are irregular (*fast, well, hard, late*).
- **Classifications and subcategories:**
  - *Adverbs of Manner:* Describe *how* an action occurs (*efficiently, quietly*).
  - *Adverbs of Time and Place:* Describe *when* or *where* (*yesterday, abroad, nearby*).
  - *Adverbs of Frequency:* Describe *how often* (*always, seldom, rarely*).
  - *Adverbs of Degree:* Describe intensity or extent (*extremely, very, quite*).

## Main Concept 2: Prepositions, Conjunctions, and Interjections

### Subtopic 1: Prepositions

A **preposition** shows the spatial, temporal, or logical relationship between a noun or pronoun (its *object*) and other words in the sentence.

- **Prepositional Phrase:** Comprises the preposition + its object + any associated modifiers (e.g., *under the heavy wooden table*).
- **Core Types:**
  - *Time:* *at* (exact time), *on* (days/dates), *in* (months/years/centuries).
  - *Place/Location:* *in* (enclosed space), *on* (surface), *at* (specific point).
  - *Direction/Movement:* *into, toward, through, across*.

### Subtopic 2: Conjunctions and Interjections

- **Conjunctions:** Words that connect words, phrases, or clauses:
  - *Coordinating Conjunctions:* Join elements of equal grammatical rank. Remembered via **FANBOYS** (*For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So*).
  - *Subordinating Conjunctions:* Join an independent clause to a dependent clause (*because, although, since, unless, while*).
  - *Correlative Conjunctions:* Paired conjunctions working together (*either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also*).
- **Interjections:** Exclamatory words expressing sudden emotion or exclamation (*Oh!, Alas!, Bravo!*). They have no structural connection to the rest of the sentence.

## Important Classifications / Structures / Processes

| Part of Speech | Primary Function | Sub-types / Mnemonic | Example Sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adverb | Modifies V / Adj / Adv | Manner, Degree, Frequency | She spoke *extremely* (*Degree*) *clearly* (*Manner*). |
| Preposition | Establishes spatial/temporal link | Time, Place, Direction | The meeting starts *at* 9 AM *on* Monday. |
| Coordinating Conjunction | Joins equal syntactic units | **FANBOYS** | We studied hard, *but* the test was tough. |
| Subordinating Conjunction | Introduces dependent clause | Cause, Condition, Time | He left *because* the event concluded. |
| Correlative Conjunction | Paired elements | *both...and*, *either...or* | *Not only* did she win, *but* she *also* broke the record. |

## Important Formulas / Rules / Relationships

Prepositional Phrase Structure:

$$\text{Prepositional Phrase} = \text{Preposition} + [\text{Modifiers}] + \text{Object of Preposition (Noun/Pronoun)}$$

Conjunctive Adverb Punctuation Rule between independent clauses:

$$\text{Clause 1 (Independent)} + \mathbf{;} + \text{Conjunctive Adverb} + \mathbf{,} + \text{Clause 2 (Independent)}$$

Example: *The evidence was compelling; however, the jury remained skeptical.*

## Common Exam Traps

- **Adjective vs. Adverb Misuse after Action Verbs:** Using adjectives instead of adverbs to modify action verbs (e.g., *He drives quick* instead of *He drives quickly*).
- **Prepositional Object Case Errors:** The object of a preposition must always take the **objective pronoun case** (e.g., *between you and me*, NOT *between you and I*).
- **Dangling Prepositions in Formal Register:** While casual speech accepts end-prepositions, academic English prefers placing prepositions before relative pronouns (e.g., *the topic about which she wrote*, rather than *the topic she wrote about*).

## Quiz

1. Identify the option where a preposition is followed by the correct pronoun case.
  - [ ] The manager split the project assignments between she and I.
  - [ ] The grant was awarded directly to he and his team.
  - [x] The committee distributed the research materials among them and us.
  - [ ] Please send the invitation to she and her colleagues.
   > Prepositions require objective case pronouns (*them, us, him, her, me*). "Among them and us" correctly uses the objective case.

2. Which coordinating conjunction correctly completes the clause relationship expressing contrast: "The initial trial failed, _____ the team persevered with a new hypothesis."
  - [ ] so
  - [ ] for
  - [x] yet
  - [ ] or
   > "Yet" acts as a coordinating conjunction indicating contrast between the initial failure and the subsequent perseverance.

3. Select the sentence with correct conjunctive adverb punctuation.
  - [ ] The storm was severe, however we arrived safely.
  - [x] The storm was severe; however, we arrived safely.
  - [ ] The storm was severe; however we arrived safely.
  - [ ] The storm was severe however, we arrived safely.
   > Joining two independent clauses with a conjunctive adverb requires a semicolon before the adverb and a comma immediately following it.
