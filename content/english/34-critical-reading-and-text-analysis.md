Critical reading involves evaluating an author's argument for structural soundness, identifying underlying assumptions, detecting rhetorical fallacies, and recognizing bias. Critical readers deconstruct texts to assess evidence quality and logical validity.

## Main Concept 1: Argumentative Anatomy (Claims, Premises, and Assumptions)

An **argument** consists of a conclusion (claim) supported by evidence (premises), resting upon unstated underlying assumptions.

- **Definitions and terminology:**
  - *Claim:* The central conclusion the author seeks to establish.
  - *Premise:* A factual statement, statistic, or evidence offered to support the claim.
  - *Unstated Assumption:* An implicit, unwritten belief required to bridge the premise to the claim.
  - *Warrant:* The logical justification connecting premise to claim.

$$\text{Premise} + \mathbf{\text{[Unstated Assumption]}} \implies \text{Claim (Conclusion)}$$

- **Evaluating Validity:** An argument is logically *valid* if the conclusion follows necessarily from the premises; it is *sound* if it is valid AND the premises are factually true.

## Main Concept 2: Informal Rhetorical Fallacies

Fallacies are errors in reasoning that undermine the logic of an argument.

### Subtopic 1: Fallacies of Relevance and Attack

- **Ad Hominem:** Attacking the character or motives of the person making the argument rather than addressing the argument itself (*Dismiss his study because he works for a private firm*).
- **Straw Man:** Misrepresenting, exaggerating, or oversimplifying an opponent's argument to make it easier to attack (*Proponents of green energy want to destroy the economy*).
- **Red Herring:** Introducing an irrelevant topic to divert attention from the original issue.

### Subtopic 2: Fallacies of Presumption and Causal Logic

- **False Dilemma (Either/Or Fallacy):** Presenting only two extreme alternatives when other viable options exist (*Either we cut the research budget completely, or the university goes bankrupt*).
- **Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc (False Cause):** Incorrectly assuming that because event $B$ followed event $A$, event $A$ caused event $B$ (*The new policy was adopted in May, and sales dropped in June; therefore, the policy caused the drop*).
- **Slippery Slope:** Claiming without proof that a minor first step will inevitably lead to a chain of catastrophic consequences.

## Important Classifications / Structures / Processes

| Rhetorical Fallacy | Fallacy Type | Core Logical Error | Example Stem |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Ad Hominem* | Relevance | Attacks speaker, not logic | "You can't trust her data because she's young." |
| *Straw Man* | Distortion | Distorts argument to attack | "They want to ban all personal vehicles." |
| *Post Hoc* | Causal | Confuses correlation with causation | "After $X$ happened, $Y$ occurred; so $X$ caused $Y$." |
| *False Dilemma* | Presumption | Ignores middle options | "Choose option $A$ or face absolute ruin." |
| *Circular Logic* | Begging Question | Conclusion used as premise | "The law is just because it is legal." |

## Important Formulas / Rules / Relationships

Assumption Identification Equation:

$$\text{Premise: } A \longrightarrow \text{Claim: } B \quad \implies \quad \mathbf{\text{Necessary Assumption: }} \text{If } A, \text{ then } B \text{ holds true without exception.}$$

Sufficient vs. Necessary Conditions:

$$\text{Sufficient Condition } (S): \text{If } S \text{ happens}, \text{ then } N \text{ MUST happen } (S \implies N).$$

$$\text{Fallacy of Affirming the Consequent}: \text{Observing } N \text{ does NOT mean } S \text{ caused it!}$$

## Common Exam Traps

- **Confusing Correlation with Causation:** Assuming that because two variables move together in time, one directly causes the other (e.g., validating a *Post Hoc* fallacy on critical reading sections).
- **Selecting "True" Outside Facts on Assumption Questions:** Choosing an answer choice on critical reasoning items that is factually true in real life, but does not represent a necessary unstated bridge for the specific passage argument.
- **Confounding Strengthening and Weakening Tasks:** Selecting an option that *strengthens* an argument when the prompt explicitly asks for the option that *weakens* it.

## Quiz

1. Identify the logical fallacy in the statement: "We must either ban all artificial intelligence development immediately, or human creativity will cease to exist within five years."
  - [ ] Ad Hominem
  - [x] False Dilemma
  - [ ] Circular Reasoning
  - [ ] Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc
   > The statement forces a choice between two extreme endpoints while ignoring all intermediate regulatory or collaborative possibilities (False Dilemma).

2. Consider the argument: "City $X$ increased its public park funding in 2020. By 2022, citizen health scores improved. Therefore, the park funding directly caused the improved health scores." Which fallacy does this exhibit?
  - [ ] Straw Man
  - [ ] Ad Hominem
  - [x] Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc
  - [ ] Red Herring
   > Assuming that the temporal sequence of two events proves a direct cause-and-effect relationship is a classic *Post Hoc* (false cause) fallacy.

3. An analyst argues: "Implementing strict water rationing will lower municipal consumption." Which unstated assumption is strictly necessary for this claim to hold?
  - [ ] Municipal water supplies are currently at record lows.
  - [x] Residents will comply with the mandatory rationing rules.
  - [ ] Neighboring cities have successfully introduced water rationing.
  - [ ] Agricultural water use exceeds municipal water consumption.
   > For rationing to lower consumption, residents must actually comply with the rules; if non-compliance is rampant, the claim fails.
