Reading comprehension and foundational writing form an interdependent cycle in literacy development. Extracting structural meaning from a text directly informs the ability to construct organized, coherent paragraphs.

## Main Concept 1: Core Reading Skills

Active reading requires extracting explicit facts, identifying implicit central ideas, and recognizing the author's organizational framework.

- **Definitions and terminology:** 
  - *Main Idea:* The overarching central point or claim of a text.
  - *Skimming:* Rapidly reading across headings, topic sentences, and keywords to grasp the general gist of a passage.
  - *Scanning:* Searching a text rapidly for specific target information, numbers, or keywords without reading every word.
- **Important principles and rules:**
  - Direct factual details are explicitly stated in the body of the text.
  - Inferences require combining explicit textual evidence with background knowledge to deduce unstated conclusions.

## Main Concept 2: Paragraph Construction and Structure

A standard academic paragraph is a self-contained unit of writing focusing on a single central topic.

### Subtopic 1: Structural Components of a Paragraph

1. **Topic Sentence:** The opening sentence declaring the paragraph's main point or claim.
2. **Supporting Sentences:** 3 to 5 sentences providing evidence, explanations, statistics, or examples supporting the topic sentence.
3. **Concluding Sentence:** A terminal sentence summarizing the key point or transitioning to the next paragraph.

### Subtopic 2: Coherence, Transitions, and Proofreading

- **Coherence:** Logical flow achieved by ordering sentences chronologically, spatially, or in order of importance.
- **Transitional Devices:** Words linking ideas smoothly (*furthermore, in addition, however, consequently, in contrast*).
- **Basic Proofreading:** Checking written draft for subject-verb agreement, verb tense consistency, correct punctuation, and spelling accuracy.

## Important Classifications / Structures / Processes

| Transition Category | Functional Purpose | Representative Transition Words |
|---|---|---|
| Addition | Introduces supplementary info | *Furthermore, moreover, in addition, also* |
| Contrast | Highlights differences/objections | *However, nevertheless, in contrast, on the other hand* |
| Cause & Effect | Shows logical consequences | *Therefore, consequently, as a result, thus* |
| Example | Provides concrete illustration | *For instance, specifically, to illustrate* |
| Summary | Concludes an argument | *In conclusion, overall, to summarize* |

## Important Formulas / Rules / Relationships

Standard Paragraph Structure Equation:

$$\text{Academic Paragraph} = \text{Topic Sentence} + \text{Supporting Evidence (2--4 sentences)} + \text{Analysis/Warrant} + \text{Concluding Sentence}$$

Reading Comprehension Inference Formula:

$$\text{Textual Evidence (Explicit Details)} + \text{Prior Knowledge / Logic} \implies \text{Valid Inference}$$

## Common Exam Traps

- **Confusing Topic Sentence with Supporting Detail:** mistaking a specific narrow fact for the broad main idea of a passage.
- **Abrupt Idea Jumps (Lack of Coherence):** Juxtaposing two ideas without a transitional marker to clarify their logical relationship.
- **Failing to Distinguish Fact from Opinion:** Treating an author's subjective value judgment as an objective, verifiable factual detail.
