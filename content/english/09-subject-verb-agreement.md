Subject–Verb Agreement (also called subject-verb concord) is the fundamental grammatical rule stating that a finite verb must match its subject in person (first, second, third) and number (singular, plural).

## Main Concept 1: The Core Agreement Principle

A singular subject requires a singular verb form; a plural subject requires a plural verb form.

- **Definitions and terminology:** *Concord* is the structural harmony between connected sentence elements.
- **Third-Person Singular Rule:** In the present simple tense, singular subjects take verbs ending in *-s* or *-es* ($V_5$), whereas plural subjects take base verb forms ($V_1$).
  - *Singular:* *The scientist (she/he/it) conducts an experiment.*
  - *Plural:* *The scientists (they) conduct an experiment.*
- **Intervening Phrases:** Prepositional phrases or clauses coming between the subject and the verb do **not** alter subject number.
  - *Example:* *The results [of the initial experiment] are promising.* (Subject is plural *results*, NOT singular *experiment*).

## Main Concept 2: Compound Subjects and Special Connectives

Compound subjects formed by joining multiple nouns present specific agreement rules.

### Subtopic 1: Subjects Joined by "And"

- **Standard Plural Rule:** Two or more singular subjects joined by *and* take a plural verb (*The director and the manager are present.*).
- **Single Unit Exception:** When two words joined by *and* express a single combined idea, dish, or title, they take a singular verb (*Bread and butter is a staple breakfast*; *Trial and error is essential*).

### Subtopic 2: Disjunctive Connectives ("Or", "Nor", "Either...Or", "Neither...Nor")

When subjects are connected by *or, nor, either...or, neither...nor*, or *not only...but also*, the verb agrees with the **closest subject** (The Rule of Proximity).

- *Example 1 (Near subject singular):* *Neither the assistants nor the professor is available.*
- *Example 2 (Near subject plural):* *Neither the professor nor the assistants are available.*

## Main Concept 3: Collective Nouns and Indefinite Pronouns

- **Collective Nouns:** Nouns representing groups (*team, committee, family, jury, audience*):
  - Take a **singular verb** when acting as a unified single body (*The committee has reached a decision.*).
  - Take a **plural verb** when individual members act independently (*The committee are divided in their opinions.*).
- **Indefinite Pronouns:**
  - *Always Singular:* *everyone, everybody, someone, nobody, anyone, each, either, neither, one*. (*Each of the answers is correct.*)
  - *Always Plural:* *both, few, many, several*. (*Several of the manuscripts remain unread.*)
  - *Variable (SANAM: Some, Any, None, All, Most):* Depends on whether the modifying prepositional object is countable or uncountable (*All of the water is gone* vs. *All of the reports are ready*).

## Important Classifications / Structures / Processes

| Subject Pattern | Applicable Concord Rule | Example Sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Intervening Prepositional Phrase | Ignore modifiers; match true head noun | The *box* [of heavy tools] *is* heavy. |
| Joined by *And* (Distinct Entities) | Always Plural Verb | The *lab* and the *library* *are* open. |
| Joined by *Or / Nor* | Proximity Rule (Match nearest subject) | Either the chief or his *helpers* *were* wrong. |
| Always Singular Indefinite | Singular Verb | *Each* of the candidates *has* five minutes. |
| Quantity / Distance / Sums | Units of measurement take Singular Verb | *Fifty thousand dollars* *is* a large sum. |

## Important Formulas / Rules / Relationships

The Rule of Proximity for Disjunctive Correlative Subjects:

$$\text{Either / Neither } \text{Subject}_1 + \text{or / nor } \mathbf{\text{Subject}_2} \longrightarrow \text{Verb matches } \mathbf{\text{Subject}_2}$$

The SANAM Variable Indefinite Pronoun Rule:

$$\text{Some / Any / None / All / Most} + \text{of the } \longrightarrow \begin{cases} \text{Uncountable Noun} \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{Singular Verb}} \\ \text{Plural Countable Noun} \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{Plural Verb}} \end{cases}$$

## Common Exam Traps

- **Distraction by Intervening Phrases:** Matching the verb to the nearest noun inside a prepositional phrase rather than the actual sentence subject (e.g., writing *The list of items are ready* instead of *The list of items is ready*).
- **Misjudging Singular Indefinite Pronouns:** Treating words like *everyone, each*, or *nobody* as plural because they imply multiple people (e.g., writing *Everyone have finished* instead of *Everyone has finished*).
- **Misapplying Inverted Word Order:** Failing to locate the subject following introductory *There is / There are* phrases (e.g., writing *There is three issues to discuss* instead of *There are three issues to discuss*).

## Quiz

1. Choose the sentence with grammatically correct subject–verb agreement.
  - [ ] The stack of official documents were misplaced yesterday.
  - [x] The stack of official documents was misplaced yesterday.
  - [ ] The stack of official documents are misplaced yesterday.
  - [ ] The stack of official documents have been misplaced yesterday.
   > The head noun of the subject is the singular "stack" (modified by the prepositional phrase "of official documents"), requiring the singular verb "was".

2. Select the sentence that correctly applies the Rule of Proximity.
  - [ ] Neither the main researcher nor his assistants was satisfied with the results.
  - [ ] Neither the main researcher nor his assistants is satisfied with the results.
  - [x] Neither the main researcher nor his assistants are satisfied with the results.
  - [ ] Neither the main researcher nor his assistants has satisfied with the results.
   > When subjects are joined by "neither...nor", the verb must agree with the nearest subject ("assistants", which is plural), requiring "are".

3. Complete the sentence correctly: "Each of the experimental trials _____ conducted under strict environmental controls."
  - [ ] were
  - [ ] are
  - [x] was
  - [ ] have been
   > "Each" is an inherently singular indefinite pronoun requiring the singular verb "was", regardless of the plural noun "trials" inside the prepositional phrase.
