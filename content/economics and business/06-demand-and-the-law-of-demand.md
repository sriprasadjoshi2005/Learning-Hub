Demand is one of the fundamental concepts in economics. It explains how consumers decide the quantities of goods and services they are willing and able to purchase at different prices.

Understanding demand helps businesses determine prices, estimate sales, plan production and analyse consumer behaviour.

## 1. Meaning of Demand

**Demand** refers to the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to purchase at different prices during a particular period, assuming other factors remain constant.

Demand involves three important conditions:

- Desire: The consumer wants the product.
- Ability to pay: The consumer has sufficient purchasing power.
- Willingness to pay: The consumer is prepared to purchase the product.

A desire without purchasing power does not necessarily constitute effective economic demand.

## 2. Individual Demand and Market Demand

### Individual Demand

Individual demand refers to the quantity of a product that a particular consumer is willing and able to purchase at different prices.

### Market Demand

Market demand represents the total quantity demanded by all consumers in a market at different prices.

Market Demand = Sum of Individual Demands

### Example

Suppose two consumers purchase a product at a price of Rs. 100.

- Consumer A demands 3 units.
- Consumer B demands 5 units.

Market Demand = 3 + 5 = 8 units.

Market demand is obtained by horizontally adding the quantities demanded by individual consumers at each price.

## 3. The Law of Demand

The **Law of Demand** states that, other things remaining constant, the quantity demanded of a good generally increases when its price decreases and decreases when its price increases.

This establishes an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.

### Demand Schedule

| Price (Rs.) | Quantity Demanded (Units) |
| --- | --- |
| 10 | 100 |
| 20 | 80 |
| 30 | 60 |
| 40 | 40 |
| 50 | 20 |

The schedule shows that consumers demand fewer units as the price increases.

### Demand Curve

A demand curve is a graphical representation of the relationship between the price of a product and its quantity demanded.

- Price is represented on the vertical axis.
- Quantity demanded is represented on the horizontal axis.
- The demand curve generally slopes downward from left to right.

The downward slope reflects the inverse relationship described by the law of demand.

## 4. Reasons for the Downward-Sloping Demand Curve

### Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility

As a consumer consumes additional units of a good, the additional satisfaction obtained from each successive unit generally decreases.

Consumers may therefore be willing to purchase additional units only at lower prices.

### Income Effect

A reduction in the price of a product increases the purchasing power of consumers' income.

Consumers can purchase more of the product with the same amount of money.

### Substitution Effect

When the price of a product decreases relative to its substitutes, consumers may switch towards the relatively cheaper product.

### Entry of New Consumers

A lower price may make a product affordable to additional consumers, increasing total market demand.

### Multiple Uses

Some goods have several possible uses. A lower price may encourage consumers to use them for additional purposes.

## 5. Determinants of Demand

Demand is influenced by several factors other than the product's own price.

### Consumer Income

An increase in income generally increases demand for normal goods.

For inferior goods, an increase in income may reduce demand.

### Prices of Related Goods

Related goods are classified as substitutes or complements.

**Substitute Goods:** Goods that can be used in place of one another.

Examples:
- Tea and coffee.
- Butter and margarine.

If the price of coffee increases, demand for tea may increase.

**Complementary Goods:** Goods that are commonly consumed together.

Examples:
- Cars and fuel.
- Printers and ink cartridges.

If the price of cars increases significantly, demand for fuel may decrease through reduced car purchases.

### Consumer Preferences

Changes in fashion, tastes, advertising and lifestyle can influence demand.

### Population

An increase in the number of consumers may increase market demand.

### Expectations

Expectations about future prices, income and product availability can influence present demand.

### Seasonal Factors

Demand for certain products changes with seasons and weather conditions.

Examples include umbrellas, winter clothing and air conditioners.

## 6. Movement Along the Demand Curve

A movement along the demand curve occurs when the product's own price changes while other factors remain constant.

### Extension of Demand

Extension occurs when quantity demanded increases because the price decreases.

### Contraction of Demand

Contraction occurs when quantity demanded decreases because the price increases.

These changes represent movements along the same demand curve.

## 7. Shift in the Demand Curve

A shift occurs when demand changes because of factors other than the product's own price.

### Increase in Demand

An increase in demand shifts the entire demand curve to the right.

Possible causes:

- Increase in income for a normal good.
- Increase in the price of a substitute.
- Decrease in the price of a complement.
- Favourable changes in consumer preferences.
- Increase in the number of consumers.

### Decrease in Demand

A decrease in demand shifts the entire demand curve to the left.

Possible causes:

- Decrease in income for a normal good.
- Decrease in the price of a substitute.
- Increase in the price of a complement.
- Unfavourable changes in consumer preferences.
- Decrease in the number of consumers.

## 8. Exceptions and Special Cases

The law of demand describes a general economic relationship, but certain goods may not follow the usual pattern.

### Giffen Goods

Giffen goods are a special category of inferior goods for which quantity demanded may increase when price increases, under particular income and substitution conditions.

The negative income effect must outweigh the substitution effect.

### Veblen Goods

Veblen goods are products whose appeal may increase with higher prices because consumers associate expensive prices with prestige or status.

Examples may include certain luxury watches and exclusive designer products.

### Expectations of Future Prices

Consumers may purchase more of a product when its current price increases if they expect its price to rise further in the future.

### Essential Goods

Demand for essential goods may be relatively insensitive to price changes, although this does not necessarily violate the law of demand.

## 9. Demand Function

A demand function expresses the relationship between quantity demanded and its determinants.

A simplified demand function is:

Qd = f(P, Y, Pr, T, N, E)

Where:

- Qd = Quantity demanded.
- P = Price of the product.
- Y = Consumer income.
- Pr = Prices of related goods.
- T = Consumer tastes and preferences.
- N = Number of consumers.
- E = Consumer expectations.

For a simple linear demand function:

Qd = a - bP

Where:

- a = Intercept.
- b = Positive coefficient.
- P = Price.
- Qd = Quantity demanded.

The negative sign indicates the usual inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.

## Common Exam Traps

- Demand requires desire, willingness and purchasing power.
- Market demand is obtained by adding individual quantities demanded at each price.
- The law of demand assumes other factors remain constant.
- A change in the product's own price causes movement along the demand curve.
- A change in income, preferences or related goods causes a shift in demand.
- Extension and contraction are movements along the demand curve.
- Increase and decrease in demand are shifts of the entire curve.
- Giffen goods and Veblen goods are different concepts.
- A normal good generally experiences increased demand when consumer income increases.
- An inferior good may experience decreased demand when consumer income increases.
