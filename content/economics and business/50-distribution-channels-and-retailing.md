A product must reach customers at the right place and time to create value. Distribution involves the activities and organisations responsible for moving products from producers to final consumers or business users.

Retailing is an important part of distribution because it connects businesses directly with customers through physical stores, online platforms, and other selling methods.

## 1. Meaning of Distribution

Distribution refers to the activities involved in making products and services available to customers.

It includes:

- Transportation.
- Warehousing.
- Inventory management.
- Order processing.
- Packaging.
- Material handling.
- Channel management.
- Delivery.

Distribution is an important element of the marketing mix and is commonly associated with the element called Place.

## 2. Meaning of Distribution Channel

A distribution channel is the route through which products move from producers to final consumers or organisational buyers.

A channel may involve intermediaries such as wholesalers, distributors, agents, and retailers.

The choice of distribution channel affects:

- Product availability.
- Distribution costs.
- Delivery speed.
- Market coverage.
- Customer convenience.
- Business profitability.

## 3. Direct Distribution Channel

A direct distribution channel allows producers to sell products directly to final customers without independent intermediaries.

Structure:

Producer -> Consumer

Examples:

- A manufacturer selling through its own website.
- A farmer selling vegetables directly to customers.
- A software company selling subscriptions through its website.

Advantages:

- Greater control over customer relationships.
- Direct customer feedback.
- Greater control over pricing and brand presentation.
- No need to share intermediary margins.

Disadvantages:

- Requires investment in distribution infrastructure.
- The producer must manage customer service and delivery.
- Reaching geographically distant customers may be difficult.

## 4. Indirect Distribution Channel

An indirect distribution channel uses one or more intermediaries between producers and final customers.

### One-Level Channel

Producer -> Retailer -> Consumer

The retailer purchases products from the producer and sells them to final customers.

### Two-Level Channel

Producer -> Wholesaler -> Retailer -> Consumer

The wholesaler purchases products in bulk and supplies retailers.

### Three-Level Channel

Producer -> Agent -> Wholesaler -> Retailer -> Consumer

An agent helps connect producers with wholesalers or other channel members.

The exact channel structure depends on the industry, product, market, and distribution strategy.

## 5. Types of Marketing Intermediaries

### Wholesalers

Wholesalers purchase goods in large quantities from producers and resell them to retailers or other business customers.

Functions include:

- Bulk purchasing.
- Warehousing.
- Breaking bulk into smaller quantities.
- Transportation.
- Market information.
- Credit facilities.
- Risk bearing.

### Retailers

Retailers sell goods or services directly to final consumers for personal or household use.

Examples include supermarkets, department stores, convenience stores, and online retailers.

### Agents

Agents act on behalf of producers or buyers to facilitate transactions.

They generally earn commissions rather than purchasing and reselling the products themselves.

### Distributors

Distributors purchase and resell products within an assigned market or geographical territory.

They may provide storage, transportation, technical support, and market development.

## 6. Functions of Distribution Channels

Distribution channels perform several important functions.

### Information

Channel members collect and communicate information about customer needs, market trends, and competitors.

### Promotion

Intermediaries may promote products through displays, demonstrations, and local advertising.

### Contact

They help producers reach potential customers.

### Matching

Intermediaries organise products according to customer requirements, including quantity, assortment, and packaging.

### Negotiation

Channel members help negotiate prices, delivery conditions, and other transaction terms.

### Physical Distribution

They support the storage, transportation, and handling of products.

### Financing

Some intermediaries provide credit or financial support to channel participants.

### Risk Bearing

Intermediaries may assume risks related to inventory, damage, storage, and changing market demand.

## 7. Distribution Intensity

Distribution intensity refers to how widely a business makes its products available through different outlets.

Three major approaches are commonly used.

### Intensive Distribution

Intensive distribution attempts to make products available through as many suitable outlets as possible.

It is commonly used for frequently purchased convenience products.

Examples:

- Bottled water.
- Packaged snacks.
- Toothpaste.
- Everyday household products.

Advantages:

- High product availability.
- Greater market coverage.
- Convenient purchasing.

Limitations:

- Lower control over individual outlets.
- Greater channel-management complexity.
- Potential pressure on profit margins.

### Selective Distribution

Selective distribution uses a limited number of carefully selected intermediaries.

It is suitable for products requiring a certain level of service, product knowledge, or brand presentation.

Examples:

- Consumer electronics.
- Furniture.
- Home appliances.

Advantages:

- Better control over distribution.
- Stronger retailer relationships.
- More manageable channel operations.

Limitations:

- Lower market coverage than intensive distribution.
- Some customers may have limited access.

### Exclusive Distribution

Exclusive distribution grants selling rights to one or a very small number of intermediaries within a specified territory or market.

It is commonly associated with luxury, specialised, or premium products.

Advantages:

- Greater control over brand presentation.
- Stronger relationships with selected sellers.
- Reduced competition among authorised outlets.

Limitations:

- Limited availability.
- Dependence on selected intermediaries.
- Reduced geographical coverage.

## 8. Meaning of Retailing

Retailing refers to the activities involved in selling products or services directly to final consumers for personal or household use.

Retailers purchase goods from manufacturers, wholesalers, or distributors and make them available in quantities and forms suitable for customers.

Retailing may take place through physical stores, online platforms, mobile applications, or other channels.

## 9. Types of Retailers

### Department Stores

Department stores offer a wide range of products organised into separate departments.

Examples include clothing, cosmetics, household goods, and accessories.

### Supermarkets

Supermarkets are self-service retail stores that primarily sell food, beverages, and household necessities.

### Convenience Stores

Convenience stores are relatively small retail outlets that focus on frequently purchased products and convenient locations.

### Specialty Stores

Specialty stores concentrate on a particular product category or specialised market.

Examples include footwear stores, bookshops, and electronics stores.

### Discount Stores

Discount stores sell products at relatively low prices, often through cost-efficient operations and high sales volumes.

### Hypermarkets

Hypermarkets combine supermarket and department-store characteristics and offer a broad range of products in large retail spaces.

### Online Retailers

Online retailers sell products through websites or mobile applications.

Customers can browse products, compare prices, place orders, and arrange delivery digitally.

## 10. E-Commerce and Digital Retailing

E-commerce refers to the buying and selling of goods and services through electronic networks, particularly the internet.

Digital retailing allows businesses to reach customers without requiring them to visit physical stores.

Important features include:

- Online product catalogues.
- Digital payment systems.
- Customer reviews.
- Product recommendations.
- Order tracking.
- Home delivery.
- Digital customer support.

### Advantages of E-Commerce

- Wider geographical reach.
- Convenient shopping.
- Lower dependence on physical retail space.
- Easy product comparison.
- Access to customer purchasing data.
- Continuous availability of online storefronts.

### Limitations of E-Commerce

- Customers may be unable to physically inspect products.
- Delivery delays can occur.
- Cybersecurity and privacy risks exist.
- Returns and refunds may be complicated.
- Businesses depend on reliable technology and logistics.

## 11. Omnichannel and Multichannel Retailing

### Multichannel Retailing

Multichannel retailing involves selling through multiple channels, such as physical stores, websites, mobile applications, and marketplaces.

These channels may operate separately.

### Omnichannel Retailing

Omnichannel retailing integrates different sales and communication channels to provide a connected customer experience.

Example:

A customer may browse a product through a mobile application, check its availability at a physical store, purchase it online, and collect it from the store.

The main objective is to make the transition between channels convenient and consistent.

## 12. Physical Distribution and Logistics

Physical distribution refers to the movement and storage of finished products from producers to customers.

Logistics involves planning, implementing, and controlling the efficient movement and storage of goods, services, and related information.

Important logistics activities include:

### Transportation

Moving products between production facilities, warehouses, retailers, and customers.

Common transportation modes include:

- Road.
- Rail.
- Air.
- Water.
- Pipelines.

### Warehousing

Storing products until they are needed by customers or distribution partners.

### Inventory Management

Maintaining suitable stock levels to meet demand while controlling storage and holding costs.

### Order Processing

Receiving, verifying, preparing, and fulfilling customer orders.

### Material Handling

Moving, loading, unloading, and organising goods within warehouses and distribution facilities.

## 13. Inventory Management

Inventory refers to the goods and materials held by a business for production or sale.

Effective inventory management ensures that products are available when required without maintaining unnecessarily high stock levels.

### Types of Inventory

**Raw materials:** Inputs used in manufacturing.

**Work-in-progress:** Products that are partially completed.

**Finished goods:** Products ready for sale.

**Maintenance and operating supplies:** Materials used to support business operations.

### Inventory Costs

**Ordering costs:** Expenses associated with placing and receiving orders.

**Holding costs:** Expenses associated with storing inventory.

**Shortage costs:** Costs caused by insufficient stock.

**Purchase costs:** Amounts paid to acquire inventory.

## 14. Economic Order Quantity

Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) is a model used to determine the order quantity that minimises the combined annual ordering and holding costs under specified assumptions.

Formula:

EOQ = sqrt((2 x D x S) / H)

Where:

D = Annual demand in units.

S = Ordering cost per order.

H = Annual holding cost per unit.

Example:

A business has annual demand of 1,000 units, ordering cost of 50 per order, and annual holding cost of 2 per unit.

EOQ = sqrt((2 x 1000 x 50) / 2)

EOQ = sqrt(50,000)

EOQ is approximately 224 units.

The EOQ model assumes relatively stable demand, known ordering and holding costs, and other simplifying conditions.

## 15. Just-in-Time Inventory Management

Just-in-Time (JIT) is an inventory management approach in which materials and products are received or produced close to the time they are needed.

The objective is to reduce unnecessary inventory and associated storage costs.

Advantages:

- Lower inventory holding costs.
- Reduced storage requirements.
- Less capital tied up in inventory.
- Potential reduction in waste.

Limitations:

- Dependence on reliable suppliers.
- Production disruptions can occur when deliveries are delayed.
- Unexpected demand increases can create shortages.
- Requires effective coordination.

## 16. Factors Affecting the Choice of Distribution Channel

Businesses should consider several factors when selecting distribution channels.

**Nature of the product:** Perishable, fragile, expensive, or technical products may require specialised distribution.

**Target customers:** Customer location, purchasing habits, and convenience requirements influence channel selection.

**Market coverage:** Businesses must decide how widely their products should be distributed.

**Cost:** Transportation, storage, intermediary margins, and channel management affect profitability.

**Control:** Direct distribution provides greater control, while indirect distribution may offer wider reach.

**Competition:** Competitor distribution strategies influence customer expectations.

**Business resources:** Financial capacity, technology, personnel, and infrastructure affect channel choices.

**Legal requirements:** Distribution agreements and industry regulations may influence the available options.

## 17. Challenges in Distribution and Retailing

Businesses face several challenges when managing distribution networks.

- Rising transportation costs.
- Supply chain disruptions.
- Inventory shortages.
- Excess inventory.
- Delivery delays.
- Product damage.
- Changing customer expectations.
- Competition from online retailers.
- Cybersecurity risks in digital retailing.
- Environmental concerns associated with packaging and transportation.

Businesses can address these challenges through improved planning, technology, supplier relationships, and efficient logistics management.

- Selective distribution uses a limited number of selected intermediaries.
