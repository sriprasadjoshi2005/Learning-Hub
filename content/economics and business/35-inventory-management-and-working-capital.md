Inventory management and working capital management are important for maintaining the financial and operational stability of a business.

Inventory management determines how much stock an organisation should hold, when it should reorder, and how stock should be controlled.

Working capital management focuses on managing short-term assets and liabilities so that an organisation can meet its immediate financial obligations and continue operating efficiently.

## 1. Meaning of Inventory

Inventory refers to the goods and materials held by a business for production, resale, or operational use.

It is an important current asset for many organisations.

Common inventory categories include:

### Raw Materials

Materials purchased for use in manufacturing.

Example: Steel used by an automobile manufacturer.

### Work-in-Progress

Products that have entered the production process but are not yet complete.

Example: A partially assembled vehicle.

### Finished Goods

Completed products that are ready for sale or distribution.

Example: Packaged electronic devices waiting to be shipped.

### Maintenance, Repair, and Operating Supplies

Materials used to maintain business operations.

Examples include lubricants, cleaning supplies, and replacement machine components.

## 2. Importance of Inventory Management

Inventory management aims to maintain sufficient stock without holding unnecessarily large quantities.

Its objectives include:

- Preventing stockouts.
- Reducing storage costs.
- Avoiding excessive capital investment.
- Maintaining production continuity.
- Meeting customer demand.
- Reducing damage and obsolescence.
- Improving stock visibility.
- Supporting efficient purchasing decisions.

Poor inventory management can lead to lost sales, interrupted production, unnecessary expenses, and dissatisfied customers.

## 3. Types of Inventory Costs

Holding inventory involves several costs.

### Ordering Costs

Ordering costs are expenses associated with placing and receiving orders.

Examples include:

- Purchase order processing.
- Supplier communication.
- Transportation arrangements.
- Receiving and inspection activities.

### Holding Costs

Holding costs are expenses associated with keeping inventory over time.

Examples include:

- Warehouse rent.
- Insurance.
- Security.
- Handling.
- Deterioration.
- Obsolescence.
- Opportunity cost of capital.

### Stockout Costs

Stockout costs arise when inventory is unavailable when required.

Examples include:

- Lost sales.
- Delayed production.
- Emergency purchasing.
- Customer dissatisfaction.
- Contractual penalties.

### Purchase Costs

Purchase costs represent the amount paid to acquire inventory.

They may depend on supplier prices, order quantities, and discounts.

## 4. Economic Order Quantity

Economic Order Quantity is a model used to determine an order quantity that minimises the combined annual ordering and holding costs under specified assumptions.

The basic EOQ formula is:

EOQ = sqrt((2 × D × S) / H)

Where:

- D = Annual demand in units.
- S = Ordering cost per order.
- H = Annual holding cost per unit.

### Example

A business has:

- Annual demand = 2,000 units.
- Ordering cost = Rs. 50 per order.
- Annual holding cost = Rs. 4 per unit.

EOQ = sqrt((2 × 2,000 × 50) / 4)

EOQ = sqrt(50,000)

EOQ is approximately 224 units.

The business should order approximately 224 units per order under the assumptions of the basic model.

### Assumptions of EOQ

The basic EOQ model assumes:

- Demand is known and constant.
- Ordering cost per order is constant.
- Holding cost per unit is constant.
- Replenishment is instantaneous or occurs according to the model's simplified assumptions.
- No stockouts occur.
- Purchase price per unit is constant.

Actual businesses may need more advanced models when these assumptions do not hold.

## 5. Reorder Level

The reorder level is the inventory position at which a new order should be placed to avoid running out of stock before replenishment.

A simple formula is:

Reorder Level = Demand During Lead Time

If safety stock is maintained:

Reorder Level = Demand During Lead Time + Safety Stock

### Example

A business uses 20 units per day.

The supplier's lead time is 5 days.

Demand During Lead Time = 20 × 5 = 100 units.

If safety stock is 30 units:

Reorder Level = 100 + 30 = 130 units.

The business should place a new order when its relevant inventory position reaches approximately 130 units.

## 6. Safety Stock

Safety stock is additional inventory maintained to protect against unexpected demand increases or supply delays.

It reduces the risk of stockouts.

The required safety stock depends on factors such as:

- Demand variability.
- Supplier reliability.
- Lead-time variability.
- Desired service level.
- Cost of holding additional inventory.

Maintaining more safety stock can improve product availability but also increases holding costs.

## 7. Inventory Control Techniques

### ABC Analysis

ABC analysis classifies inventory according to its annual consumption value.

Annual Consumption Value = Annual Usage × Unit Cost

The usual categories are:

| Category | General Characteristics | Management Approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Small number of items with high annual consumption value | Close monitoring and strict control |
| B | Items with moderate annual consumption value | Regular monitoring |
| C | Large number of items with low annual consumption value | Simpler control procedures |

The exact proportions vary between organisations.

ABC analysis helps managers focus attention on inventory items with the greatest financial importance.

### Just-in-Time Inventory

Just-in-Time aims to receive materials and produce goods close to the time they are needed.

Its objectives include:

- Reducing unnecessary inventory.
- Minimising storage costs.
- Reducing waste.
- Improving production flow.

However, JIT systems can be vulnerable to supplier delays, transportation disruptions, and unexpected shortages.

### Perpetual Inventory System

A perpetual inventory system updates stock records continuously as purchases, sales, and other inventory movements occur.

It provides frequent information about inventory levels.

### Periodic Inventory System

A periodic inventory system updates inventory records at specific intervals through physical counting and accounting adjustments.

It may be simpler to operate but provides less continuous stock information.

### Two-Bin System

The two-bin system stores inventory in two separate containers.

The first bin is used during normal operations. When it becomes empty, the second bin provides stock while a replenishment order is placed.

It is particularly useful for managing simple consumable items.

## 8. Inventory Turnover Ratio

Inventory turnover measures how frequently inventory is sold or used during a period.

Inventory Turnover Ratio = Cost of Goods Sold / Average Inventory

Average Inventory = (Opening Inventory + Closing Inventory) / 2

### Example

A business has:

- Cost of Goods Sold = Rs. 600,000.
- Opening Inventory = Rs. 80,000.
- Closing Inventory = Rs. 120,000.

Average Inventory = (80,000 + 120,000) / 2

Average Inventory = Rs. 100,000.

Inventory Turnover Ratio = 600,000 / 100,000

Inventory Turnover Ratio = 6 times.

The business has turned over its average inventory approximately six times during the period.

A high turnover may indicate efficient inventory management, but an excessively high ratio can also indicate insufficient stock.

## 9. Meaning of Working Capital

Working capital refers to the funds invested in current assets used to support everyday business operations.

Net Working Capital = Current Assets - Current Liabilities

Current assets commonly include:

- Cash and cash equivalents.
- Accounts receivable.
- Inventory.
- Short-term investments.

Current liabilities commonly include:

- Accounts payable.
- Short-term loans.
- Outstanding expenses.
- Other obligations due within the operating period.

### Example

A business has:

- Current Assets = Rs. 500,000.
- Current Liabilities = Rs. 300,000.

Net Working Capital = 500,000 - 300,000

Net Working Capital = Rs. 200,000.

Positive working capital means current assets exceed current liabilities.

However, positive working capital does not automatically guarantee that the business has sufficient cash available at the exact time payments are due.

## 10. Importance of Working Capital Management

Working capital management ensures that a business can meet its short-term obligations while using resources efficiently.

Its objectives include:

- Maintaining sufficient liquidity.
- Paying suppliers and employees on time.
- Avoiding unnecessary borrowing.
- Supporting continuous operations.
- Reducing idle funds.
- Improving profitability.
- Maintaining financial stability.

An organisation must balance liquidity and profitability.

Holding excessive cash may reduce investment returns, while holding insufficient cash may create payment difficulties.

## 11. Current Ratio

The current ratio measures the ability of an organisation to cover its current liabilities using current assets.

Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities

### Example

Current Assets = Rs. 600,000.

Current Liabilities = Rs. 300,000.

Current Ratio = 600,000 / 300,000 = 2.

The current ratio is 2:1.

This means the business has Rs. 2 in current assets for every Rs. 1 of current liabilities.

A suitable current ratio depends on the organisation's industry, operating cycle, and the quality of its current assets.

## 12. Quick Ratio

The quick ratio measures the ability of a business to meet short-term obligations using its more liquid current assets.

A common formula is:

Quick Ratio = (Current Assets - Inventory - Prepaid Expenses) / Current Liabilities

Inventory and prepaid expenses are excluded because they may not be readily available to pay immediate obligations.

### Example

A business has:

- Current Assets = Rs. 500,000.
- Inventory = Rs. 150,000.
- Prepaid Expenses = Rs. 50,000.
- Current Liabilities = Rs. 200,000.

Quick Ratio = (500,000 - 150,000 - 50,000) / 200,000

Quick Ratio = 300,000 / 200,000

Quick Ratio = 1.5:1.

## 13. Cash Management

Cash management involves planning and controlling cash inflows and outflows.

Cash inflows may include:

- Customer payments.
- Loan proceeds.
- Investment income.
- Sale of assets.

Cash outflows may include:

- Supplier payments.
- Employee salaries.
- Rent.
- Taxes.
- Loan repayments.
- Operating expenses.

Effective cash management helps businesses avoid liquidity problems.

### Cash Budget

A cash budget estimates expected cash receipts and payments over a future period.

Closing Cash Balance = Opening Cash Balance + Cash Receipts - Cash Payments

A cash budget helps identify periods when additional financing may be required.

## 14. Receivables Management

Accounts receivable represents amounts owed to a business by customers who purchased goods or services on credit.

Receivables management aims to collect customer payments on time while maintaining good customer relationships.

Important practices include:

- Establishing credit policies.
- Checking customer creditworthiness.
- Setting credit limits.
- Monitoring overdue accounts.
- Sending payment reminders.
- Following up on delayed payments.

Offering credit can increase sales, but excessive credit risk may create bad debts and cash flow problems.

### Average Collection Period

Average Collection Period = (Average Accounts Receivable / Credit Sales) × Number of Days

This estimates the average time taken to collect credit sales.

## 15. Payables Management

Accounts payable represents amounts a business owes to suppliers for goods and services purchased on credit.

Payables management involves planning payments while maintaining supplier relationships.

Businesses should:

- Pay according to agreed terms.
- Avoid unnecessary late-payment penalties.
- Use available credit terms effectively.
- Maintain accurate payment records.
- Communicate with suppliers about payment difficulties.

Delaying payments excessively can damage supplier relationships and reduce future credit availability.

## 16. The Cash Conversion Cycle

The cash conversion cycle measures the time between paying for inventory and collecting cash from customers.

It reflects how efficiently a business manages inventory, receivables, and payables.

Cash Conversion Cycle = Inventory Conversion Period + Receivables Collection Period - Payables Deferral Period

A shorter cash conversion cycle generally means cash is recovered more quickly.

However, excessively delaying supplier payments or maintaining insufficient inventory can create operational problems.

## 17. Strategies for Improving Working Capital

Businesses can improve working capital through several methods:

- Reducing unnecessary inventory.
- Collecting receivables faster.
- Negotiating suitable supplier payment terms.
- Preparing accurate cash budgets.
- Reducing operating expenses.
- Avoiding unnecessary short-term borrowing.
- Improving demand forecasts.
- Monitoring liquidity ratios.

Working capital decisions should consider the overall effect on business operations, supplier relationships, and customer satisfaction.

## Common Exam Traps

- Raw materials, work-in-progress, and finished goods are different inventory categories.
- EOQ aims to minimise combined ordering and holding costs under specified assumptions.
- Safety stock protects against unexpected demand and supply variations.
- ABC analysis classifies inventory by annual consumption value, not simply by physical size.
- Just-in-Time reduces inventory but can increase vulnerability to supply disruptions.
- Net working capital equals current assets minus current liabilities.
- The current ratio includes inventory, while the quick ratio excludes inventory and commonly prepaid expenses.
- Inventory turnover uses cost of goods sold divided by average inventory.
- The cash conversion cycle subtracts the payables deferral period from the combined inventory and receivables periods.
