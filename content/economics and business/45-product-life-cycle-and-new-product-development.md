title: Product Life Cycle and New Product Development
level: exam
---

Every product passes through different stages in the market, from its introduction to eventual decline. Understanding the **Product Life Cycle (PLC)** helps businesses plan marketing strategies, manage product investments, and respond to changing customer preferences.

Businesses must also continuously develop new products to remain competitive, satisfy changing consumer needs, and create future sources of revenue.

## 1. Meaning of Product Life Cycle

The Product Life Cycle describes the stages through which a product generally passes during its existence in the market.

The traditional Product Life Cycle consists of four stages:

1. Introduction
2. Growth
3. Maturity
4. Decline

Each stage has different characteristics regarding sales, profits, competition, customers, and marketing strategies.

## 2. Stages of the Product Life Cycle

### Introduction Stage

The introduction stage begins when a new product is launched in the market.

Characteristics:

- Sales are generally low because customers are unfamiliar with the product.
- Promotional and distribution expenses are high.
- Profits may be low or negative.
- The business must create awareness and encourage product trials.
- Competition may be limited if the product is genuinely innovative.

Marketing strategies:

- Use advertising to introduce the product.
- Educate customers about its features and benefits.
- Establish distribution channels.
- Offer demonstrations and introductory promotions.
- Select an appropriate pricing strategy.

### Growth Stage

The growth stage occurs when customers increasingly accept the product.

Characteristics:

- Sales increase rapidly.
- Profits generally improve.
- Competitors enter the market.
- Distribution becomes more extensive.
- Customers become more familiar with the product.

Marketing strategies:

- Improve product quality and features.
- Expand distribution.
- Strengthen brand identity.
- Enter new market segments.
- Introduce competitive pricing when appropriate.

### Maturity Stage

The maturity stage occurs when sales growth slows and the product reaches a high level of market acceptance.

Characteristics:

- Sales reach their peak or remain relatively stable.
- Competition becomes intense.
- Price competition may increase.
- Businesses spend more on differentiation and customer retention.
- Profit margins may come under pressure.

Marketing strategies:

- Improve product features and packaging.
- Introduce variations and updated versions.
- Offer loyalty programmes.
- Explore new customer segments.
- Improve operational efficiency.
- Use promotional campaigns to protect market share.

### Decline Stage

The decline stage occurs when demand for a product decreases over time.

Characteristics:

- Sales fall.
- Profits decline.
- Customer preferences may shift towards alternatives.
- Some competitors leave the market.
- Distribution and promotional activities may be reduced.

Marketing strategies:

- Reduce unnecessary costs.
- Focus on profitable customer segments.
- Modify or reposition the product.
- Sell the product to another business.
- Discontinue the product when continued operation is no longer profitable.

## 3. Product Life Cycle Diagram

The traditional Product Life Cycle can be represented as a curve showing sales over time.

| Stage | Sales Trend | Competition | Main Business Objective |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Introduction | Low | Usually limited | Create awareness |
| Growth | Rapidly increasing | Increasing | Expand market share |
| Maturity | High or stable | Intense | Defend market position |
| Decline | Decreasing | Often decreasing | Reduce costs or withdraw |

The duration of each stage varies between products. Some products remain in maturity for decades, while others become outdated quickly.

## 4. Pricing Strategies Across the Product Life Cycle

Pricing decisions may change as a product moves through its life cycle.

### Price Skimming

Price skimming involves initially charging a high price for a new product and gradually reducing it over time.

It is commonly used when:

- The product is innovative.
- Early customers are willing to pay more.
- Competition is initially limited.
- The business has a strong brand or technological advantage.

Advantages:

- Helps recover development costs.
- Generates high revenue from early adopters.
- Creates a premium market position.

Disadvantages:

- High prices may limit initial sales.
- Competitors may introduce cheaper alternatives.
- Customers may delay purchases expecting future price reductions.

### Penetration Pricing

Penetration pricing involves introducing a product at a relatively low price to attract customers and gain market share quickly.

It is suitable when:

- The market is highly competitive.
- Customers are price-sensitive.
- Large-scale production can reduce costs.
- The business wants to establish a customer base quickly.

Advantages:

- Encourages product trials.
- Helps attract price-sensitive customers.
- Can discourage competitors from entering the market.

Disadvantages:

- Initial profit margins may be low.
- Customers may associate the product with low prices.
- Increasing prices later may be difficult.

## 5. Product Life Cycle Extension Strategies

Businesses sometimes attempt to extend the maturity stage or delay decline.

Common extension strategies include:

**Product modification:** Improving product quality, design, features, or performance.

**Market modification:** Finding new customer groups or entering new geographical markets.

**Marketing modification:** Changing promotional methods, distribution channels, or pricing strategies.

**Repositioning:** Changing how customers perceive the product compared with competing products.

**New uses:** Encouraging customers to use an existing product in additional ways.

Example:

A smartphone manufacturer may extend the life of an existing product line by introducing improved cameras, software updates, new colours, and additional storage options.

## 6. Limitations of the Product Life Cycle Model

Although the Product Life Cycle is useful, it has several limitations.

- Not every product follows the same four stages.
- The duration of each stage is difficult to predict.
- Some products experience renewed growth after decline.
- External factors can change demand unexpectedly.
- Product categories and individual brands may follow different life cycles.
- Businesses cannot always identify the current stage accurately.

The Product Life Cycle should therefore be treated as a planning framework rather than an exact prediction of future sales.

## 7. Meaning of New Product Development

New Product Development (NPD) is the process of creating and introducing new products or significantly improved products into the market.

It includes identifying customer needs, generating ideas, evaluating opportunities, developing prototypes, testing products, and launching them commercially.

Businesses develop new products to:

- Meet changing customer requirements.
- Respond to technological developments.
- Enter new markets.
- Increase revenue and profitability.
- Replace declining products.
- Gain competitive advantages.

## 8. Stages of New Product Development

### Idea Generation

The process begins with collecting ideas for new products.

Sources of ideas include:

- Customer feedback.
- Employee suggestions.
- Competitor analysis.
- Market research.
- Scientific research.
- Technological developments.
- Suppliers and distributors.

### Idea Screening

Businesses evaluate ideas and eliminate unsuitable ones.

An idea may be rejected if it has:

- Insufficient market demand.
- Excessive development costs.
- Technical difficulties.
- Limited profitability.
- Poor compatibility with business objectives.

### Concept Development and Testing

A promising idea is converted into a clear product concept.

The concept describes:

- Target customers.
- Main product benefits.
- Important features.
- Intended price range.
- Competitive advantages.

Potential customers may be shown the concept to collect feedback.

### Business Analysis

The business evaluates the financial and commercial feasibility of the product.

Important considerations include:

- Expected demand.
- Development expenditure.
- Production costs.
- Expected selling price.
- Estimated revenue.
- Profitability.
- Financial risks.

### Product Development

The business creates a working version or prototype of the product.

Activities may include:

- Designing the product.
- Developing technical specifications.
- Selecting materials.
- Building prototypes.
- Conducting performance tests.
- Improving product quality.

### Test Marketing

The product is introduced in a limited market or controlled environment to evaluate customer responses.

Businesses observe:

- Customer acceptance.
- Actual purchasing behaviour.
- Pricing effectiveness.
- Promotional performance.
- Distribution problems.

Test marketing helps identify problems before a full commercial launch.

### Commercialisation

Commercialisation is the full-scale introduction of the product into the market.

Important decisions include:

- Launch timing.
- Target geographical markets.
- Production capacity.
- Distribution arrangements.
- Promotional campaigns.
- Pricing decisions.

## 9. Research and Development

Research and Development (R&D) refers to systematic activities undertaken to create new knowledge, technologies, products, or processes and improve existing ones.

### Basic Research

Basic research aims to increase scientific or technical knowledge without necessarily targeting an immediate commercial application.

### Applied Research

Applied research uses existing knowledge to solve specific practical problems.

### Development

Development involves using research findings and technical knowledge to create or improve commercial products and processes.

R&D can help businesses achieve:

- Product innovation.
- Better quality.
- Reduced production costs.
- Improved efficiency.
- Technological advantages.
- Long-term competitiveness.

## 10. Types of Product Innovation

### Incremental Innovation

Incremental innovation involves making gradual improvements to existing products.

Examples:

- Improving smartphone battery life.
- Increasing the efficiency of a refrigerator.
- Adding new features to existing software.
- Improving the packaging of a food product.

### Radical Innovation

Radical innovation involves introducing major technological or conceptual changes that can create entirely new products or transform existing markets.

Examples include the development of the first commercially successful smartphones and major advances in electric vehicle technology.

### Disruptive Innovation

Disruptive innovation occurs when a new business model or technology changes an existing market, often by initially serving overlooked customers or offering a simpler or more affordable alternative.

Not every technological improvement is disruptive. The concept involves a broader change in market structure and competitive conditions.

## 11. Product Adoption Process

The product adoption process describes the stages through which an individual moves before accepting and regularly using a new product.

The main stages are:

1. Awareness: The customer learns that the product exists.
2. Interest: The customer seeks more information.
3. Evaluation: The customer considers its usefulness and advantages.
4. Trial: The customer tests the product on a limited basis.
5. Adoption: The customer decides to use the product regularly.

Businesses can support adoption through demonstrations, free trials, informative advertising, and customer support.

## 12. Categories of Adopters

Consumers adopt innovations at different speeds.

The five commonly recognised adopter categories are:

| Category | Approximate Share | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Innovators | 2.5% | First to experiment with new products |
| Early adopters | 13.5% | Adopt early and influence others |
| Early majority | 34% | Adopt after observing evidence of success |
| Late majority | 34% | Adopt after the product becomes widely accepted |
| Laggards | 16% | Adopt relatively late and prefer established products |

These percentages are part of the commonly used diffusion of innovations model.

## 13. Factors Affecting the Adoption of Innovation

The speed at which a product is adopted depends on several characteristics.

**Relative advantage:** The extent to which customers believe the new product is better than existing alternatives.

**Compatibility:** How well the product fits existing customer habits, values, and needs.

**Complexity:** How difficult the product is to understand and use.

**Trialability:** Whether customers can experiment with the product before purchasing it.

**Observability:** How easily customers can see the benefits of using the product.

Products that offer clear advantages and are easy to understand and test generally have better chances of gaining acceptance.

## 14. Reasons for New Product Failure

Not every new product succeeds in the market.

Common reasons include:

- Inadequate market research.
- Incorrect estimation of customer demand.
- Poor product quality.
- Uncompetitive pricing.
- Ineffective promotion.
- Weak distribution.
- Strong competitor responses.
- Technical problems.
- Poor timing.
- Failure to meet customer expectations.

Businesses can reduce failure risks through careful research, product testing, financial analysis, and continuous customer feedback.

## Common Exam Traps

- The Product Life Cycle has four traditional stages: introduction, growth, maturity, and decline.
- Sales generally increase rapidly during the growth stage.
- High competition and slower sales growth are common characteristics of maturity.
- Declining sales do not always mean that a product must immediately be discontinued.
- Price skimming begins with a relatively high price, while penetration pricing begins with a relatively low price.
- Test marketing takes place before full commercialisation.
- Idea screening eliminates unsuitable product ideas.
- Incremental innovation involves gradual improvements, while radical innovation involves major changes.
- Early adopters are different from innovators; they are the second adopter category.
- The five adopter categories are innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards.
