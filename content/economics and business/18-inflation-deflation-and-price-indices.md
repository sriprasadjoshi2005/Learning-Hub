Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services in an economy over time. It reduces the purchasing power of money when incomes do not increase at the same rate.

Understanding inflation is important for households, businesses, governments, and financial institutions because changes in prices influence consumption, investment, production, and economic stability.

## 1. Meaning of Inflation

Inflation refers to a continuous rise in the general price level of goods and services over a period of time.

It does not mean that the price of every individual product must increase.

For example, food prices may rise while the price of electronic products falls. Inflation occurs when the overall price level increases.

### Purchasing Power of Money

Purchasing power refers to the quantity of goods and services that a unit of money can purchase.

When the general price level rises, the purchasing power of money generally falls.

For example, if Rs. 100 purchased five units of a product earlier but now purchases only four units, the purchasing power of money has declined.

## 2. Types of Inflation Based on Speed

### Creeping Inflation

Creeping inflation refers to a relatively slow and gradual increase in prices.

It is generally associated with low inflation rates.

### Walking Inflation

Walking inflation refers to a moderate but noticeable increase in prices.

It can create pressure on household budgets and business costs.

### Running Inflation

Running inflation refers to a rapid increase in the general price level.

It can create uncertainty and reduce the stability of money.

### Hyperinflation

Hyperinflation is an extremely rapid and uncontrolled increase in prices.

It severely damages the purchasing power of money and may reduce confidence in the currency.

## 3. Causes of Inflation

Inflation can arise from several economic factors.

### A. Demand-Pull Inflation

Demand-pull inflation occurs when aggregate demand increases faster than the economy's ability to produce goods and services.

It is commonly explained as too much spending chasing a limited supply of output.

Possible causes include:

- Increased household consumption.
- Higher government expenditure.
- Increased business investment.
- Rapid growth in exports.
- Expansion of money and credit.

### B. Cost-Push Inflation

Cost-push inflation occurs when the cost of production increases and businesses raise prices to maintain their profit margins.

Possible causes include:

- Higher wages.
- Increased raw material prices.
- Higher energy costs.
- Increased transportation expenses.
- Supply shortages.
- Currency depreciation that raises import costs.

### C. Built-In Inflation

Built-in inflation can occur when workers demand higher wages to compensate for rising prices and businesses increase prices to cover higher labour costs.

This may create a wage-price spiral.

A wage-price spiral occurs when rising wages and rising prices reinforce each other.

## 4. Effects of Inflation

### Effects on Consumers

- Reduces purchasing power.
- Increases the cost of living.
- Creates pressure on fixed-income households.
- Makes long-term financial planning more difficult.

### Effects on Businesses

- Increases input and operating costs.
- Creates uncertainty about future prices.
- Makes budgeting more difficult.
- May encourage firms to revise selling prices.

### Effects on Savers and Borrowers

Unexpected inflation can reduce the real value of money held by savers.

Borrowers with fixed-rate loans may benefit if inflation reduces the real burden of their repayments.

### Effects on the Economy

- Can create uncertainty.
- May distort investment decisions.
- Can affect international competitiveness.
- May redistribute income and wealth between economic groups.

Moderate and predictable inflation is generally easier to manage than very high or unstable inflation.

## 5. Deflation and Disinflation

These concepts describe different changes in the price level.

**Deflation**

Deflation is a sustained decrease in the general price level.

It increases the purchasing power of money, but persistent deflation can discourage spending and investment.

Consumers may postpone purchases if they expect prices to fall further.

Businesses may experience lower revenue, reduced profits, and pressure to cut production or employment.

**Disinflation**

Disinflation refers to a reduction in the rate of inflation.

Prices are still increasing, but they are increasing more slowly.

Example:

- Inflation falls from 8% to 5%: disinflation.
- Inflation falls from 5% to -2%: deflation.

## 6. Consumer Price Index

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) measures changes in the average price of a selected basket of goods and services purchased by households.

The basket may include:

- Food and beverages.
- Housing.
- Clothing.
- Transportation.
- Healthcare.
- Education.
- Communication.

CPI is commonly used to measure changes in the cost of living.

CPI = (Cost of Basket in Current Year / Cost of Basket in Base Year) x 100

Example:

Cost of basket in base year = Rs. 2,000

Cost of basket in current year = Rs. 2,300

CPI = (2,300 / 2,000) x 100

CPI = 115

The price of the selected basket is 15% higher than in the base year.

## 7. Inflation Rate Using CPI

Inflation Rate = [(Current CPI - Previous CPI) / Previous CPI] x 100

Example:

Previous CPI = 120

Current CPI = 126

Inflation Rate = [(126 - 120) / 120] x 100

Inflation Rate = 5%

The inflation rate is 5%.

## 8. Wholesale Price Index

The Wholesale Price Index (WPI) measures changes in the prices of selected goods at the wholesale level.

It focuses on prices received or paid in bulk transactions rather than the final retail prices paid by consumers.

In India, WPI is used as an important indicator of price movements at the wholesale level.

CPI and WPI may move differently because they cover different baskets and stages of transactions.

## 9. GDP Deflator

The GDP deflator measures changes in the prices of all domestically produced final goods and services included in GDP.

GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) x 100

Unlike CPI, the GDP deflator is not restricted to a fixed basket of household consumer goods.

### CPI and GDP Deflator Comparison

| Feature | CPI | GDP Deflator |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main coverage | Household consumer basket | Domestically produced final output |
| Basket | Relatively fixed selected basket | Changes with current production |
| Imports | Imported consumer goods may be included | Imported goods are excluded |
| Main use | Measuring consumer price changes | Measuring price changes in domestic GDP |

## 10. Controlling Inflation

Governments and central banks use different policies to manage inflation.

### A. Monetary Policy

Monetary policy is implemented by the central bank to influence money, credit, and financial conditions.

Possible measures include:

- Increasing policy interest rates.
- Reducing excessive credit growth.
- Selling government securities through open market operations.
- Using reserve requirements and other liquidity measures.

Higher interest rates can reduce borrowing and spending, which may lower demand-pull inflation.

### B. Fiscal Policy

Fiscal policy involves government taxation and expenditure.

Possible anti-inflation measures include:

- Reducing unnecessary public expenditure.
- Increasing certain taxes to reduce excessive demand.
- Improving public expenditure efficiency.

### C. Supply-Side Measures

Supply-side measures aim to increase the availability of goods and services.

Examples include:

- Improving transport infrastructure.
- Increasing agricultural productivity.
- Reducing supply bottlenecks.
- Encouraging competition.
- Supporting efficient production.

## Common Exam Traps

- Inflation means a sustained increase in the general price level, not merely an increase in one product's price.
- Disinflation means inflation is slowing, not that prices are necessarily falling.
- Deflation means the general price level is decreasing.
- Demand-pull inflation originates from excess aggregate demand.
- Cost-push inflation originates from rising production costs.
- CPI focuses on a consumer basket, while the GDP deflator covers domestically produced final output.
- A higher CPI indicates a higher basket cost relative to the base year, not necessarily the current inflation rate by itself.
