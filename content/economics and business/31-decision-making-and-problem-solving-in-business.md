Business decision-making is the process of selecting the most suitable course of action from available alternatives. Managers make decisions about production, finance, marketing, employees, customers, and long-term strategy.

Effective decisions help organisations use resources efficiently, solve problems, reduce uncertainty, and achieve their objectives.

## 1. Meaning of Decision-Making

Decision-making involves identifying a situation that requires action, examining possible alternatives, evaluating their consequences, and selecting an appropriate option.

A business decision may be:

- Strategic: A long-term decision affecting the overall direction of the organisation.
- Tactical: A decision about implementing organisational strategies.
- Operational: A routine decision concerning everyday activities.

For example, entering a new country is a strategic decision, selecting a regional advertising campaign is tactical, and preparing the daily employee schedule is operational.

## 2. Types of Business Decisions

### Programmed Decisions

Programmed decisions are routine decisions made using established rules, procedures, or policies.

Examples include:

- Reordering products when stock reaches a specified level.
- Approving standard employee leave requests.
- Following established procedures for customer refunds.

These decisions are generally predictable and occur frequently.

### Non-Programmed Decisions

Non-programmed decisions concern unfamiliar, unusual, or complex situations that require individual judgement.

Examples include:

- Responding to a major economic crisis.
- Developing a completely new product.
- Deciding whether to acquire another company.

These decisions often involve uncertainty and greater risk.

### Individual and Group Decisions

Individual decisions are made by one person, usually within their area of authority.

Group decisions involve multiple people sharing information, evaluating alternatives, and reaching a conclusion.

Group decisions can introduce different perspectives, although they may require more time.

## 3. The Decision-Making Process

A systematic decision-making process improves the quality and consistency of business decisions.

The main stages are:

1. Identify the problem: Recognise the gap between the current situation and the desired outcome.
2. Diagnose the problem: Determine its underlying causes.
3. Establish objectives: Define what the decision should achieve.
4. Generate alternatives: Develop possible courses of action.
5. Evaluate alternatives: Compare costs, benefits, risks, and feasibility.
6. Select an alternative: Choose the most suitable option.
7. Implement the decision: Allocate resources and communicate responsibilities.
8. Monitor results: Measure performance and make adjustments when necessary.

**Example:**

A company experiences declining sales.

- Problem: Monthly sales have decreased.
- Diagnosis: Customers are switching to competitors because of poor delivery times.
- Alternatives: Improve logistics, introduce additional delivery partners, or open regional distribution centres.
- Evaluation: Compare costs and expected improvements.
- Decision: Introduce additional delivery partners.
- Monitoring: Compare delivery times and sales after implementation.

## 4. Rational Decision-Making

The rational decision-making model assumes that decision-makers have sufficient information, clearly defined objectives, and the ability to evaluate alternatives logically.

Its main characteristics include:

- Clearly identifying the problem.
- Collecting relevant information.
- Considering available alternatives.
- Evaluating expected consequences.
- Selecting the alternative that best achieves the objectives.

In practice, completely rational decisions are difficult because information, time, and resources are limited.

## 5. Bounded Rationality

Bounded rationality explains that people make decisions within limitations imposed by available information, cognitive ability, and time.

Instead of finding the theoretically perfect solution, decision-makers often choose an alternative that is sufficiently satisfactory.

This is called **satisficing**.

For example, a business may select a reliable supplier that meets its quality and delivery requirements rather than spending additional months searching for a potentially better supplier.

## 6. Decision-Making Under Different Conditions

Business decisions are made under different levels of information and uncertainty.

### Decision-Making Under Certainty

The decision-maker knows the available alternatives and their outcomes with reasonable confidence.

Example: Selecting a supplier whose prices and delivery conditions are contractually fixed.

### Decision-Making Under Risk

The possible outcomes are known, and probabilities can be estimated.

Expected monetary value can be calculated using:

Expected Value = Sum of (Probability × Outcome)

**Example:**

A business considers a project with the following possible outcomes:

| Outcome | Probability | Financial result |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| High demand | 0.6 | Rs. 100,000 |
| Low demand | 0.4 | Rs. 40,000 |

Expected Value = (0.6 × 100,000) + (0.4 × 40,000)

Expected Value = Rs. 76,000

The expected value is an average weighted by probabilities. It does not guarantee the actual result.

### Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

The decision-maker cannot reliably estimate the probabilities of possible outcomes.

Example: Launching a product in a completely unfamiliar market with limited information about customer behaviour.

## 7. Common Decision-Making Techniques

### Cost-Benefit Analysis

Cost-benefit analysis compares the expected costs of an action with its expected benefits.

Net Benefit = Total Benefits - Total Costs

An alternative with a positive net benefit may be attractive, provided that important risks and non-financial effects are also considered.

### Decision Trees

A decision tree is a diagram representing decisions, possible events, and their consequences.

It helps managers examine alternative paths and calculate expected outcomes.

### SWOT Analysis

SWOT analysis evaluates four dimensions:

| Factor | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Strengths | Internal advantages |
| Weaknesses | Internal limitations |
| Opportunities | External favourable conditions |
| Threats | External challenges |

It can help organisations understand their position before making strategic decisions.

### Brainstorming

Brainstorming encourages participants to generate ideas without immediately criticising them.

The ideas are evaluated later to identify practical solutions.

### Pareto Analysis

Pareto analysis is based on the observation that a relatively small number of causes may account for a large proportion of problems.

The 80/20 principle is commonly used as a practical guideline, not an exact universal law.

For example, a company may discover that a small number of recurring product defects account for most customer complaints.

## 8. Problem-Solving Methods

Problem-solving involves understanding the causes of an issue and developing actions that address it.

### Root Cause Analysis

Root cause analysis identifies the underlying reason behind a problem rather than treating only its visible symptoms.

### Five Whys Technique

The Five Whys technique repeatedly asks why a problem occurred until a deeper cause is identified.

Example:

- Problem: Orders are frequently delivered late.
- Why? Packages are dispatched late.
- Why? Packaging takes longer than expected.
- Why? The packaging process is inefficient.
- Why? The equipment is poorly arranged.
- Why? The original workspace layout was not designed for the current order volume.

The organisation can investigate the workspace layout instead of simply asking employees to work faster.

### Fishbone Diagram

A fishbone diagram, also called an Ishikawa diagram, organises possible causes of a problem into categories.

Common categories include:

- People
- Methods
- Machines
- Materials
- Measurement
- Environment

It helps teams investigate several possible causes systematically.

## 9. Biases and Barriers in Decision-Making

Decision-making can be affected by psychological and organisational limitations.

- Confirmation bias: Giving greater importance to information that supports existing beliefs.
- Anchoring bias: Depending too heavily on the first information received.
- Overconfidence: Overestimating the accuracy of one's judgement.
- Availability bias: Giving excessive importance to information that is easily recalled.
- Groupthink: Suppressing disagreement to maintain group harmony.
- Escalation of commitment: Continuing an unsuccessful course of action because significant resources have already been invested.

Managers can reduce these problems by encouraging disagreement, checking evidence, consulting different perspectives, and reviewing decisions objectively.

## 10. Evaluating Business Decisions

A decision should be evaluated after implementation.

Useful measures include:

- Financial performance.
- Customer satisfaction.
- Productivity.
- Quality.
- Employee performance.
- Risk reduction.
- Achievement of organisational objectives.

A decision that initially appears successful may still require modification if circumstances change.

## Common Exam Traps

- Programmed decisions concern routine and predictable situations; non-programmed decisions concern unusual problems.
- Satisficing means choosing an acceptable solution, not necessarily the mathematically optimal solution.
- Risk involves estimable probabilities, whereas uncertainty involves probabilities that cannot be reliably determined.
- Root cause analysis investigates underlying causes rather than only visible symptoms.
- The 80/20 principle is a guideline and does not mean every business problem follows an exact 80-to-20 ratio.
- Group decisions can improve the range of ideas but may also create groupthink.
