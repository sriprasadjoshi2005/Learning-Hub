title: Pricing Strategies and Methods

Businesses use different pricing strategies to achieve their financial and marketing objectives. The choice of strategy depends on production costs, customer demand, competition, product characteristics, and market positioning.

Pricing methods provide systematic approaches for determining the selling price of a product or service.

## 1. Meaning of Pricing Strategy

A pricing strategy is the overall approach a business uses to determine the prices of its products or services.

It considers the organisation's objectives, target customers, competitive environment, and expected profitability.

A pricing strategy should support the wider marketing strategy and maintain a balance between customer value and business sustainability.

## 2. Cost-Based Pricing

Cost-based pricing determines the selling price by considering production or purchase costs and adding a suitable profit margin.

### Cost-Plus Pricing

Cost-plus pricing involves adding a predetermined markup to the cost of producing or purchasing a product.

Formula:

Selling Price = Unit Cost + Markup

Example:

If the cost of producing a product is 500 and the business adds a markup of 20% of cost:

Markup = 500 x 0.20 = 100

Selling Price = 500 + 100 = 600

Advantages:

- Simple to calculate.
- Helps cover production costs.
- Provides a predictable markup.
- Useful when costs can be estimated accurately.

Disadvantages:

- May ignore customer demand.
- May overlook competitor prices.
- Does not guarantee that the product will sell.
- Can encourage inefficient cost structures.

### Target Return Pricing

Target return pricing sets a price intended to achieve a specified return on investment.

The business estimates the required revenue based on its investment and desired financial return.

This approach is useful when organisations have clear investment recovery objectives.

## 3. Competition-Based Pricing

Competition-based pricing determines prices by considering the prices charged by competing businesses.

### Going-Rate Pricing

The business sets its price close to the prevailing market price.

This approach is common in markets where products are relatively similar.

### Below-Competition Pricing

The business charges a lower price than competitors.

It may use this approach to attract price-sensitive customers and increase market share.

### Above-Competition Pricing

The business charges more than competitors.

This approach may be suitable when it offers superior quality, stronger branding, specialised features, or better customer service.

### Competitive Parity Pricing

The business maintains prices similar to those of major competitors.

This can reduce the risk of losing customers through significant price differences.

## 4. Value-Based Pricing

Value-based pricing determines prices according to the perceived value of a product to customers rather than relying only on production costs.

The business attempts to understand how much customers are willing to pay for the benefits offered.

Factors influencing perceived value include:

- Product quality.
- Convenience.
- Brand reputation.
- Customer service.
- Product uniqueness.
- Reliability.
- Emotional benefits.

Example:

A premium software product may charge more than a basic alternative because customers value its advanced features, security, reliability, and technical support.

Advantages:

- Aligns price with customer expectations.
- Can support higher profit margins.
- Encourages businesses to understand customer needs.
- Supports product differentiation.

Disadvantages:

- Customer value can be difficult to measure.
- Perceptions vary between customer groups.
- Requires continuous market research.
- Incorrect estimates can lead to unsuitable prices.

## 5. Penetration Pricing

Penetration pricing involves introducing a product at a relatively low price to attract customers and gain market share.

It is particularly useful when:

- Customers are price-sensitive.
- The market contains strong competitors.
- Large sales volumes can reduce average costs.
- The business wants rapid market acceptance.

The business may increase prices later after establishing its market position, although this must be managed carefully.

## 6. Price Skimming

Price skimming involves introducing a product at a relatively high price and gradually reducing it as the market develops.

It is suitable when:

- The product is innovative.
- Early customers are willing to pay more.
- Competition is initially limited.
- The business has a strong technological advantage.

Example:

A company may launch a new premium electronic device at a high price and gradually reduce its price as newer models become available.

## 7. Psychological Pricing

Psychological pricing uses customer perceptions to influence purchasing decisions.

### Odd-Even Pricing

Prices are set just below a rounded number.

Example:

A product priced at 199 may appear less expensive than one priced at 200.

### Prestige Pricing

A high price is used to communicate luxury, exclusivity, or superior quality.

### Price Bundling

Several products are offered together at one combined price.

Example:

A software company may sell multiple applications through one subscription package.

### Reference Pricing

A business displays a comparison price to help customers evaluate an offer.

For example, a product may be displayed with its original price and current selling price.

Reference prices must be presented honestly and comply with applicable consumer protection requirements.

## 8. Promotional Pricing

Promotional pricing involves temporarily reducing prices or offering special purchasing benefits to encourage sales.

Common techniques include:

- Seasonal discounts.
- Limited-period offers.
- Coupons.
- Cashback offers.
- Introductory discounts.
- Quantity discounts.
- Festival promotions.

Promotional pricing can increase short-term demand, attract new customers, and help clear excess inventory.

However, excessive discounting may reduce profit margins and create expectations of permanently low prices.

## 9. Differential Pricing

Differential pricing involves charging different prices to different customers or market segments for the same or similar product, depending on relevant conditions.

Examples include:

**Customer-segment pricing:** Different customer groups receive different prices.

**Location-based pricing:** Prices vary across geographical markets.

**Time-based pricing:** Prices change according to the time of purchase.

**Quantity-based pricing:** Customers purchasing larger quantities receive lower prices per unit.

**Dynamic pricing:** Prices change in response to demand, supply, availability, or other market conditions.

Differential pricing must comply with applicable laws and should not involve unlawful discrimination or deceptive practices.

## 10. Geographic Pricing

Geographic pricing considers differences in location and distribution costs.

Common approaches include:

### FOB-Origin Pricing

The customer pays the transportation cost from the seller's location.

The seller's price generally covers the product up to the specified shipping point, subject to the agreed terms.

### Uniform Delivered Pricing

The business charges the same delivered price to all customers, regardless of their location.

Transportation costs may be averaged across customers.

### Zone Pricing

The business divides geographical areas into zones and charges different prices for each zone.

### Freight-Absorption Pricing

The seller absorbs some or all transportation costs to remain competitive in a particular market.

## 11. Product Line Pricing

Product line pricing involves establishing prices for different products within the same product range.

The business considers differences in features, quality, production costs, and customer expectations.

Example:

A smartphone manufacturer may offer basic, standard, and premium models at different prices.

The price differences should communicate meaningful differences in product value.

## 12. Captive Product Pricing

Captive product pricing involves selling a main product and charging separately for complementary products required or commonly used with it.

Examples:

- Printers and ink cartridges.
- Gaming consoles and compatible games.
- Coffee machines and specialised capsules.

The main product may be priced competitively while complementary products generate additional revenue.

## 13. Optional Product Pricing

Optional product pricing involves charging additional amounts for optional features or accessories.

Examples:

- Additional vehicle equipment.
- Premium software features.
- Extended product warranties.
- Optional hotel services.

Customers can choose whether to purchase the additional features.

## 14. By-Product Pricing

By-product pricing involves selling secondary outputs generated during the production of a main product.

The business attempts to recover value from materials or outputs that might otherwise be discarded.

Example:

A food-processing company may sell certain agricultural residues for use in animal feed or industrial applications.

## 15. Choosing an Appropriate Pricing Strategy

Businesses should evaluate several factors before selecting a pricing strategy.

| Factor | Important Consideration |
| --- | --- |
| Production costs | Can the price cover costs? |
| Customer demand | How much are customers willing to pay? |
| Competition | What are competing businesses charging? |
| Product quality | Does the product justify a premium price? |
| Business objectives | Is the priority profit, growth, or survival? |
| Market conditions | Is the market growing or declining? |
| Legal requirements | Does the price comply with applicable regulations? |

A business may combine different pricing methods to achieve its objectives.

For example, it may use cost analysis to establish a minimum acceptable price, competitor analysis to understand market conditions, and value-based research to determine what customers are willing to pay.

## Common Exam Traps

- Cost-plus pricing adds a markup to product cost.
- Value-based pricing focuses on customer-perceived value.
- Competition-based pricing considers rival businesses' prices.
- Penetration pricing starts with a relatively low price.
- Price skimming starts with a relatively high price.
- Psychological pricing uses customer perceptions.
- Dynamic pricing changes prices according to market conditions.
- Captive product pricing involves a main product and complementary products.
- Optional product pricing charges separately for additional features.
- Promotional pricing is generally temporary.

## Quiz

1. A business produces a product at a cost of 400 and adds a markup of 25% of cost. What is its selling price?

- [ ] 425
- [ ] 450
- [x] 500
- [ ] 600

> Explanation: The markup is 400 x 0.25 = 100. Therefore, the selling price is 400 + 100 = 500.

2. Which pricing strategy focuses primarily on the value customers perceive in a product?

- [ ] Cost-plus pricing
- [x] Value-based pricing
- [ ] Going-rate pricing
- [ ] Penetration pricing

> Explanation: Value-based pricing determines prices according to customer-perceived benefits and willingness to pay.

3. A printer manufacturer sells its printers at competitive prices but earns additional revenue from compatible ink cartridges. Which pricing approach is this?

- [ ] Optional product pricing
- [ ] Geographic pricing
- [x] Captive product pricing
- [ ] Prestige pricing

> Explanation: Captive product pricing involves a main product and complementary products that customers commonly need to use with it.
