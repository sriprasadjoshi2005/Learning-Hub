Supply is a fundamental economic concept that explains the quantity of goods and services producers are willing and able to offer for sale at different prices during a particular period.

Understanding supply helps businesses plan production, manage costs, determine pricing strategies and respond to market conditions.

## 1. Meaning of Supply

**Supply** refers to the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at different prices during a particular period, assuming other factors remain constant.

Supply depends on both the willingness and ability of producers to sell a product.

For example, a manufacturer may be willing to produce 1,000 units of a product, but its actual supply may be limited by machinery, labour and raw material availability.

## 2. Individual Supply and Market Supply

### Individual Supply

Individual supply refers to the quantity of a product that a particular producer is willing and able to sell at different prices.

### Market Supply

Market supply represents the total quantity supplied by all producers in a market at different prices.

Market Supply = Sum of Individual Supplies

### Example

Suppose two firms supply a product at a price of Rs. 50.

- Firm A supplies 100 units.
- Firm B supplies 150 units.

Market Supply = 100 + 150 = 250 units.

Market supply is obtained by horizontally adding the quantities supplied by individual producers at each price.

## 3. The Law of Supply

The **Law of Supply** states that, other things remaining constant, the quantity supplied of a good generally increases when its price increases and decreases when its price decreases.

This establishes a direct relationship between price and quantity supplied.

### Supply Schedule

| Price (Rs.) | Quantity Supplied (Units) |
| --- | --- |
| 10 | 20 |
| 20 | 40 |
| 30 | 60 |
| 40 | 80 |
| 50 | 100 |

The schedule shows that producers are willing to supply greater quantities at higher prices.

### Supply Curve

A supply curve is a graphical representation of the relationship between the price of a product and its quantity supplied.

- Price is represented on the vertical axis.
- Quantity supplied is represented on the horizontal axis.
- The supply curve generally slopes upward from left to right.

## 4. Reasons for the Upward-Sloping Supply Curve

### Profit Incentive

Higher prices can increase potential revenue and encourage firms to expand production.

### Increasing Marginal Cost

As production expands, firms may need to use less efficient resources, overtime labour or more expensive inputs.

Consequently, additional production may require higher prices to remain profitable.

### Entry of New Producers

Higher market prices may attract additional businesses into an industry, increasing market supply.

### Resource Allocation

Producers may shift resources towards products offering higher returns.

## 5. Determinants of Supply

Supply is influenced by several factors other than the product's own price.

### Cost of Production

An increase in production costs generally reduces supply.

Examples:
- Higher wages.
- Increased electricity costs.
- More expensive raw materials.
- Higher transportation expenses.

### Technology

Improved technology can increase productivity and reduce production costs, increasing supply.

### Taxes and Subsidies

Higher production taxes may increase costs and reduce supply.

Government subsidies can lower production costs and encourage greater supply.

### Number of Producers

An increase in the number of firms generally increases market supply.

### Prices of Related Products

Producers may shift resources between alternative products depending on their relative profitability.

### Expectations

Expectations about future prices may influence present production and sales decisions.

### Natural Conditions

Agricultural supply can be influenced by:

- Rainfall.
- Temperature.
- Soil quality.
- Pests.
- Natural disasters.

## 6. Movement Along the Supply Curve

A movement along the supply curve occurs when the product's own price changes while other factors remain constant.

### Extension of Supply

Extension occurs when quantity supplied increases because the price increases.

### Contraction of Supply

Contraction occurs when quantity supplied decreases because the price decreases.

These changes represent movements along the same supply curve.

## 7. Shift in the Supply Curve

A shift occurs when supply changes because of factors other than the product's own price.

### Increase in Supply

An increase in supply shifts the entire supply curve to the right.

Possible causes:

- Improved production technology.
- Lower input costs.
- Government subsidies.
- Increase in the number of producers.
- Favourable production conditions.

### Decrease in Supply

A decrease in supply shifts the entire supply curve to the left.

Possible causes:

- Higher production costs.
- Increased taxes.
- Natural disasters.
- Reduced availability of resources.
- Closure of businesses.

## 8. Supply Function

A supply function expresses the relationship between quantity supplied and its determinants.

Qs = f(P, C, T, G, N, E)

Where:

- Qs = Quantity supplied.
- P = Price of the product.
- C = Cost of production.
- T = Technology.
- G = Government policies.
- N = Number of producers.
- E = Producer expectations.

A simplified linear supply function is:

Qs = a + bP

Where:

- a = Intercept.
- b = Positive coefficient.
- P = Price.
- Qs = Quantity supplied.

The positive coefficient represents the usual direct relationship between price and quantity supplied.

## 9. Exceptions to the Law of Supply

Although the law of supply describes a general relationship, some situations may not follow the usual pattern.

### Agricultural Products

Agricultural output may be limited by growing seasons, weather and biological conditions.

Higher prices cannot immediately increase the quantity available for sale.

### Perishable Goods

Producers may sell perishable goods quickly, even at lower prices, to avoid spoilage.

### Limited Resources

A producer with fixed productive capacity may be unable to increase output despite higher prices.

### Expectations of Future Prices

Producers may withhold products from the current market if they expect significantly higher prices in the future and storage is feasible.

### Labour Supply

In some circumstances, workers may reduce working hours after reaching a sufficiently high income because they value additional leisure.

This is an example of a backward-bending individual labour supply curve.

## Common Exam Traps

- The law of supply establishes a direct relationship between price and quantity supplied.
- Supply requires willingness and ability to sell.
- Market supply is the horizontal addition of individual supply schedules.
- A change in the product's own price causes movement along the supply curve.
- Changes in production costs or technology cause shifts in supply.
- Extension and contraction are movements along the supply curve.
- Increase and decrease in supply are shifts of the entire curve.
- Improved technology generally increases supply.
- Higher production costs generally decrease supply.
- Higher prices do not guarantee immediate increases in production when capacity is fixed.
