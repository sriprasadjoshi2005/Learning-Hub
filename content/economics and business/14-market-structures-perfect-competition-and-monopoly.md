Market structure refers to the characteristics and organisation of a market that influence competition, pricing, production and business behaviour.

Different market structures have different numbers of sellers, degrees of product differentiation, barriers to entry and levels of control over prices.

Perfect competition and monopoly represent two important market structures used in economic analysis.

## 1. Meaning of Market Structure

**Market structure** describes the competitive environment in which businesses operate.

It influences:

- Number of firms.
- Nature of products.
- Pricing decisions.
- Market entry and exit.
- Competition.
- Consumer choice.
- Business profitability.

The four commonly studied market structures are:

1. Perfect competition.
2. Monopoly.
3. Monopolistic competition.
4. Oligopoly.

This lesson focuses on perfect competition and monopoly.

## 2. Perfect Competition

**Perfect competition** is an idealised market structure in which a large number of buyers and sellers trade identical products, with no individual participant having significant control over the market price.

The market price is determined by the interaction of total demand and total supply.

### Characteristics of Perfect Competition

**Large Number of Buyers and Sellers**

The market contains many buyers and sellers, so individual participants cannot independently influence the market price.

**Homogeneous Products**

All firms sell identical or perfectly standardised products.

Consumers consider the products interchangeable.

**Free Entry and Exit**

Firms can enter or leave the industry without significant barriers in the long run.

**Perfect Information**

Buyers and sellers are assumed to have complete information about relevant prices and market conditions.

**Price-Taking Behaviour**

Individual firms accept the market price rather than independently setting it.

**Perfect Mobility of Factors**

Factors of production are assumed to move freely between alternative uses.

## 3. Revenue Under Perfect Competition

A perfectly competitive firm is a price taker.

Its average revenue equals the market price.

AR = TR / Q

For a firm selling all units at the market price:

AR = P

Marginal revenue also equals price:

MR = AR = P

Total Revenue:

TR = P x Q

Where:

- TR = Total Revenue.
- P = Market Price.
- Q = Quantity Sold.

### Example

A firm sells 100 units at Rs. 50 per unit.

TR = 100 x 50

TR = Rs. 5,000.

Average Revenue = 5,000 / 100

AR = Rs. 50.

## 4. Profit Maximisation Under Perfect Competition

A competitive firm generally maximises profit at the output where:

MR = MC

Since MR = P under perfect competition:

P = MC

The firm selects its profit-maximising output where marginal cost equals market price, subject to the relevant cost and shutdown conditions.

### Short-Run Outcomes

In the short run, a firm may experience:

- Supernormal profit.
- Normal profit.
- Economic loss.

A firm may continue operating during a short-run loss if revenue covers its variable costs and helps contribute towards fixed costs.

Under the standard model, a firm shuts down in the short run when price falls below minimum average variable cost.

## 5. Long-Run Equilibrium Under Perfect Competition

In the long run, firms can enter or leave the industry.

### Supernormal Profit

If firms earn economic profit, new firms may enter the industry.

This increases market supply and places downward pressure on market price.

### Economic Loss

If firms experience persistent economic losses, some may leave the industry.

This reduces market supply and places upward pressure on market price.

### Long-Run Equilibrium

Under the standard assumptions of perfect competition:

P = MR = MC = Minimum ATC

Firms earn normal profit, meaning economic profit is zero.

Normal profit is included as part of economic cost.

## 6. Advantages and Limitations of Perfect Competition

### Advantages

- Strong competitive pressure.
- Efficient allocation under ideal assumptions.
- Limited individual control over prices.
- Consumer access to standardised products.
- Incentives for cost efficiency.

### Limitations

- Perfect competition is an idealised model.
- Identical products may not reflect actual consumer preferences.
- Perfect information is rarely available.
- Free entry and exit may not exist in practice.
- Innovation and product differentiation may be less prominent in some perfectly competitive settings.

## 7. Monopoly

**Monopoly** is a market structure in which a single seller supplies a product or service for which close substitutes are unavailable, with significant barriers preventing competing firms from entering the market.

A monopolist has substantial market power, although its pricing decisions remain constrained by consumer demand and other market conditions.

### Characteristics of Monopoly

**Single Seller**

One firm supplies the relevant market.

**No Close Substitutes**

Consumers have limited alternatives for satisfying the same need.

**Barriers to Entry**

Other firms face obstacles that make market entry difficult.

**Price-Making Power**

A monopolist has the ability to influence market price by adjusting output, subject to demand.

**Possibility of Long-Run Economic Profit**

Strong entry barriers may allow a monopolist to maintain economic profit over an extended period.

## 8. Sources of Monopoly Power

### Legal Barriers

Governments may grant exclusive rights through patents, licences or other legal arrangements.

### Control of Essential Resources

A firm may control an important resource required for production.

### Natural Monopoly

A natural monopoly may arise when one supplier can serve the market at a lower average cost than multiple competing firms because of substantial economies of scale.

Examples may include certain utility infrastructure networks.

### Network Effects

A service may become more valuable as the number of users increases, making it difficult for competitors to attract customers.

### High Entry Costs

Large initial investment requirements may discourage new competitors.

## 9. Revenue Under Monopoly

A monopolist faces the market demand curve.

To sell additional units, it generally needs to reduce the price.

Therefore, marginal revenue is usually below average revenue.

For a downward-sloping linear demand curve:

MR < AR

Since:

AR = P

It follows that:

MR < P

A monopolist does not have a separate supply curve in the same way as a perfectly competitive firm because its output decision depends on both demand and cost conditions.

## 10. Profit Maximisation Under Monopoly

A monopolist generally selects output where:

MR = MC

It then determines the maximum price consumers are willing to pay for that output using the market demand curve.

Unlike a perfectly competitive firm, a monopolist generally has:

P > MR

At a standard interior profit-maximising equilibrium with an upward-sloping marginal cost curve, price is generally greater than marginal cost.

## 11. Advantages and Disadvantages of Monopoly

### Potential Advantages

- Large-scale production may create economies of scale.
- High profits may finance research and development.
- Certain infrastructure networks may be more efficient under a single provider.
- Stable investment may be possible in industries with high initial costs.

### Potential Disadvantages

- Higher prices may occur.
- Output may be restricted.
- Consumer choice may be limited.
- Competitive pressure may be weak.
- Productive inefficiency may arise.
- Market power may be abused.

The actual effects depend on industry characteristics, regulation and business behaviour.

## 12. Comparison Between Perfect Competition and Monopoly

| Feature | Perfect Competition | Monopoly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of sellers | Very large | One |
| Product | Homogeneous | No close substitutes |
| Entry barriers | Absent in the ideal model | Significant |
| Price control | Individual firms are price takers | Firm has substantial price-setting power |
| Demand curve for firm | Horizontal | Downward-sloping |
| Relationship between price and MR | P = MR | P > MR |
| Long-run economic profit | Zero under standard assumptions | May remain positive |
| Consumer choice | Many sellers | Limited alternatives |

## 13. Market Power and Regulation

Market power refers to the ability of a business to influence market prices or trading conditions.

Governments may regulate businesses with significant market power to protect consumers and promote fair competition.

Possible measures include:

- Competition laws.
- Price regulation.
- Consumer protection rules.
- Anti-cartel enforcement.
- Merger review.
- Public utility regulation.

The objective is to prevent harmful market practices while maintaining incentives for investment and innovation.

## Common Exam Traps

- Perfect competition assumes many buyers and sellers.
- A perfectly competitive firm is a price taker.
- Under perfect competition, P = AR = MR.
- Monopoly involves a single seller in the relevant market.
- A monopoly requires significant barriers to entry and the absence of close substitutes.
- A monopolist generally faces a downward-sloping demand curve.
- Under monopoly, marginal revenue is generally below average revenue.
- Both competitive firms and monopolists generally use MR = MC as the profit-maximisation condition.
- A monopolist generally charges a price above marginal revenue.
- Normal profit means zero economic profit, not zero accounting profit.
- A monopolist does not necessarily earn a profit; it can also experience losses.
- Natural monopoly arises from cost conditions, particularly substantial economies of scale.
