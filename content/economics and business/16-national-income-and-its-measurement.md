title: National Income and Its Measurement
level: intermediate
---

National income measures the total income earned by the residents of a country from producing goods and services during a specific period, usually one financial year. It helps economists understand the size, structure, and performance of an economy.

## 1. Meaning of National Income

National income is the total factor income earned by the normal residents of a country during an accounting year.

Factor income refers to income earned by providing factors of production:

- **Wages and salaries:** Income earned by labour.
- **Rent:** Income earned by owners of land and property.
- **Interest:** Income earned by providing capital.
- **Profit:** Income earned by entrepreneurs.

National income does not simply measure the amount of money circulating in an economy. It measures income generated through production.

## 2. Related National Income Concepts

Several economic measures are used to calculate national output and income.

### Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

GDP is the total market value of all final goods and services produced within the domestic territory of a country during a given period.

### Gross National Product (GNP)

GNP measures the value of final goods and services produced using the factors of production owned by the normal residents of a country, including their net factor income from abroad.

GNP = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad

### Net National Product (NNP)

NNP is obtained by subtracting depreciation from GNP.

NNP = GNP - Depreciation

Depreciation represents the loss in value of fixed assets due to wear and tear, ageing, or obsolescence.

### National Income

In the traditional factor-cost approach:

National Income = NNP at Factor Cost

Factor cost refers to the actual income received by factors of production.

## 3. Gross and Net Concepts

The terms gross and net distinguish between measures that include or exclude depreciation.

- **Gross:** Includes depreciation.
- **Net:** Excludes depreciation.

For example, gross domestic product includes the value of production before deducting depreciation. Net domestic product deducts depreciation.

NDP = GDP - Depreciation

## 4. Domestic and National Concepts

The terms domestic and national distinguish between production within a country's territory and income earned by its residents.

- **Domestic:** Based on the geographical territory where production takes place.
- **National:** Based on the normal residents who earn the income.

Net Factor Income from Abroad is calculated as:

NFIA = Factor Income Received from Abroad - Factor Income Paid Abroad

Therefore:

GNP = GDP + NFIA

If residents earn more factor income from abroad than foreign residents earn domestically, NFIA is positive.

If factor income paid abroad exceeds factor income received from abroad, NFIA is negative.

## 5. Methods of Measuring National Income

There are three major methods of measuring national income.

### A. Product or Value Added Method

This method calculates national income by measuring the value added by each producing unit.

Value Added = Value of Output - Intermediate Consumption

Intermediate consumption refers to goods and services used up during production.

Example:

A farmer sells wheat for Rs. 500 to a flour mill. The mill sells flour for Rs. 800 to a bakery. The bakery sells bread for Rs. 1,200.

| Producer | Value of Output | Intermediate Consumption | Value Added |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Farmer | 500 | 0 | 500 |
| Flour mill | 800 | 500 | 300 |
| Bakery | 1,200 | 800 | 400 |
| Total |  |  | 1,200 |

Total value added is Rs. 1,200.

This method avoids double counting because it measures only the additional value created at each stage.

### B. Income Method

This method calculates national income by adding factor incomes earned during production.

National Income = Wages + Rent + Interest + Profit + Mixed Income

Mixed income refers to the combined income of self-employed individuals whose labour income and entrepreneurial profit cannot easily be separated.

Transfer payments, such as pensions and unemployment allowances, are excluded because they are not payments for current production.

### C. Expenditure Method

This method measures national income by adding expenditure on final goods and services.

GDP = C + I + G + (X - M)

Where:

- C = Private final consumption expenditure
- I = Investment expenditure
- G = Government expenditure
- X = Exports
- M = Imports

Imports are subtracted because they represent goods and services produced outside the domestic economy.

## 6. Double Counting

Double counting occurs when the value of the same good or service is included more than once while calculating national output.

For example, adding the full value of wheat, flour, and bread would count the value of intermediate products repeatedly.

It can be avoided by:

- Counting only final goods and services.
- Using the value-added method.
- Excluding intermediate consumption from final output calculations.

## 7. Nominal and Real National Income

National income can be measured using current or constant prices.

**Nominal National Income**

Nominal national income measures output using the prices prevailing during the current period.

It can increase because of higher production, higher prices, or both.

**Real National Income**

Real national income measures output using the prices of a selected base year.

It adjusts for changes in the general price level and provides a clearer picture of changes in actual production.

Real GDP = (Nominal GDP / GDP Deflator) x 100

## 8. Importance of National Income Accounting

National income statistics help governments, businesses, and researchers understand economic performance.

Major uses include:

- Measuring the size of an economy.
- Comparing economic performance across years.
- Estimating economic growth.
- Formulating fiscal and monetary policies.
- Understanding the contribution of different sectors.
- Supporting business investment decisions.
- Comparing living standards across countries.

## 9. Limitations of National Income Measurement

National income figures do not capture every aspect of economic well-being.

Important limitations include:

- **Income inequality:** A high national income does not mean that income is distributed equally.
- **Unpaid work:** Household work and voluntary activities are generally not included in measured market output.
- **Informal economy:** Some economic activities may be difficult to record accurately.
- **Environmental damage:** GDP may rise even when production causes pollution or resource depletion.
- **Quality of life:** Leisure, health, happiness, and personal security are not directly measured.
- **Data limitations:** Incomplete records and estimation difficulties can reduce accuracy.

## Common Exam Traps

- GDP measures production within domestic territory, not production by residents worldwide.
- GNP includes net factor income from abroad.
- NNP is calculated after deducting depreciation.
- Intermediate goods should not be counted separately when their value is already included in final goods.
- Transfer payments are not factor incomes from current production.
- Nominal income can rise because of inflation even when real production remains unchanged.
- The income method adds factor incomes, while the expenditure method adds spending on final output.

   - [ ] Rs. 750 billion
   - [ ] Rs. 800 billion
   - [x] Rs. 850 billion
   - [ ] Rs. 900 billion
