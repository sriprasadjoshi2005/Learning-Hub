title: Monopolistic Competition and Oligopoly
level: intermediate

Monopolistic competition and oligopoly are two important market structures found in modern economies.

Unlike perfect competition and monopoly, these structures involve varying degrees of product differentiation, competition and interdependence between firms.

Understanding them helps explain pricing strategies, advertising, branding, competition and strategic business decisions.

## 1. Monopolistic Competition

**Monopolistic competition** is a market structure in which many firms sell products that are similar but differentiated through features such as quality, branding, design, location and customer service.

Each firm has some control over the price of its product because consumers may prefer its particular characteristics.

However, competition from other firms limits this pricing power.

### Examples

Markets that may display characteristics of monopolistic competition include:

- Restaurants.
- Clothing brands.
- Personal care products.
- Coffee shops.
- Hair salons.
- Consumer service businesses.

The actual market structure depends on how the relevant market is defined.

## 2. Characteristics of Monopolistic Competition

### Large Number of Firms

Many businesses operate in the market, and each firm controls only a relatively small share.

### Product Differentiation

Products are similar but not identical.

Differentiation may involve:

- Quality.
- Design.
- Packaging.
- Branding.
- Customer service.
- Location.
- Product features.

### Relatively Free Entry and Exit

Businesses can generally enter or leave the market without the substantial barriers found in monopoly markets.

### Some Degree of Price Control

Firms have limited pricing power because consumers may prefer their differentiated products.

### Non-Price Competition

Businesses compete through advertising, promotions, branding and service quality, not only through price.

### Imperfect Information

Consumers may not have complete information about all available products and prices.

## 3. Demand Curve Under Monopolistic Competition

A firm under monopolistic competition faces a downward-sloping demand curve.

This occurs because consumers may prefer the firm's differentiated product.

However, the presence of competing firms makes demand relatively responsive to price and product changes.

If a firm raises its price significantly, consumers may switch to competing brands.

## 4. Revenue Under Monopolistic Competition

Average Revenue is generally equal to price:

AR = TR / Q

For a firm with a downward-sloping demand curve:

MR < AR

Marginal revenue is lower than average revenue because selling additional units generally requires reducing price.

## 5. Short-Run Equilibrium

In the short run, a firm under monopolistic competition may earn:

- Supernormal profit.
- Normal profit.
- Economic loss.

The firm generally selects output where:

MR = MC

The corresponding selling price is determined from its demand curve.

## 6. Long-Run Equilibrium

If firms earn supernormal economic profit, new competitors may enter the market.

New firms introduce additional product varieties and attract some customers.

As a result, the demand facing existing firms may decrease.

If firms experience persistent losses, some may leave the market, potentially improving the demand conditions of remaining firms.

Under the traditional long-run model:

- Firms earn normal profit.
- The demand curve is tangent to the average total cost curve.
- Price generally exceeds marginal cost.
- Firms may operate with excess capacity.

### Excess Capacity

Excess capacity occurs when a firm produces less than the output level that minimises average total cost.

This is a characteristic of the standard long-run monopolistic competition model.

## 7. Advantages of Monopolistic Competition

- Provides consumer choice.
- Encourages product differentiation.
- Promotes branding and innovation.
- Allows new businesses to enter markets.
- Encourages competition through quality and service.
- Supports specialised products for different consumer preferences.

## 8. Limitations of Monopolistic Competition

- Advertising may increase business costs.
- Product differentiation can make price comparisons difficult.
- Firms may operate with excess capacity.
- Prices may exceed marginal cost.
- Some advertising may create artificial brand preferences.
- Competition may involve unnecessary duplication of products.

## 9. Oligopoly

**Oligopoly** is a market structure dominated by a small number of large firms whose decisions significantly influence one another.

Because only a few major businesses operate in the market, each firm must consider how competitors may respond to its pricing, production and marketing decisions.

### Examples

Industries that may exhibit oligopolistic characteristics include:

- Commercial aviation.
- Telecommunications.
- Automobile manufacturing.
- Cement production.
- Certain consumer technology markets.

Market classification depends on the relevant geographical market and product definition.

## 10. Characteristics of Oligopoly

### Few Dominant Firms

A small number of firms account for a significant share of market activity.

### Interdependence

Each firm's decisions may affect the profits and strategies of competitors.

### Significant Entry Barriers

New businesses may face high capital requirements, technology barriers, strong brands or regulatory restrictions.

### Product Differentiation

Products may be either homogeneous or differentiated.

### Non-Price Competition

Firms may compete through:

- Advertising.
- Product development.
- Customer service.
- Technology.
- Brand image.
- Loyalty programmes.

### Strategic Behaviour

Firms consider possible competitor reactions before making major decisions.

## 11. Types of Oligopoly

### Pure Oligopoly

A pure oligopoly involves firms selling relatively homogeneous products.

Example: Certain standardised industrial materials.

### Differentiated Oligopoly

A differentiated oligopoly involves firms selling products with different brands, features or qualities.

Example: Certain automobile and smartphone markets.

### Collusive Oligopoly

Collusion occurs when firms coordinate their decisions to influence prices, output or market conditions.

Such arrangements may violate competition laws.

### Non-Collusive Oligopoly

Firms make independent decisions while considering the likely reactions of competitors.

## 12. Price Rigidity

Price rigidity refers to a situation in which prices remain relatively stable despite changes in market conditions.

In oligopoly, firms may hesitate to change prices because competitors could respond strategically.

### Kinked Demand Curve Model

The kinked demand curve is a traditional model used to explain price rigidity in oligopoly.

It assumes:

- If a firm increases its price, competitors may not follow, causing the firm to lose customers.
- If a firm reduces its price, competitors may also reduce their prices, limiting the firm's potential gain.

The demand curve is therefore more elastic above the existing price and less elastic below it in the traditional model.

This can create a discontinuity in the marginal revenue curve and help explain why prices may remain stable.

The model is an explanation of possible price rigidity, not a universal description of every oligopoly.

## 13. Game Theory and Strategic Decisions

Game theory studies strategic interactions in which the outcome for one participant depends partly on the decisions of others.

It is particularly useful for analysing oligopoly because firms are interdependent.

### Example: Pricing Decisions

Suppose two competing firms must decide whether to maintain or reduce their prices.

Each firm's profit depends on:

- Its own pricing decision.
- The competitor's pricing decision.
- Customer responses.
- Production costs.

A firm may choose a strategy based on the expected actions of its competitor.

### Prisoner's Dilemma

The prisoner's dilemma is a game theory model in which individually rational decisions can produce an outcome that is worse for all participants than cooperation would have been.

In business economics, it helps explain why firms may engage in aggressive competition even when mutual cooperation appears more profitable.

## 14. Comparison of Monopolistic Competition and Oligopoly

| Feature | Monopolistic Competition | Oligopoly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of firms | Many | Few dominant firms |
| Product differentiation | Generally present | May be present or absent |
| Entry barriers | Relatively low | Generally significant |
| Interdependence | Limited | High |
| Price control | Some | Considerable but strategically constrained |
| Non-price competition | Common | Common |
| Strategic interaction | Less central | Very important |
| Examples | Restaurants and clothing brands | Telecommunications and automobiles |

## 15. Comparison of the Four Major Market Structures

| Feature | Perfect Competition | Monopoly | Monopolistic Competition | Oligopoly |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Number of firms | Very large | One | Many | Few dominant firms |
| Product | Identical | No close substitutes | Differentiated | Identical or differentiated |
| Entry barriers | Very low in the ideal model | High | Relatively low | Generally high |
| Price control | None for individual firms | Substantial | Limited | Strategic |
| Interdependence | Negligible | Not applicable in the standard model | Limited | Significant |
| Long-run economic profit | Zero under standard assumptions | May remain positive | Zero under standard assumptions | May remain positive |

## 16. Importance for Business Strategy

Understanding market structures helps businesses:

- Identify competitors.
- Develop pricing strategies.
- Differentiate products.
- Understand customer preferences.
- Evaluate market entry.
- Plan advertising expenditure.
- Anticipate competitor reactions.
- Develop competitive advantages.

Market structure also influences government competition policy and consumer welfare.

## Common Exam Traps

- Monopolistic competition involves many firms selling differentiated products.
- Oligopoly involves a small number of dominant and interdependent firms.
- Product differentiation is a major characteristic of monopolistic competition.
- Entry barriers are generally higher in oligopoly than in monopolistic competition.
- Firms under monopolistic competition have some pricing power but face competition from substitutes.
- Oligopolistic firms must consider the likely reactions of competitors.
- Collusion involves coordination between competing firms.
- The kinked demand curve model explains possible price rigidity in oligopoly.
- Game theory analyses strategic decision-making.
- The prisoner's dilemma demonstrates how individually rational decisions can produce collectively inferior outcomes.
- In the traditional long-run monopolistic competition model, firms earn normal economic profit but may operate with excess capacity.
- Oligopolies do not necessarily engage in collusion; many compete independently.
