title: Financial Ratio Analysis and Business Performance

Financial ratio analysis is a method of evaluating the financial position and performance of an organisation by comparing related figures from financial statements.

It helps managers, investors, lenders, and other stakeholders understand profitability, liquidity, efficiency, and financial risk.

Ratios make it easier to compare financial information across different periods and, with appropriate adjustments, between organisations.

## 1. Meaning of Financial Ratio Analysis

A financial ratio expresses the relationship between two or more financial figures.

For example, the current ratio compares current assets with current liabilities.

Financial ratio analysis helps answer questions such as:

- Is the business generating sufficient profit?
- Can it meet short-term obligations?
- Is it using its assets efficiently?
- Does it depend heavily on borrowed funds?
- Is its financial performance improving?
- How does it compare with competitors?

Ratios should be interpreted in context rather than viewed as isolated numbers.

## 2. Categories of Financial Ratios

Financial ratios are commonly grouped into four major categories:

1. Liquidity ratios.
2. Profitability ratios.
3. Efficiency or activity ratios.
4. Solvency or leverage ratios.

Each category examines a different aspect of business performance.

## 3. Liquidity Ratios

Liquidity ratios measure the ability of a business to meet short-term financial obligations.

### Current Ratio

Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities

Example:

Current Assets = Rs. 600,000.

Current Liabilities = Rs. 300,000.

Current Ratio = 600,000 / 300,000

Current Ratio = 2:1.

This indicates Rs. 2 of current assets for every Rs. 1 of current liabilities.

### Quick Ratio

The quick ratio measures short-term payment ability using relatively liquid current assets.

Quick Ratio = (Current Assets - Inventory - Prepaid Expenses) / Current Liabilities

Example:

- Current Assets = Rs. 500,000.
- Inventory = Rs. 150,000.
- Prepaid Expenses = Rs. 50,000.
- Current Liabilities = Rs. 200,000.

Quick Ratio = (500,000 - 150,000 - 50,000) / 200,000

Quick Ratio = 1.5:1.

### Cash Ratio

The cash ratio measures the ability to meet current liabilities using cash and cash equivalents.

Cash Ratio = (Cash + Cash Equivalents) / Current Liabilities

It is a stricter liquidity measure than the current ratio because it excludes inventory and receivables.

## 4. Profitability Ratios

Profitability ratios measure the ability of a business to generate profits relative to revenue, assets, or equity.

### Gross Profit Margin

Gross Profit Margin = (Gross Profit / Revenue) × 100

Example:

Revenue = Rs. 500,000.

Gross Profit = Rs. 200,000.

Gross Profit Margin = (200,000 / 500,000) × 100

Gross Profit Margin = 40%.

This means gross profit represents 40% of revenue.

### Operating Profit Margin

Operating Profit Margin = (Operating Profit / Revenue) × 100

It measures the proportion of revenue remaining after operating expenses, before relevant finance costs and taxes.

### Net Profit Margin

Net Profit Margin = (Net Profit / Revenue) × 100

Example:

Net Profit = Rs. 70,000.

Revenue = Rs. 500,000.

Net Profit Margin = (70,000 / 500,000) × 100

Net Profit Margin = 14%.

This indicates that the business earns Rs. 14 in net profit for every Rs. 100 of revenue.

### Return on Assets

Return on Assets measures how effectively a business uses its assets to generate profit.

A commonly used formula is:

ROA = (Net Profit / Average Total Assets) × 100

The exact profit measure used may vary according to the analytical purpose.

### Return on Equity

Return on Equity measures the return generated relative to shareholders' equity.

ROE = (Profit Attributable to Ordinary Shareholders / Average Ordinary Shareholders' Equity) × 100

A higher ROE may indicate stronger returns on shareholders' investment, but it should be examined alongside financial leverage and risk.

## 5. Efficiency Ratios

Efficiency ratios measure how effectively a business uses its assets and manages its operating activities.

### Inventory Turnover Ratio

Inventory Turnover Ratio = Cost of Goods Sold / Average Inventory

Average Inventory = (Opening Inventory + Closing Inventory) / 2

Example:

Cost of Goods Sold = Rs. 600,000.

Opening Inventory = Rs. 80,000.

Closing Inventory = Rs. 120,000.

Average Inventory = (80,000 + 120,000) / 2

Average Inventory = Rs. 100,000.

Inventory Turnover Ratio = 600,000 / 100,000

Inventory Turnover Ratio = 6 times.

### Receivables Turnover Ratio

Receivables Turnover Ratio = Net Credit Sales / Average Trade Receivables

It measures how frequently a business collects its average trade receivables during a period.

### Average Collection Period

Average Collection Period = (Average Trade Receivables / Net Credit Sales) × Number of Days

Example:

Average Trade Receivables = Rs. 50,000.

Annual Net Credit Sales = Rs. 600,000.

Using 365 days:

Average Collection Period = (50,000 / 600,000) × 365

Average Collection Period is approximately 30.4 days.

### Payables Turnover Ratio

Payables Turnover Ratio = Net Credit Purchases / Average Trade Payables

It measures how frequently a business pays its average trade payables during a period.

### Total Asset Turnover Ratio

Total Asset Turnover Ratio = Revenue / Average Total Assets

It measures how efficiently a business uses its assets to generate revenue.

## 6. Solvency and Leverage Ratios

Solvency ratios examine the ability of an organisation to meet long-term obligations.

They also help evaluate the extent to which a business relies on borrowed funds.

### Debt-to-Equity Ratio

Debt-to-Equity Ratio = Total Debt / Total Equity

Example:

Total Debt = Rs. 400,000.

Total Equity = Rs. 200,000.

Debt-to-Equity Ratio = 400,000 / 200,000

Debt-to-Equity Ratio = 2:1.

This means the business has Rs. 2 of debt for every Rs. 1 of equity, based on the figures and debt definition used.

### Debt Ratio

Debt Ratio = Total Liabilities / Total Assets

It measures the proportion of assets financed by liabilities.

### Interest Coverage Ratio

Interest Coverage Ratio = Earnings Before Interest and Tax / Interest Expense

It measures how many times operating earnings cover interest expenses.

A higher ratio generally indicates a greater ability to meet interest obligations.

## 7. Market-Based Ratios

Market-based ratios are particularly relevant to publicly traded companies.

### Earnings Per Share

Earnings Per Share measures the profit attributable to each ordinary share.

EPS = (Profit Attributable to Ordinary Shareholders - Preference Dividends) / Weighted Average Ordinary Shares Outstanding

### Price-to-Earnings Ratio

P/E Ratio = Market Price per Share / Earnings Per Share

It shows how much investors are paying for each unit of earnings per share.

A high P/E ratio may reflect growth expectations, but it can also indicate that a share is expensive relative to current earnings.

### Dividend Yield

Dividend Yield = (Annual Dividend per Share / Market Price per Share) × 100

It measures the annual dividend return relative to the market price of a share.

## 8. Comparative Financial Analysis

Financial ratios become more useful when compared with appropriate reference points.

### Horizontal Analysis

Horizontal analysis compares financial information across different periods.

Example:

A business compares revenue, expenses, and profit over three consecutive years to identify trends.

### Vertical Analysis

Vertical analysis expresses each financial statement item as a percentage of a selected base figure.

For an income statement, revenue is commonly used as the base.

For a balance sheet, total assets may be used as the base.

### Trend Analysis

Trend analysis examines changes in financial figures over several periods.

It helps identify patterns such as increasing expenses, declining profit margins, or growing debt.

### Benchmarking

Benchmarking compares an organisation's performance with industry standards, competitors, or recognised best practices.

Comparisons should account for differences in business models, accounting policies, and operating conditions.

## 9. Interpreting Financial Ratios

Ratios must be interpreted carefully.

For example:

- A higher current ratio may indicate stronger liquidity, but it may also reflect excessive inventory or idle cash.
- A higher gross profit margin may indicate better pricing or cost control.
- A high inventory turnover ratio may indicate efficient stock management, but it may also indicate insufficient inventory.
- A high debt-to-equity ratio may indicate greater financial risk.
- A high return on equity may partly result from high borrowing rather than improved operating performance.

A ratio that is favourable for one industry may be unsuitable for another.

## 10. DuPont Analysis

DuPont analysis explains the factors contributing to return on equity.

A common three-part DuPont formula is:

ROE = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier

Where:

Net Profit Margin = Net Income / Revenue

Asset Turnover = Revenue / Average Total Assets

Equity Multiplier = Average Total Assets / Average Equity

The three components represent:

- Profitability.
- Asset efficiency.
- Financial leverage.

This analysis helps managers identify whether changes in return on equity are caused by profit margins, asset utilisation, or leverage.

## 11. Limitations of Financial Ratio Analysis

Financial ratios are useful but have limitations.

- They are generally based on historical information.
- Inflation can distort comparisons.
- Different accounting policies may reduce comparability.
- Seasonal businesses may show unusual period-end figures.
- Ratios do not directly measure all qualitative factors.
- A single ratio cannot fully explain financial performance.
- Industry differences make universal benchmarks unreliable.
- Management estimates can influence reported figures.

Ratio analysis should be combined with financial statement review, business knowledge, and relevant economic information.

## Common Exam Traps

- Liquidity ratios measure short-term payment ability.
- Solvency ratios examine long-term financial stability.
- Gross profit margin uses gross profit, while net profit margin uses net profit.
- Inventory turnover uses cost of goods sold divided by average inventory.
- The current ratio includes inventory; the quick ratio excludes inventory and commonly prepaid expenses.
- Debt-to-equity compares debt with equity, while the debt ratio compares liabilities with assets.
- Interest coverage uses earnings before interest and tax divided by interest expense.
- A high return on equity may be influenced by financial leverage.
- Financial ratios should be compared with appropriate industry and historical benchmarks.

## Quiz

1. Which financial ratio measures the ability of a business to meet current liabilities using relatively liquid assets?

- [ ] Gross profit margin
- [ ] Debt-to-equity ratio
- [x] Quick ratio
- [ ] Inventory turnover ratio

> The quick ratio excludes inventory and commonly prepaid expenses to focus on relatively liquid current assets.

2. A business has gross profit of Rs. 150,000 and revenue of Rs. 500,000. What is its gross profit margin?

- [ ] 15%
- [ ] 25%
- [x] 30%
- [ ] 40%

> Gross Profit Margin = (Gross Profit / Revenue) × 100 = (150,000 / 500,000) × 100 = 30%.

3. Which ratio measures the relationship between total debt and shareholders' equity?

- [ ] Current ratio
- [ ] Asset turnover ratio
- [ ] Interest coverage ratio
- [x] Debt-to-equity ratio

> The debt-to-equity ratio compares the amount of debt with the amount of equity used to finance the business.
