Financial planning is the process of estimating the financial resources required by a business and determining how those resources should be obtained and used.

Forecasting estimates future financial conditions, while budgeting converts organisational plans into financial and operational targets.

Together, these activities help businesses control expenses, manage cash flow, allocate resources, and prepare for future opportunities and risks.

## 1. Meaning of Financial Planning

Financial planning involves determining the financial requirements of an organisation and developing strategies to meet them.

Its main objectives include:

- Estimating future funding requirements.
- Maintaining sufficient liquidity.
- Supporting investment decisions.
- Controlling costs.
- Managing financial risks.
- Improving resource allocation.
- Supporting long-term growth.

Financial planning connects organisational objectives with financial resources.

## 2. Importance of Financial Planning

Financial planning helps businesses prepare for expected and unexpected financial requirements.

Its importance includes:

- Reducing the possibility of cash shortages.
- Identifying potential funding gaps.
- Improving investment decisions.
- Controlling unnecessary expenditure.
- Coordinating departmental activities.
- Measuring financial performance.
- Supporting business expansion.
- Improving communication between managers.

Without proper financial planning, a profitable business may still experience difficulties if it cannot meet immediate payment obligations.

## 3. Financial Forecasting

Financial forecasting is the process of estimating future financial results using historical information, current conditions, assumptions, and expected business activities.

Forecasts may include:

- Sales revenue.
- Production costs.
- Operating expenses.
- Cash receipts.
- Cash payments.
- Profit.
- Capital requirements.

### Sales Forecast

A sales forecast estimates the revenue expected from future sales.

A basic formula is:

Forecast Revenue = Expected Sales Quantity × Expected Selling Price

Example:

A business expects to sell 2,000 units at Rs. 500 each.

Forecast Revenue = 2,000 × 500

Forecast Revenue = Rs. 10,00,000.

### Cost Forecast

Cost forecasting estimates the expenses expected during a future period.

It may include:

- Fixed costs.
- Variable costs.
- Labour expenses.
- Material costs.
- Transportation expenses.
- Administrative expenses.

### Cash Flow Forecast

A cash flow forecast estimates future cash inflows and outflows.

It helps identify when additional funds may be needed.

## 4. Meaning of a Budget

A budget is a formal financial or quantitative plan prepared for a specified future period.

It expresses expected activities and resource requirements in measurable terms.

Budgets may be prepared for:

- A department.
- A project.
- A product.
- A business unit.
- An entire organisation.

A budget provides a standard against which actual performance can be compared.

## 5. Objectives of Budgeting

Budgeting serves several purposes:

- Planning: Establishing future targets.
- Coordination: Connecting activities across departments.
- Communication: Sharing expected objectives and resource limits.
- Control: Comparing actual results with planned results.
- Performance evaluation: Assessing managerial and organisational performance.
- Resource allocation: Distributing funds according to priorities.

## 6. Types of Budgets

### Sales Budget

A sales budget estimates the quantity and value of products or services expected to be sold.

It is often used as the starting point for other budgets.

### Production Budget

A production budget estimates the number of units that should be manufactured to meet expected sales and inventory requirements.

Production Budget = Budgeted Sales + Desired Closing Finished Goods Inventory - Opening Finished Goods Inventory

### Materials Budget

A materials budget estimates the quantity and cost of raw materials required for production.

### Labour Budget

A labour budget estimates the workforce hours and labour costs required to complete planned production.

### Overhead Budget

An overhead budget estimates indirect production costs such as factory rent, utilities, and maintenance.

### Cash Budget

A cash budget estimates expected cash receipts, cash payments, and closing cash balances.

### Capital Expenditure Budget

A capital expenditure budget plans major purchases of long-term assets such as buildings, vehicles, and machinery.

### Master Budget

A master budget combines the major budgets of an organisation into an overall financial plan.

It commonly includes operating budgets and financial budgets.

## 7. Fixed Budget and Flexible Budget

### Fixed Budget

A fixed budget is prepared for one specified level of activity.

It does not automatically change when actual production or sales volume differs from the planned level.

It is relatively simple but may be unsuitable when activity levels vary considerably.

### Flexible Budget

A flexible budget adjusts according to the actual level of activity.

It helps compare costs more fairly when production or sales volume changes.

Example:

A business estimates variable costs of Rs. 20 per unit.

| Production Volume | Expected Variable Cost |
| --- | ---: |
| 1,000 units | Rs. 20,000 |
| 1,500 units | Rs. 30,000 |
| 2,000 units | Rs. 40,000 |

A flexible budget adjusts expected variable costs according to actual production volume.

## 8. Incremental Budgeting

Incremental budgeting uses an existing budget as the starting point and adjusts it for expected changes.

For example, a department may increase its previous year's budget by 5%.

### Advantages

- Simple to prepare.
- Requires less time.
- Provides continuity.
- Useful when activities remain relatively stable.

### Limitations

- May preserve unnecessary expenditure.
- Can encourage departments to spend their entire allocated budgets.
- May not reflect changing organisational priorities.

## 9. Zero-Based Budgeting

Zero-Based Budgeting requires activities and expenditure proposals to be justified from the beginning of each budgeting period.

Instead of automatically carrying forward previous allocations, managers examine whether each expense is necessary.

### Advantages

- Encourages cost control.
- Identifies unnecessary expenditure.
- Supports resource allocation based on priorities.
- Challenges outdated activities.

### Limitations

- Time-consuming.
- Requires detailed analysis.
- Can create administrative complexity.
- May be difficult to implement across very large organisations.

## 10. Budgetary Control

Budgetary control is the process of comparing actual performance with budgeted performance and taking corrective action when necessary.

The main stages include:

1. Establish budgets.
2. Communicate budget targets.
3. Record actual performance.
4. Compare actual results with budgeted figures.
5. Identify significant differences.
6. Investigate the causes.
7. Take corrective action.
8. Review future budgets.

Budgetary control helps managers identify financial problems before they become serious.

## 11. Variance Analysis

Variance analysis examines the difference between actual results and budgeted or standard results.

Variance = Actual Result - Budgeted Result

A favourable or adverse interpretation depends on the type of item being measured.

For revenue, actual revenue above budget is generally favourable.

For expenses, actual expenses above budget are generally adverse.

### Example: Sales Variance

Budgeted Sales Revenue = Rs. 500,000.

Actual Sales Revenue = Rs. 550,000.

Sales Variance = 550,000 - 500,000

Sales Variance = Rs. 50,000 favourable.

### Example: Expense Variance

Budgeted Expenses = Rs. 100,000.

Actual Expenses = Rs. 120,000.

Expense Variance = 120,000 - 100,000

Expense Variance = Rs. 20,000 adverse.

A variance does not automatically indicate poor management. Managers should investigate its causes and consider changes in business activity and external conditions.

## 12. Break-Even Analysis and Financial Planning

Break-even analysis identifies the level of sales at which total revenue equals total costs.

At the break-even point:

Total Revenue = Total Cost

Break-Even Quantity = Fixed Costs / Contribution per Unit

Contribution per Unit = Selling Price per Unit - Variable Cost per Unit

### Example

A business has:

- Fixed Costs = Rs. 60,000.
- Selling Price = Rs. 100 per unit.
- Variable Cost = Rs. 60 per unit.

Contribution per Unit = 100 - 60 = Rs. 40.

Break-Even Quantity = 60,000 / 40

Break-Even Quantity = 1,500 units.

The business must sell 1,500 units to cover its total costs under these assumptions.

## 13. Scenario Planning

Scenario planning examines how different possible future conditions could affect business performance.

A business may develop:

- Optimistic scenario.
- Expected or base scenario.
- Pessimistic scenario.

For example, a manufacturer may prepare financial plans for high demand, normal demand, and a significant decline in demand.

Scenario planning helps businesses prepare for uncertainty rather than depending on a single forecast.

## 14. Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis examines how changes in one or more assumptions affect a financial result.

For example, a business may evaluate how its profit changes when:

- Selling prices decrease.
- Raw material costs increase.
- Sales volume changes.
- Interest rates rise.
- Exchange rates fluctuate.

It helps identify the assumptions that have the greatest influence on financial performance.

## 15. Limitations of Financial Planning and Budgeting

Financial planning and budgeting are useful but have limitations.

- Forecasts may be inaccurate.
- Unexpected economic changes may affect results.
- Budgets can become outdated.
- Excessively rigid budgets may reduce flexibility.
- Preparing detailed budgets requires time and resources.
- Managers may manipulate estimates to achieve targets.
- Employees may resist unrealistic financial restrictions.

Budgets should be reviewed regularly and adjusted when circumstances change.

## Common Exam Traps

- A forecast estimates future outcomes; a budget establishes a formal plan or target.
- A production budget considers sales and desired inventory levels.
- A fixed budget is prepared for one activity level, while a flexible budget adjusts to activity.
- Incremental budgeting starts from an existing budget.
- Zero-based budgeting requires expenditure to be justified rather than automatically carried forward.
- Budgetary control compares actual performance with planned performance.
- A favourable revenue variance generally occurs when actual revenue exceeds budget.
- A favourable expense variance generally occurs when actual expenses are lower than budget.
- Scenario planning examines different possible futures, while sensitivity analysis studies the effect of changing assumptions.
