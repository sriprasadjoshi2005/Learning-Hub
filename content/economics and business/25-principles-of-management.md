Management is the process of planning, organising, staffing, directing, and controlling resources to achieve organisational objectives efficiently and effectively.

Management is essential because businesses require coordination between people, finances, technology, materials, and information.

## 1. Meaning of Management

Management is the process of achieving organisational goals through the effective and efficient use of resources.

**Effectiveness**

Effectiveness means achieving the intended objectives.

Example: A business successfully reaches its target of selling 10,000 products.

**Efficiency**

Efficiency means achieving objectives while using resources economically.

Example: A factory produces the same number of products using less raw material and time.

An organisation should aim to be both effective and efficient.

## 2. Importance of Management

Management contributes to organisational success by:

- Establishing clear objectives.
- Coordinating employee activities.
- Improving productivity.
- Reducing unnecessary costs.
- Supporting innovation.
- Managing uncertainty.
- Improving communication.
- Maintaining discipline.
- Encouraging employee development.
- Ensuring effective resource utilisation.

## 3. Functions of Management

The major functions of management are planning, organising, staffing, directing, and controlling.

### A. Planning

Planning involves deciding in advance what needs to be achieved and how it will be achieved.

It includes:

- Establishing objectives.
- Analysing available resources.
- Evaluating alternatives.
- Selecting strategies.
- Preparing action plans.
- Establishing schedules.

Example:

A company plans to launch a new product within six months by setting a budget, identifying suppliers, and preparing a marketing strategy.

### B. Organising

Organising involves arranging resources and assigning responsibilities to achieve planned objectives.

It includes:

- Dividing work.
- Creating departments.
- Assigning authority.
- Establishing reporting relationships.
- Coordinating activities.

Example:

A business creates separate marketing, finance, production, and human resources departments.

### C. Staffing

Staffing involves obtaining, developing, and retaining suitable employees.

It includes:

- Workforce planning.
- Recruitment.
- Selection.
- Training.
- Performance evaluation.
- Compensation.
- Employee development.

### D. Directing

Directing involves guiding, instructing, motivating, and supervising employees.

It includes:

- Leadership.
- Communication.
- Motivation.
- Supervision.
- Guidance.

### E. Controlling

Controlling involves comparing actual performance with planned standards and taking corrective action when necessary.

The process includes:

1. Establishing performance standards.
2. Measuring actual performance.
3. Comparing actual performance with standards.
4. Identifying deviations.
5. Taking corrective action.

Example:

A company discovers that production costs are 12% above the planned budget and investigates the causes.

## 4. Levels of Management

Management is commonly divided into three levels.

### A. Top-Level Management

Top-level management is responsible for the overall direction and long-term strategy of the organisation.

Examples include:

- Chief Executive Officer.
- Managing Director.
- President.
- Board-level executives.

Responsibilities include:

- Setting organisational objectives.
- Establishing major policies.
- Approving strategic decisions.
- Managing external relationships.
- Evaluating overall performance.

### B. Middle-Level Management

Middle-level managers connect top-level management with operational employees.

Examples include:

- Department managers.
- Regional managers.
- Branch managers.
- Production managers.

Responsibilities include:

- Implementing organisational plans.
- Coordinating departments.
- Supervising lower-level managers.
- Allocating departmental resources.
- Reporting performance.

### C. Lower-Level Management

Lower-level management directly supervises employees involved in routine operations.

Examples include:

- Supervisors.
- Team leaders.
- Forepersons.
- Shift managers.

Responsibilities include:

- Assigning daily tasks.
- Monitoring work.
- Maintaining quality.
- Reporting operational problems.
- Supporting employees.

## 5. Henri Fayol's Principles of Management

Henri Fayol developed 14 principles that provide guidance for organising and managing businesses.

### 1. Division of Work

Work should be divided into specialised tasks to improve efficiency and expertise.

### 2. Authority and Responsibility

Managers need authority to perform their duties, and authority should be accompanied by responsibility.

### 3. Discipline

Employees and managers should follow organisational rules and agreements.

### 4. Unity of Command

Each employee should ideally receive orders from one superior to avoid conflicting instructions.

### 5. Unity of Direction

Activities with the same objective should be guided by one plan and one manager.

### 6. Subordination of Individual Interest to General Interest

Organisational interests should take priority over individual interests when they conflict.

### 7. Remuneration

Employees should receive fair and reasonable compensation.

### 8. Centralisation

The degree of decision-making authority retained at higher levels should be appropriate to the organisation's circumstances.

### 9. Scalar Chain

There should be a clear line of authority from the highest to the lowest level of management.

### 10. Order

People and materials should be placed in suitable positions to support efficient operations.

### 11. Equity

Managers should treat employees with fairness, kindness, and impartiality.

### 12. Stability of Tenure of Personnel

Reasonable employee stability supports experience, continuity, and organisational efficiency.

### 13. Initiative

Employees should be encouraged to develop ideas and take responsible action.

### 14. Esprit de Corps

Management should encourage unity, teamwork, and organisational spirit.

## 6. F. W. Taylor and Scientific Management

Frederick Winslow Taylor is associated with the development of scientific management.

Scientific management aims to improve productivity by analysing work scientifically rather than relying only on traditional methods.

### Principles of Scientific Management

**Science, Not Rule of Thumb**

Work methods should be studied scientifically to identify efficient procedures.

**Harmony, Not Discord**

Management and workers should cooperate rather than remain in conflict.

**Cooperation, Not Individualism**

Managers and workers should work together to achieve organisational objectives.

**Development of Each Person to Greatest Efficiency**

Employees should be selected, trained, and developed according to their abilities.

### Techniques of Scientific Management

**Time Study**

Determines the standard time required to complete a task.

**Motion Study**

Examines movements involved in performing work and identifies unnecessary movements.

**Method Study**

Identifies the most effective way of performing a task.

**Fatigue Study**

Examines how rest periods and working conditions affect employee fatigue.

**Differential Piece Rate System**

Provides different wage rates depending on whether workers meet or exceed specified production standards.

## 7. Management Skills

Managers require different skills depending on their responsibilities.

### A. Technical Skills

Technical skills involve knowledge of specific methods, processes, and tools.

Examples include accounting knowledge, programming, manufacturing procedures, and data analysis.

These skills are particularly important for lower-level managers.

### B. Human Skills

Human skills involve communicating, motivating, cooperating, and working effectively with people.

They are important at every level of management.

### C. Conceptual Skills

Conceptual skills involve understanding the organisation as a whole and analysing how different parts interact.

They are particularly important for top-level managers.

## 8. Leadership and Management

Leadership and management are related but distinct concepts.

**Management**

Management focuses on planning, organising, coordinating, and controlling resources.

**Leadership**

Leadership focuses on influencing and motivating people towards shared objectives.

A successful manager often requires leadership abilities, while effective leadership also benefits from organisational and management skills.

## 9. Management Styles

### A. Autocratic Management

The manager makes decisions with limited employee participation.

Advantages:

- Quick decision-making.
- Clear authority.
- Useful during emergencies.

Disadvantages:

- Limited employee participation.
- May reduce motivation.
- Can restrict creativity.

### B. Democratic Management

Employees participate in discussions and decision-making.

Advantages:

- Encourages participation.
- Improves communication.
- Can increase employee commitment.

Disadvantages:

- Decision-making may take longer.
- Disagreements may delay action.

### C. Laissez-Faire Management

Employees receive substantial freedom to make decisions and organise their work.

Advantages:

- Encourages independence.
- Supports creativity.
- Suitable for experienced teams.

Disadvantages:

- May create coordination problems.
- Responsibilities can become unclear.
- Requires capable and self-motivated employees.

## 10. Management and Organisational Performance

Effective management improves performance by coordinating different organisational functions.

For example, launching a new product requires:

- Marketing to identify customer needs.
- Finance to allocate funds.
- Production to manufacture the product.
- Human resources to recruit suitable employees.
- Management to coordinate the entire process.

Without coordination, departments may work towards conflicting priorities.

## Common Exam Traps

- Effectiveness means achieving objectives, while efficiency means using resources economically.
- Planning determines what should be done and how.
- Organising allocates resources and responsibilities.
- Staffing deals with obtaining and developing employees.
- Directing involves guiding and motivating employees.
- Controlling compares actual performance with planned standards.
- Unity of command means one employee should ideally receive orders from one superior.
- Unity of direction means activities with the same objective follow one plan.
- Taylor is associated with scientific management, while Fayol is associated with administrative management principles.
- Technical skills are especially important at lower management levels, while conceptual skills are particularly important at top levels.
- Human skills are important at every level of management.
