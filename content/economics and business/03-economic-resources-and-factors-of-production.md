---
title: Economic Resources and Factors of Production
level: foundation
---

Economic resources are the inputs used to produce goods and services. Since these resources are limited, their efficient utilisation is essential for economic growth and business success.

The traditional economic classification identifies four major factors of production: land, labour, capital and entrepreneurship.

Understanding these factors helps explain how production takes place and how income is distributed among resource owners.

## 1. Meaning of Factors of Production

**Factors of production** are the resources used by businesses and organisations to create goods and services.

For example, a bakery requires:

- Land or premises for its operations.
- Labour provided by bakers and employees.
- Capital in the form of ovens and equipment.
- Entrepreneurship to organise production and manage business risks.

All four factors contribute to the production process.

## 2. Land

Land refers to all natural resources used in production.

It includes more than physical plots of land. It also includes natural resources available from the environment.

### Examples

- Agricultural land.
- Forests.
- Rivers and water resources.
- Minerals.
- Petroleum and natural gas.
- Solar and wind energy.

### Characteristics of Land

- Land is a gift of nature.
- Its total physical supply is relatively fixed.
- Its quality and productivity vary across locations.
- It is geographically immobile.
- It can have alternative uses.

### Reward for Land

The traditional economic reward for land is **rent**.

Rent is the payment received by landowners for allowing others to use their land.

## 3. Labour

Labour refers to the physical and mental effort contributed by people during the production of goods and services.

### Types of Labour

**Physical Labour:** Work involving physical effort.

Examples:
- Construction workers.
- Agricultural workers.
- Factory employees.

**Mental Labour:** Work involving intellectual effort and specialised knowledge.

Examples:
- Engineers.
- Doctors.
- Accountants.
- Software developers.

### Characteristics of Labour

- Labour is a human factor of production.
- It cannot be separated from the person providing it.
- Its productivity depends on education, training and experience.
- Labour is relatively mobile, although geographical and occupational barriers may exist.
- Labour productivity varies across individuals.

### Division of Labour

Division of labour refers to dividing a production process into specialised tasks performed by different workers.

For example, automobile production may involve separate teams responsible for:

- Engine manufacturing.
- Body assembly.
- Painting.
- Electrical systems.
- Quality testing.

Specialisation can increase productivity, reduce production time and improve efficiency.

### Reward for Labour

The traditional reward for labour is **wages and salaries**.

## 4. Capital

Capital refers to produced assets used to create additional goods and services.

It is different from money alone. Money becomes productive financial capital when it is used to acquire productive resources or finance economic activity.

### Types of Capital

| Type | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical capital | Man-made productive assets | Machinery |
| Human capital | Skills, knowledge and abilities | Employee training |
| Financial capital | Funds available for investment | Business investment funds |
| Fixed capital | Assets used over several production cycles | Buildings and equipment |
| Working capital | Resources used for daily operations | Inventory and cash |

### Characteristics of Capital

- Capital is produced through investment.
- It increases productive capacity.
- It can improve labour productivity.
- It may depreciate over time.
- It can be accumulated through saving and investment.

### Depreciation

Depreciation refers to the reduction in the value of a fixed asset over time due to usage, ageing or obsolescence.

For example, industrial machinery may lose value as it becomes older and newer technology becomes available.

### Reward for Capital

The traditional reward for capital is **interest**.

## 5. Entrepreneurship

Entrepreneurship is the ability to identify opportunities, organise resources, introduce innovations and undertake business risks.

An entrepreneur combines the other factors of production to establish and operate a business.

### Functions of an Entrepreneur

- Identifying business opportunities.
- Organising land, labour and capital.
- Making strategic decisions.
- Introducing innovations.
- Managing uncertainty and risk.
- Coordinating production.
- Developing markets and customers.

### Characteristics of Entrepreneurship

- Innovation.
- Decision-making.
- Leadership.
- Risk-bearing.
- Opportunity recognition.
- Resource coordination.

### Reward for Entrepreneurship

The traditional economic reward for entrepreneurship is **profit**.

Profit represents the return earned after accounting for business costs.

## 6. Factor Payments

The traditional relationship between factors of production and their rewards is:

| Factor of Production | Reward |
| --- | --- |
| Land | Rent |
| Labour | Wages |
| Capital | Interest |
| Entrepreneurship | Profit |

These payments form an important part of the distribution of national income.

In modern economies, actual income arrangements can be more complex. For example, an entrepreneur may also receive a salary as a company employee.

## 7. Productivity of Factors of Production

Productivity measures the output produced relative to the inputs used.

Labour Productivity = Total Output / Labour Input

### Example

A factory produces 1,000 units using 20 workers.

Labour Productivity = 1,000 / 20

Labour Productivity = 50 units per worker

If the factory increases output to 1,200 units using the same number of workers:

Labour Productivity = 1,200 / 20

Labour Productivity = 60 units per worker

Productivity can improve through:

- Better technology.
- Employee training.
- Improved management.
- Specialisation.
- Better infrastructure.
- Efficient resource allocation.

## 8. Technology and Modern Production

Modern production increasingly combines traditional factors with technology, data and automation.

Examples include:

- Robots in automobile manufacturing.
- Artificial intelligence in business operations.
- Automated agricultural equipment.
- Digital platforms in financial services.
- Computerised inventory management.

Technology can improve productivity, although it may also change employment requirements and the demand for particular skills.

## Common Exam Traps

- Land includes all natural resources, not merely agricultural or construction land.
- Labour includes both physical and mental effort.
- Capital refers to productive assets, not simply cash or money.
- Human capital represents skills, education and knowledge embodied in people.
- Entrepreneurship involves organising resources, innovation and risk-bearing.
- The traditional reward for capital is interest, while the reward for entrepreneurship is profit.
- Depreciation reduces the value of fixed assets over time.
- Higher output does not necessarily mean higher productivity if input usage increases proportionately or more rapidly.

- [ ] 20 units per worker
- [ ] 25 units per worker
