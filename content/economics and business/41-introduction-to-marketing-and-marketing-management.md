Marketing is the process of understanding customer needs, creating valuable products and services, communicating their benefits, and making them available to customers.

It connects businesses with their markets and helps organisations build customer relationships, generate revenue, and achieve competitive advantage.

Marketing is not limited to advertising or selling. It includes market research, product development, pricing, distribution, customer service, and relationship management.

## 1. Meaning of Marketing

Marketing is a broad business activity concerned with identifying customer needs and satisfying them through valuable exchanges.

Its major activities include:

- Understanding customer requirements.
- Identifying target markets.
- Developing suitable products and services.
- Determining appropriate prices.
- Selecting distribution channels.
- Communicating product value.
- Building long-term customer relationships.

**Example:**

A company planning to introduce a new smartphone must understand customer preferences, determine the required features, establish a suitable price, arrange distribution, and communicate the product's benefits.

These activities form part of marketing.

## 2. Marketing and Selling

Marketing and selling are related but different concepts.

| Basis | Marketing | Selling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main focus | Customer needs | Products and transactions |
| Starting point | Understanding the market | Existing products |
| Objective | Creating customer value | Completing sales |
| Approach | Broad and long-term | Often transaction-oriented |
| Activities | Research, product, pricing, distribution, promotion | Persuasion and sales conversion |

Selling is one component of marketing.

A business that focuses only on selling may achieve short-term sales without developing lasting customer relationships.

## 3. Importance of Marketing

Marketing contributes to business success in several ways.

### Understanding Customers

Marketing research helps businesses understand customer needs, preferences, expectations, and purchasing behaviour.

### Creating Demand

Advertising, promotions, and communication can introduce products and encourage customers to consider purchasing them.

### Building Brand Awareness

Marketing helps customers recognise and remember a business and its products.

### Increasing Sales

Effective marketing can attract customers, improve conversion rates, and encourage repeat purchases.

### Supporting Innovation

Customer feedback and market research help organisations identify opportunities for product improvement and development.

### Building Customer Relationships

Marketing supports customer satisfaction, loyalty, and long-term engagement.

## 4. Evolution of Marketing Concepts

Different marketing philosophies influence how businesses approach their markets.

### Production Concept

The production concept assumes that customers prefer products that are widely available and affordable.

Businesses focus on improving production efficiency and increasing output.

It may be suitable when demand exceeds supply or when reducing production costs is a major priority.

### Product Concept

The product concept assumes that customers prefer products offering superior quality, features, and performance.

Businesses concentrate on product improvement and technical excellence.

A limitation is that organisations may become too focused on product features and overlook actual customer needs.

### Selling Concept

The selling concept emphasises aggressive selling and promotional activities to encourage customers to purchase products.

It is often associated with products that customers may not actively seek.

### Marketing Concept

The marketing concept focuses on identifying target customer needs and satisfying them more effectively than competitors.

It places customer requirements at the centre of business decisions.

### Societal Marketing Concept

The societal marketing concept considers customer satisfaction, organisational objectives, and the long-term welfare of society.

It encourages businesses to consider environmental, ethical, and social consequences.

## 5. Marketing Management

Marketing management involves planning, organising, implementing, and controlling marketing activities to achieve organisational objectives.

Its responsibilities include:

- Analysing market opportunities.
- Selecting target customers.
- Developing marketing strategies.
- Managing product decisions.
- Establishing pricing policies.
- Coordinating promotion.
- Managing distribution.
- Evaluating marketing performance.

Marketing management ensures that different marketing activities work together.

## 6. Marketing Objectives

Marketing objectives should support the overall goals of the organisation.

Common marketing objectives include:

- Increasing market share.
- Improving brand awareness.
- Attracting new customers.
- Increasing customer retention.
- Launching new products.
- Improving customer satisfaction.
- Increasing sales revenue.
- Entering new geographical markets.

Effective objectives should be clear and measurable.

### SMART Objectives

SMART is a framework for developing effective objectives.

- Specific: Clearly states what should be achieved.
- Measurable: Includes a method of measurement.
- Achievable: Is realistic given available resources.
- Relevant: Supports important organisational goals.
- Time-bound: Specifies a deadline.

Example:

Increase online sales by 15% within the next six months.

This is more useful than simply stating that the business wants to improve sales.

## 7. Marketing Environment

The marketing environment consists of internal and external factors that influence marketing decisions.

### Internal Environment

Internal factors are within the organisation.

Examples include:

- Financial resources.
- Employees.
- Management.
- Organisational culture.
- Production capacity.
- Technology.

### External Environment

External factors exist outside the organisation.

They may influence demand, competition, costs, and customer behaviour.

The external environment is commonly divided into microenvironment and macroenvironment.

## 8. Marketing Microenvironment

The microenvironment includes individuals and organisations that directly influence the company's ability to serve customers.

Important elements include:

### The Company

Different departments must coordinate to support marketing objectives.

### Suppliers

Suppliers provide the materials, components, and services required by the business.

### Marketing Intermediaries

Intermediaries help promote, distribute, and sell products.

Examples include wholesalers, retailers, and distribution partners.

### Customers

Customers are the individuals or organisations purchasing products and services.

### Competitors

Competitors offer alternative products or services and influence pricing, positioning, and market strategy.

### Publics

Publics are groups that may influence or be influenced by the organisation.

Examples include media organisations, financial institutions, communities, and government bodies.

## 9. Marketing Macroenvironment

The macroenvironment includes broader external forces that influence organisations and markets.

PESTLE analysis is commonly used to examine these forces.

| Factor | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Political | Government policies and political conditions | Trade restrictions |
| Economic | Economic conditions | Inflation and interest rates |
| Social | Cultural and demographic changes | Lifestyle preferences |
| Technological | Technological developments | Artificial intelligence |
| Legal | Laws and regulations | Consumer protection laws |
| Environmental | Ecological conditions | Climate change |

Businesses monitor these forces because they can create opportunities and threats.

## 10. Marketing Information Systems

A Marketing Information System collects, analyses, and distributes information required for marketing decisions.

It may include:

- Internal sales records.
- Customer databases.
- Market intelligence.
- Competitor information.
- Marketing research reports.
- Digital analytics.

A well-designed information system helps managers make evidence-based decisions.

## 11. Marketing Ethics and Social Responsibility

Marketing activities should be conducted honestly and responsibly.

Important principles include:

- Accurate product claims.
- Transparent pricing.
- Respect for customer privacy.
- Responsible advertising.
- Fair treatment of customers.
- Avoidance of misleading promotions.
- Protection of vulnerable consumers.
- Environmental responsibility.

Unethical marketing may damage customer trust and create legal and reputational risks.

## 12. Measuring Marketing Performance

Businesses evaluate marketing performance using measurable indicators.

Common measures include:

- Sales revenue.
- Market share.
- Customer acquisition cost.
- Customer retention rate.
- Conversion rate.
- Customer satisfaction.
- Brand awareness.
- Return on marketing investment.

Marketing performance should be assessed against clearly defined objectives.

## Common Exam Traps

- Marketing is broader than selling and includes research, product development, pricing, distribution, and promotion.
- The production concept focuses on availability and affordability.
- The product concept emphasises product quality and features.
- The selling concept focuses on persuasive selling activities.
- The marketing concept begins with customer needs.
- The societal marketing concept considers customers and wider social welfare.
- Suppliers and competitors belong to the microenvironment.
- Inflation is an economic factor in PESTLE analysis.
- SMART objectives must be measurable and time-bound.
