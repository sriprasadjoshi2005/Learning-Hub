Operations management is concerned with designing, organising, and controlling the processes through which an organisation transforms inputs into goods and services.

It plays an important role in manufacturing companies, hospitals, restaurants, banks, transportation services, and technology businesses.

Effective operations management helps organisations improve productivity, maintain quality, reduce costs, and satisfy customers.

## 1. Meaning of Operations Management

Operations management involves managing the resources and activities required to produce goods or deliver services.

Its major responsibilities include:

- Planning production activities.
- Selecting production methods.
- Managing equipment and facilities.
- Allocating labour and materials.
- Monitoring productivity.
- Maintaining quality standards.
- Improving operational processes.

For example, a car manufacturer must coordinate raw materials, machinery, employees, assembly processes, and quality inspections to produce vehicles efficiently.

## 2. The Input-Transformation-Output Model

The input-transformation-output model explains how organisations convert resources into finished products or services.

### Inputs

Inputs are the resources used in production.

They include:

- Raw materials.
- Labour.
- Capital and machinery.
- Energy.
- Information.
- Technology.

### Transformation Process

The transformation process converts inputs into outputs.

Examples include:

- Manufacturing: Converting raw materials into finished products.
- Healthcare: Using medical resources and professional knowledge to provide treatment.
- Education: Combining teachers, learning materials, and technology to deliver instruction.

### Outputs

Outputs are the final goods or services produced by the organisation.

**Example: Bakery**

| Stage | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Inputs | Flour, sugar, employees, ovens |
| Transformation | Mixing, baking, decorating |
| Outputs | Bread, cakes, pastries |

## 3. Goods and Services Operations

Operations differ depending on whether an organisation produces physical goods or provides services.

| Feature | Goods | Services |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nature | Usually tangible | Usually intangible |
| Storage | Often possible | Generally cannot be stored as finished services |
| Production and consumption | May occur separately | Often occur simultaneously |
| Customer involvement | May be limited | Often significant |
| Quality evaluation | Can use physical specifications | Often includes customer experience |

Many organisations provide a combination of goods and services.

For example, a restaurant provides food as a physical product and dining experience as a service.

## 4. Types of Production Processes

### Job Production

Job production involves producing an individual product or completing a specific task according to customer requirements.

Characteristics:

- High customisation.
- Low production volume.
- Skilled labour requirements.
- Higher cost per unit.

Examples include custom furniture, specialised machinery, and individually designed buildings.

### Batch Production

Batch production involves manufacturing products in groups or batches.

Characteristics:

- Products are made in specified quantities.
- Equipment can be adjusted between batches.
- Greater flexibility than continuous production.
- Suitable for moderate production volumes.

Examples include bakery products, clothing collections, and pharmaceutical batches.

### Mass Production

Mass production involves manufacturing large quantities of standardised products.

Characteristics:

- High production volume.
- Standardised products.
- Repetitive activities.
- Use of assembly lines and specialised equipment.

Examples include automobiles, packaged food, and household appliances.

### Continuous Production

Continuous production operates continuously or for extended periods with a highly standardised process.

Examples include petroleum refining, chemical processing, and electricity generation.

## 5. Facility Layout

Facility layout refers to the physical arrangement of equipment, departments, workstations, and other resources within an organisation.

A suitable layout reduces unnecessary movement, saves time, and improves workflow.

### Product Layout

Machines and workstations are arranged according to the sequence of production.

Example: An automobile assembly line.

Advantages:
- Smooth workflow.
- High production efficiency.
- Lower material-handling time.

Limitations:
- Limited flexibility.
- A breakdown at one stage may interrupt the entire line.
- High initial investment may be required.

### Process Layout

Similar machines or activities are grouped together.

Example: A hospital arranging departments into radiology, surgery, and laboratory sections.

Advantages:
- Greater flexibility.
- Suitable for different types of work.
- Equipment can be shared across different tasks.

Limitations:
- More complex movement of materials.
- Longer processing times may occur.
- Scheduling can be difficult.

### Fixed-Position Layout

The product remains in one location while workers, equipment, and materials move to it.

Examples include shipbuilding, aircraft construction, and large infrastructure projects.

### Cellular Layout

Machines and workstations are organised into cells designed to complete related groups of tasks.

It combines aspects of product and process layouts and can improve workflow for related products.

## 6. Production Planning and Control

Production planning determines what should be produced, how much should be produced, and which resources are required.

Production control ensures that actual activities follow the production plan.

Major activities include:

- Routing: Determining the sequence of operations.
- Scheduling: Establishing when activities should occur.
- Dispatching: Authorising the start of production activities.
- Follow-up: Monitoring progress and identifying delays.
- Inspection: Checking whether output meets required standards.

Effective production planning reduces delays, improves resource utilisation, and helps meet customer demand.

## 7. Capacity Planning

Capacity refers to the maximum output that an organisation can produce under specified conditions during a given period.

Capacity planning determines whether available resources are sufficient to meet expected demand.

### Design Capacity

Design capacity is the theoretical maximum output that a facility is designed to produce.

### Effective Capacity

Effective capacity is the realistic output possible after accounting for planned maintenance, scheduling restrictions, product mix, and other operational limitations.

### Actual Output

Actual output is the quantity genuinely produced during a particular period.

### Capacity Utilisation

Capacity Utilisation = (Actual Output / Design Capacity) × 100

**Example:**

A factory has a design capacity of 10,000 units per month and produces 8,000 units.

Capacity Utilisation = (8,000 / 10,000) × 100

Capacity Utilisation = 80%

High capacity utilisation can improve resource efficiency, but operating at maximum capacity continuously may reduce flexibility and increase breakdown risks.

## 8. Productivity and Efficiency

Productivity measures the relationship between output and the resources used to produce it.

Productivity = Output / Input

**Example:**

A factory produces 600 units using 20 labour hours.

Labour Productivity = 600 / 20 = 30 units per labour hour.

Efficiency concerns how economically resources are used to achieve an output.

Effectiveness concerns whether the intended objectives are achieved.

An organisation can be efficient in using resources but ineffective if it produces goods that customers do not want.

## 9. Technology in Operations Management

Technology helps organisations improve operational speed, accuracy, and coordination.

Important applications include:

- Automation: Machines perform tasks with reduced human intervention.
- Robotics: Programmable machines perform physical operations.
- Computer-Aided Design: Software supports product design.
- Computer-Aided Manufacturing: Computer systems control manufacturing activities.
- Enterprise Resource Planning: Integrates information across departments.
- Artificial Intelligence: Supports forecasting, scheduling, inspection, and process optimisation.
- Internet of Things: Connected devices collect and exchange operational information.

Technology can increase productivity but may require significant investment, employee training, and cybersecurity measures.

## 10. Location Decisions

Facility location refers to selecting the geographical position of a factory, warehouse, office, or service centre.

Important factors include:

- Availability of raw materials.
- Distance from customers.
- Labour availability and cost.
- Transportation infrastructure.
- Electricity and water supply.
- Government policies and taxation.
- Land and construction costs.
- Environmental regulations.
- Access to suppliers.

For example, a food-processing business may prefer a location close to agricultural suppliers to reduce transportation costs and preserve freshness.

## 11. Operational Performance Objectives

Operations managers commonly focus on five performance objectives:

| Objective | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Quality | Producing goods or services that meet requirements |
| Speed | Completing operations quickly |
| Dependability | Delivering as promised |
| Flexibility | Adapting to changing requirements |
| Cost | Producing economically |

These objectives can influence one another.

For example, increasing flexibility may require additional resources, while improving quality may reduce the cost of defects and rework.

## Common Exam Traps

- Operations management applies to both manufacturing and service organisations.
- Inputs are transformed into outputs through an operational process.
- Job production is highly customised, while mass production focuses on standardised products.
- Product layout follows the production sequence; process layout groups similar activities.
- Design capacity is theoretical, whereas effective capacity accounts for practical operating limitations.
- Productivity measures output relative to input; it is not identical to total production.
- High capacity utilisation does not automatically mean an organisation is operating optimally.

## Quiz

1. Which production method is most suitable for manufacturing a unique product according to an individual customer's specifications?

- [ ] Mass production
- [x] Job production
- [ ] Continuous production
- [ ] Standardised assembly production

> Job production is designed for individual or customised products and generally involves lower production volumes.

2. A factory produces 7,500 units against a design capacity of 10,000 units. What is its capacity utilisation?

- [ ] 65%
- [ ] 70%
- [x] 75%
- [ ] 85%

> Capacity utilisation = (Actual Output / Design Capacity) × 100 = (7,500 / 10,000) × 100 = 75%.

3. Which facility layout keeps the product in one location while workers and equipment move to it?

- [ ] Product layout
- [ ] Process layout
- [ ] Cellular layout
- [x] Fixed-position layout

> Fixed-position layout is appropriate for large products that are difficult or impractical to move, such as ships and aircraft.
