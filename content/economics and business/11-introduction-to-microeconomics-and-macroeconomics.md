Economics is broadly divided into two major branches: microeconomics and macroeconomics. Both examine how economic decisions are made, but they focus on different levels of economic activity.

Microeconomics studies individual consumers, businesses and markets, whereas macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole.

Understanding both branches helps explain how individual decisions influence markets and how economy-wide conditions affect households and businesses.

## 1. Meaning of Microeconomics

**Microeconomics** is the branch of economics that studies the behaviour of individual economic units and their interactions in particular markets.

It examines how consumers and producers make decisions regarding consumption, production, pricing and resource allocation.

### Main Areas of Microeconomics

- Consumer behaviour and utility.
- Demand and supply.
- Price determination.
- Production and costs.
- Market structures.
- Factor pricing.
- Business decision-making.
- Resource allocation.

### Examples

- Why does the price of coffee increase?
- How does a consumer respond to a rise in fuel prices?
- Why does a company reduce production?
- How does competition influence product prices?
- How does a business decide the quantity to produce?

## 2. Meaning of Macroeconomics

**Macroeconomics** is the branch of economics that studies the economy as a whole and examines aggregate economic variables.

It focuses on the overall performance, structure and behaviour of an economy.

### Main Areas of Macroeconomics

- National income.
- Gross Domestic Product.
- Economic growth.
- Inflation and deflation.
- Unemployment.
- Monetary policy.
- Fiscal policy.
- International trade.
- Balance of payments.
- Business cycles.

### Examples

- Why is the overall price level increasing?
- What determines a country's economic growth?
- Why does unemployment rise during a recession?
- How do central banks influence interest rates?
- How does government expenditure affect national income?

## 3. Microeconomics and Macroeconomics: Comparison

| Feature | Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Focus | Individual economic units | Entire economy |
| Main units | Consumers, firms and markets | National economy |
| Variables | Individual prices and output | Inflation, GDP and unemployment |
| Objective | Understand individual decisions and resource allocation | Understand aggregate economic performance |
| Examples | Demand for smartphones | National economic growth |

## 4. Economic Agents and Their Roles

Economic agents participate in the production, exchange and consumption of goods and services.

### Households

Households generally:

- Consume goods and services.
- Supply labour.
- Receive income.
- Save and invest.
- Pay taxes.

### Firms

Firms generally:

- Produce goods and services.
- Employ workers.
- Invest capital.
- Generate revenue.
- Make production and pricing decisions.

### Government

Government generally:

- Collects taxes.
- Provides public services.
- Regulates economic activity.
- Undertakes public investment.
- Implements economic policies.

### Financial Sector

Banks and other financial institutions:

- Accept deposits.
- Provide loans.
- Facilitate payments.
- Support investment.
- Help transfer funds between savers and borrowers.

### Foreign Sector

The foreign sector represents economic interactions with other countries through:

- Imports.
- Exports.
- Foreign investment.
- International borrowing and lending.
- Cross-border financial transactions.

## 5. Circular Flow of Income

The circular flow of income illustrates how money, goods, services and productive resources move between economic participants.

### Two-Sector Model

The simplest circular flow model contains:

- Households.
- Firms.

Households provide factors of production to firms.

Firms pay households through wages, rent, interest and profit.

Households use their income to purchase goods and services produced by firms.

This creates two interconnected flows.

**Real Flow:**
- Households provide productive resources.
- Firms provide goods and services.

**Money Flow:**
- Firms make factor payments.
- Households make consumption expenditure.

### Four-Sector Model

A more developed model includes:

- Households.
- Firms.
- Government.
- Foreign sector.

It also considers saving, taxation, investment, government expenditure, exports and imports.

## 6. Microeconomic Foundations of Macroeconomics

Individual economic decisions can influence aggregate economic outcomes.

Examples:

- Household consumption contributes to aggregate demand.
- Business investment contributes to capital formation.
- Individual employment decisions influence labour markets.
- Saving decisions influence the availability of financial resources.
- Business production contributes to national output.

However, aggregate outcomes cannot always be predicted by simply adding individual behaviour because interactions and economy-wide effects also matter.

## 7. Important Macroeconomic Objectives

Governments and policymakers generally pursue several economic objectives.

### Economic Growth

Increasing the production capacity and output of an economy over time.

### Price Stability

Maintaining relatively stable prices and controlling excessive inflation or deflation.

### Full Employment

Providing opportunities for people willing and able to work, while recognising that some unemployment may remain in a dynamic economy.

### External Stability

Maintaining sustainable international financial and trade relationships.

### Equitable Distribution of Income

Reducing excessive economic inequality and improving access to opportunities.

## 8. Importance of Microeconomics for Business

Microeconomics helps businesses:

- Estimate consumer demand.
- Determine prices.
- Understand production costs.
- Evaluate competitors.
- Select production quantities.
- Allocate resources efficiently.
- Analyse market opportunities.

## 9. Importance of Macroeconomics for Business

Macroeconomic conditions influence business performance.

For example:

- Inflation can increase production costs.
- Higher interest rates can make borrowing expensive.
- Economic growth can increase consumer demand.
- Unemployment can influence household spending.
- Exchange-rate changes can affect import and export costs.

Businesses use macroeconomic information when planning investments, budgets and long-term strategies.

## 10. Limitations of Economic Analysis

Economic models simplify complex real-world situations.

Some limitations include:

- Incomplete information.
- Changing consumer preferences.
- Unpredictable external events.
- Differences between economic assumptions and actual behaviour.
- Difficulties in measuring certain economic variables.
- Interactions between individual and aggregate economic decisions.

Economic models are useful analytical tools, but their conclusions depend on their assumptions and the quality of available information.

## Common Exam Traps

- Microeconomics studies individual consumers, firms and markets.
- Macroeconomics studies aggregate economic activity.
- The price of an individual product is generally a microeconomic variable.
- The overall inflation rate is a macroeconomic variable.
- GDP measures aggregate economic output.
- Households supply factors of production and receive factor income.
- Firms produce goods and services and make factor payments.
- The circular flow of income includes both real flows and money flows.
- Saving, taxation and imports are leakages from the simple circular flow.
- Investment, government expenditure and exports are injections into the circular flow.
- Microeconomics and macroeconomics are interconnected rather than completely independent.
