title: Business Communication and Organisational Behaviour
level: intermediate
---

Business communication is the process of exchanging information, ideas, instructions, and feedback within and outside an organisation.

Organisational behaviour is the study of how individuals and groups behave within organisations and how organisational structures and cultures influence their actions.

Both areas are important for effective teamwork, employee relationships, decision-making, and organisational performance.

## 1. Meaning of Business Communication

Business communication refers to the exchange of information between individuals, teams, departments, organisations, customers, suppliers, and other stakeholders for business purposes.

It may involve:

- Giving instructions.
- Sharing information.
- Making decisions.
- Coordinating activities.
- Negotiating agreements.
- Resolving problems.
- Providing feedback.
- Maintaining business relationships.

Effective communication ensures that information is understood and used appropriately.

## 2. Importance of Business Communication

Business communication supports organisational performance in several ways.

### A. Coordination

It helps departments and employees work towards shared objectives.

### B. Decision-Making

Managers require accurate information to evaluate alternatives and make decisions.

### C. Employee Motivation

Clear communication and constructive feedback can improve employee confidence and engagement.

### D. Customer Relationships

Businesses use communication to understand customer needs, explain products, and resolve complaints.

### E. Conflict Resolution

Open communication helps identify misunderstandings and disagreements before they become more serious.

### F. Organisational Control

Instructions, policies, reports, and performance information help maintain operational standards.

## 3. Communication Process

Communication generally involves the following elements.

### Sender

The sender is the person or organisation that initiates the communication.

### Encoding

Encoding is the process of converting an idea into words, symbols, or other forms of information.

### Message

The message is the information being communicated.

### Channel

The channel is the medium through which the message is transmitted.

Examples include email, telephone, meetings, and written reports.

### Receiver

The receiver is the person or group for whom the message is intended.

### Decoding

Decoding is the process through which the receiver interprets the message.

### Feedback

Feedback is the receiver's response, indicating whether the message has been understood.

### Noise

Noise refers to anything that interferes with effective communication.

Examples include:

- Background sounds.
- Poor internet connectivity.
- Language differences.
- Unclear instructions.
- Distractions.
- Incorrect information.

## 4. Types of Business Communication

### A. Verbal Communication

Verbal communication uses spoken words.

Examples include:

- Meetings.
- Telephone conversations.
- Interviews.
- Presentations.
- Discussions.

Advantages:

- Immediate interaction.
- Opportunity for clarification.
- Faster exchange of information.
- Ability to observe tone and responses.

Disadvantages:

- May lack a permanent written record.
- Information can be misunderstood.
- Difficult to communicate complex details without supporting material.

### B. Written Communication

Written communication uses written words to transmit information.

Examples include:

- Emails.
- Reports.
- Memos.
- Letters.
- Proposals.
- Business notices.

Advantages:

- Provides a permanent record.
- Suitable for detailed information.
- Supports formal communication.
- Can be reviewed later.

Disadvantages:

- May require more time.
- Feedback may be delayed.
- Poor writing can create misunderstandings.

### C. Non-Verbal Communication

Non-verbal communication involves conveying information without relying entirely on words.

Examples include:

- Facial expressions.
- Gestures.
- Posture.
- Eye contact.
- Body language.
- Tone of voice.

Non-verbal signals can support or contradict spoken messages.

### D. Visual Communication

Visual communication uses visual elements to present information.

Examples include:

- Charts.
- Graphs.
- Diagrams.
- Infographics.
- Presentations.
- Process maps.

It is particularly useful for explaining complex information and patterns.

## 5. Direction of Communication

Communication in an organisation may flow in different directions.

### A. Downward Communication

Downward communication flows from higher organisational levels to lower levels.

Examples:

- Management instructions.
- Policies.
- Performance expectations.
- Work schedules.

### B. Upward Communication

Upward communication flows from lower organisational levels to higher levels.

Examples:

- Employee feedback.
- Progress reports.
- Suggestions.
- Complaints.
- Requests for support.

### C. Horizontal Communication

Horizontal communication occurs between individuals or departments at similar organisational levels.

Examples:

- Coordination between marketing and finance.
- Discussions between department managers.
- Collaboration between project teams.

### D. Diagonal Communication

Diagonal communication occurs between individuals in different departments and at different organisational levels.

It can support faster coordination across complex organisations.

## 6. Formal and Informal Communication

### Formal Communication

Formal communication follows established organisational channels and procedures.

Examples include:

- Official reports.
- Policy documents.
- Management instructions.
- Formal meetings.
- Approved business emails.

Advantages:

- Clear organisational authority.
- Better documentation.
- Greater accountability.
- More consistent information.

Disadvantages:

- May be slower.
- Can become bureaucratic.
- May limit spontaneous interaction.

### Informal Communication

Informal communication occurs through unofficial personal and social interactions.

Examples include:

- Casual conversations.
- Informal team discussions.
- Social interactions.
- Unofficial employee networks.

It can support relationships and quick information sharing, but may also spread rumours or inaccurate information.

## 7. Barriers to Effective Communication

Communication barriers prevent information from being transmitted or understood correctly.

### A. Language Barriers

Differences in language, vocabulary, or technical terminology can create confusion.

### B. Psychological Barriers

Stress, fear, anger, and lack of confidence may affect communication.

### C. Physical Barriers

Distance, noise, poor lighting, and workplace conditions may interfere with communication.

### D. Organisational Barriers

Excessive hierarchy, complex procedures, and poor communication systems can delay information.

### E. Cultural Barriers

Different cultural values, communication styles, and social expectations may lead to misunderstandings.

### F. Technological Barriers

Poor connectivity, incompatible software, and technical failures may interrupt communication.

## 8. Improving Business Communication

Businesses can improve communication by:

- Using clear and simple language.
- Selecting suitable communication channels.
- Encouraging feedback.
- Listening actively.
- Avoiding unnecessary technical terms.
- Providing accurate information.
- Respecting cultural differences.
- Maintaining appropriate documentation.
- Using reliable communication systems.

## 9. Meaning of Organisational Behaviour

Organisational behaviour (OB) is the systematic study of how individuals and groups behave within organisations.

It examines how behaviour is influenced by:

- Personality.
- Motivation.
- Attitudes.
- Leadership.
- Group relationships.
- Organisational culture.
- Structure.
- Workplace environment.

The purpose is to understand and improve organisational effectiveness and employee well-being.

## 10. Levels of Organisational Behaviour

Organisational behaviour is commonly studied at three levels.

### A. Individual Level

This level examines the behaviour of individual employees.

Topics include:

- Personality.
- Perception.
- Attitudes.
- Motivation.
- Learning.
- Job satisfaction.

### B. Group Level

This level examines how people behave when working together.

Topics include:

- Teamwork.
- Leadership.
- Group decision-making.
- Communication.
- Conflict.
- Group dynamics.

### C. Organisational Level

This level examines the organisation as a whole.

Topics include:

- Organisational culture.
- Structure.
- Policies.
- Change management.
- Organisational development.

## 11. Personality and Individual Differences

Personality refers to relatively consistent patterns of thinking, feeling, and behaving.

Employees may differ in:

- Communication preferences.
- Emotional responses.
- Decision-making styles.
- Social behaviour.
- Approach to risk.
- Work preferences.

Understanding individual differences helps managers assign responsibilities, manage teams, and improve workplace relationships.

## 12. Attitudes and Job Satisfaction

An attitude is an individual's evaluation or feeling towards a person, object, event, or situation.

Job satisfaction refers to the degree of positive feeling an employee has towards their job.

Factors influencing job satisfaction include:

- Salary.
- Working conditions.
- Leadership quality.
- Recognition.
- Career opportunities.
- Workload.
- Relationships with colleagues.
- Job security.

Higher job satisfaction may support employee commitment and retention, although it does not automatically guarantee higher productivity.

## 13. Group Dynamics and Teamwork

Group dynamics refers to the interactions and relationships that influence the behaviour of individuals within a group.

Effective teamwork requires:

- Shared objectives.
- Clear responsibilities.
- Trust.
- Communication.
- Cooperation.
- Mutual respect.
- Conflict management.

Teams can combine different skills and perspectives to solve complex problems.

However, poorly managed groups may experience disagreements, unequal participation, and coordination difficulties.

## 14. Stages of Team Development

Bruce Tuckman's model identifies five stages of team development.

### Forming

Members meet, understand the task, and become familiar with one another.

### Storming

Disagreements may emerge regarding roles, responsibilities, and approaches.

### Norming

Members establish shared expectations, relationships, and working procedures.

### Performing

The team works effectively towards its objectives.

### Adjourning

The team completes its task and prepares to separate or move to another assignment.

These stages describe a common pattern, but real teams may move between stages or experience them differently.

## 15. Organisational Culture

Organisational culture refers to the shared values, beliefs, assumptions, and behavioural expectations within an organisation.

It influences:

- How employees communicate.
- How decisions are made.
- How conflicts are handled.
- How employees treat customers.
- How innovation is encouraged.
- How the organisation responds to change.

A positive organisational culture may support trust, cooperation, ethical behaviour, and employee engagement.

## 16. Organisational Conflict

Organisational conflict occurs when individuals or groups have incompatible interests, goals, values, or expectations.

Common causes include:

- Limited resources.
- Poor communication.
- Role ambiguity.
- Differences in opinions.
- Competition between departments.
- Conflicting objectives.

### Conflict Management Approaches

**Avoiding**

The individual withdraws from or postpones dealing with the conflict.

**Accommodating**

One party gives priority to the other party's concerns.

**Competing**

One party attempts to achieve its preferred outcome with limited compromise.

**Compromising**

Both parties make concessions to reach an acceptable solution.

**Collaborating**

The parties work together to find a solution that addresses their important interests.

The appropriate approach depends on the importance of the issue, available time, and relationship between the parties.

## Common Exam Traps

- The sender initiates communication, while the receiver interprets the message.
- Encoding converts an idea into a message, while decoding interprets it.
- Feedback helps determine whether communication has been understood.
- Downward communication moves from higher to lower organisational levels.
- Upward communication moves from employees towards higher management.
- Horizontal communication occurs between similar organisational levels.
- Formal communication follows official channels, while informal communication uses unofficial networks.
- Organisational behaviour studies individuals, groups, and organisations.
- Tuckman's model includes forming, storming, norming, performing, and adjourning.
- Organisational culture refers to shared values and behavioural expectations.
- Collaboration aims to address the important interests of all parties involved.
