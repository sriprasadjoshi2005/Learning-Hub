title: Revenue, Profit and Break-Even Analysis
level: intermediate
---

Revenue, cost and profit are fundamental concepts in business economics. They help businesses evaluate financial performance, determine selling prices, plan production and assess whether an activity is financially sustainable.

Break-even analysis is particularly useful because it identifies the level of sales at which a business covers all its costs without making a profit or loss.

## 1. Meaning of Revenue

**Revenue** refers to the income a business receives from selling goods or providing services.

Revenue is generally generated through the sale of products, although businesses may also earn income from other operating activities.

### Total Revenue

Total Revenue (TR) is the total amount received from selling a given quantity of products.

TR = Price x Quantity Sold

### Example

A business sells 200 units of a product at Rs. 500 per unit.

TR = 500 x 200

TR = Rs. 1,00,000

## 2. Average Revenue

Average Revenue (AR) measures revenue earned per unit sold.

AR = Total Revenue / Quantity Sold

For a business selling all units at the same price:

AR = Price

### Example

A business earns Rs. 60,000 by selling 120 units.

AR = 60,000 / 120

AR = Rs. 500 per unit.

## 3. Marginal Revenue

Marginal Revenue (MR) is the additional revenue generated by selling one more unit of output.

MR = Change in Total Revenue / Change in Quantity Sold

### Example

A company increases sales from 100 units to 110 units.

- Initial revenue = Rs. 50,000.
- New revenue = Rs. 55,000.

Change in revenue = Rs. 5,000.

Change in quantity = 10 units.

MR = 5,000 / 10

MR = Rs. 500 per additional unit.

## 4. Types of Revenue

Businesses may receive revenue from different activities.

### Operating Revenue

Revenue generated through the primary business activity.

Examples:
- Sales revenue from products.
- Fees earned by a consulting firm.
- Service charges collected by a transport company.

### Non-Operating Revenue

Income generated from activities outside the main operations of a business.

Examples may include:
- Interest income.
- Rental income from unused property.
- Certain investment income.

The classification depends on the nature of the business and its accounting policies.

## 5. Meaning of Profit

**Profit** is the financial surplus remaining after a business deducts relevant costs and expenses from its revenue.

Profit = Revenue - Cost

A positive result represents profit, while a negative result represents a loss.

### Gross Profit

Gross Profit represents revenue from sales minus the cost of goods sold.

Gross Profit = Net Sales - Cost of Goods Sold

### Operating Profit

Operating Profit measures profit from the main business operations after deducting operating expenses.

A simplified formula is:

Operating Profit = Gross Profit - Operating Expenses

### Net Profit

Net Profit is the final profit remaining after all relevant expenses, interest and taxes have been accounted for.

Net Profit = Total Income - Total Expenses

The exact presentation depends on the accounting framework and financial statement structure.

## 6. Accounting Profit and Economic Profit

### Accounting Profit

Accounting profit is calculated by deducting recorded accounting expenses from revenue.

Accounting Profit = Total Revenue - Explicit Costs

### Economic Profit

Economic profit also considers implicit costs and opportunity costs.

Economic Profit = Total Revenue - Explicit Costs - Implicit Costs

### Example

A business earns Rs. 10 lakh in annual revenue.

- Explicit costs = Rs. 6 lakh.
- Implicit costs = Rs. 2 lakh.

Accounting Profit = 10 - 6 = Rs. 4 lakh.

Economic Profit = 10 - 6 - 2 = Rs. 2 lakh.

Economic profit provides a broader measure of whether a business is generating returns above the opportunity cost of its resources.

## 7. Profit Maximisation

Profit maximisation refers to selecting the level of production or business activity that produces the highest possible profit, subject to relevant constraints.

Profit = Total Revenue - Total Cost

In standard economic theory, an interior profit-maximising output generally satisfies:

MR = MC

Where:

- MR = Marginal Revenue.
- MC = Marginal Cost.

For a maximum rather than a minimum, marginal cost should generally be rising relative to marginal revenue at the relevant intersection.

In practice, businesses may also pursue objectives such as growth, market share, customer satisfaction and long-term sustainability.

## 8. Meaning of Break-Even Analysis

**Break-even analysis** is a financial planning technique used to determine the sales quantity or revenue at which total revenue equals total cost.

At the break-even point:

Total Revenue = Total Cost

Profit = Zero

The business has covered all fixed and variable costs but has not generated a profit.

## 9. Fixed and Variable Costs

### Fixed Costs

Fixed costs remain unchanged with output over the relevant short-run range.

Examples:
- Monthly rent.
- Certain insurance payments.
- Fixed administrative expenses.

### Variable Costs

Variable costs change with the quantity produced or sold.

Examples:
- Raw materials.
- Packaging.
- Production-related labour.
- Sales commissions.

### Contribution

Contribution is the amount remaining from sales revenue after deducting variable costs.

Contribution = Sales Revenue - Total Variable Cost

Contribution per Unit = Selling Price per Unit - Variable Cost per Unit

Contribution first covers fixed costs. Once fixed costs are covered, additional contribution increases profit.

## 10. Break-Even Point Formula

Break-Even Point in Units:

BEP = Fixed Costs / Contribution per Unit

Contribution per Unit = Selling Price per Unit - Variable Cost per Unit

### Example

A business has:

- Fixed Costs = Rs. 50,000.
- Selling Price per Unit = Rs. 500.
- Variable Cost per Unit = Rs. 300.

Contribution per Unit = 500 - 300 = Rs. 200.

BEP = 50,000 / 200

BEP = 250 units.

The business must sell 250 units to break even.

## 11. Break-Even Sales Revenue

Break-Even Sales Revenue can be calculated using the contribution margin ratio.

Contribution Margin Ratio = Contribution / Sales Revenue

Break-Even Sales Revenue = Fixed Costs / Contribution Margin Ratio

For a single product:

Contribution Margin Ratio = Contribution per Unit / Selling Price per Unit

### Example

Using the previous business:

Contribution Margin Ratio = 200 / 500 = 0.40

Break-Even Sales Revenue = 50,000 / 0.40

Break-Even Sales Revenue = Rs. 1,25,000.

## 12. Margin of Safety

Margin of Safety measures how much actual or planned sales exceed break-even sales.

Margin of Safety = Actual Sales - Break-Even Sales

Margin of Safety Percentage = (Margin of Safety / Actual Sales) x 100

### Example

A business has:

- Actual sales = 400 units.
- Break-even sales = 250 units.

Margin of Safety = 400 - 250

Margin of Safety = 150 units.

Margin of Safety Percentage = (150 / 400) x 100

Margin of Safety Percentage = 37.5%.

A higher margin of safety generally indicates a greater buffer against declining sales.

## 13. Target Profit Analysis

Businesses can also calculate the sales quantity required to achieve a desired profit.

Required Sales Units = (Fixed Costs + Target Profit) / Contribution per Unit

### Example

A business has:

- Fixed Costs = Rs. 60,000.
- Target Profit = Rs. 40,000.
- Contribution per Unit = Rs. 200.

Required Sales Units = (60,000 + 40,000) / 200

Required Sales Units = 500 units.

The business must sell 500 units to achieve its target profit.

## 14. Assumptions of Break-Even Analysis

Traditional break-even analysis generally assumes:

- Selling price per unit remains constant.
- Variable cost per unit remains constant.
- Total fixed costs remain constant within the relevant output range.
- Production and sales quantities are equal or inventory changes are ignored.
- Product mix remains constant in multi-product businesses.
- Costs and revenues can be represented in a sufficiently linear manner.

These assumptions simplify real business conditions.

## 15. Limitations of Break-Even Analysis

Break-even analysis has several limitations:

- Selling prices may change with demand.
- Input costs may fluctuate.
- Fixed costs may increase when production expands.
- Sales forecasts may be inaccurate.
- Businesses may sell multiple products with different contribution margins.
- Unexpected economic conditions may affect profitability.

Therefore, break-even analysis should be used alongside broader financial and market analysis.

## 16. Importance for Business Decision-Making

Businesses use revenue, profit and break-even analysis to:

- Set selling prices.
- Estimate minimum sales requirements.
- Evaluate new products.
- Plan production levels.
- Compare investment alternatives.
- Understand financial risk.
- Establish sales targets.
- Monitor business performance.

## Common Exam Traps

- Revenue is the income generated from sales and other business activities.
- Profit is the surplus remaining after relevant costs are deducted.
- Average revenue equals total revenue divided by quantity sold.
- Marginal revenue measures additional revenue from additional sales.
- Accounting profit generally excludes implicit costs.
- Economic profit includes explicit and implicit costs.
- Break-even occurs when total revenue equals total cost.
- Contribution is sales revenue minus variable costs.
- Break-even units equal fixed costs divided by contribution per unit.
- A business earns a profit above break-even when additional contribution exceeds the relevant fixed costs already covered.
- Margin of safety measures the difference between actual sales and break-even sales.
- A higher margin of safety generally indicates lower exposure to a fall in sales.
