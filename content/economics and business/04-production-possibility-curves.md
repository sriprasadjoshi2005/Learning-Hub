A Production Possibility Curve (PPC), also known as a Production Possibility Frontier (PPF), is an economic model that illustrates the maximum possible combinations of two goods that an economy can produce using its available resources and technology efficiently.

The model demonstrates scarcity, choice, opportunity cost, productive efficiency and economic growth.

## 1. Meaning of Production Possibility Curve

A production possibility curve represents the different combinations of two goods that can be produced when:

- Available resources are fixed.
- Technology remains unchanged.
- Resources are fully and efficiently utilised.
- Production is considered over a given period.

Consider an economy that produces only two goods:

- Food.
- Machinery.

It must decide how to distribute its limited resources between these two products.

Producing more machinery may require transferring resources away from food production.

This creates a trade-off between the two goods.

## 2. Production Possibility Schedule

A production possibility schedule presents different combinations of two goods that an economy can produce.

| Combination | Food (units) | Machinery (units) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | 0 | 100 |
| B | 20 | 90 |
| C | 40 | 75 |
| D | 60 | 55 |
| E | 80 | 30 |
| F | 100 | 0 |

The schedule shows that increasing food production requires reducing machinery production.

This illustrates the opportunity cost of production.

## 3. Shape of the Production Possibility Curve

A typical production possibility curve is bowed outward from the origin.

This shape occurs because of increasing opportunity cost.

Resources are not equally suitable for producing every good.

For example:

- Some workers and machines are highly suitable for food production.
- Others are more suitable for machinery production.
- As more resources are transferred towards machinery, increasingly unsuitable resources must also be transferred.

Consequently, each additional unit of machinery may require sacrificing progressively larger quantities of food.

## 4. Opportunity Cost Using PPC

Opportunity cost measures the amount of one good sacrificed to produce additional quantities of another good.

### Example

An economy moves from combination B to combination C.

At B:
- Food = 20 units.
- Machinery = 90 units.

At C:
- Food = 40 units.
- Machinery = 75 units.

Additional food production = 40 - 20 = 20 units.

Machinery sacrificed = 90 - 75 = 15 units.

Opportunity cost of producing 20 additional units of food = 15 units of machinery.

Opportunity cost per additional unit of food:

15 / 20 = 0.75 units of machinery.

## 5. Points on, Inside and Outside the PPC

The position of a production combination provides information about resource utilisation.

### Points on the Curve

Points on the PPC represent productive efficiency.

The economy is using available resources efficiently under the model's assumptions.

### Points Inside the Curve

Points inside the PPC represent productive inefficiency or underutilisation of resources.

Possible causes include:

- Unemployment.
- Idle machinery.
- Poor management.
- Underutilised production capacity.

### Points Outside the Curve

Points outside the PPC represent production combinations that are currently unattainable using available resources and technology.

Such combinations may become attainable through economic growth.

## 6. Shifts in the Production Possibility Curve

A PPC shifts when an economy's productive capacity changes.

### Outward Shift

An outward shift represents an increase in productive capacity.

Possible causes:

- Technological advancement.
- Increase in labour supply.
- Better education and training.
- Capital accumulation.
- Discovery of natural resources.
- Improved infrastructure.

### Inward Shift

An inward shift represents a reduction in productive capacity.

Possible causes:

- Natural disasters.
- War and destruction.
- Loss of productive resources.
- Severe deterioration of infrastructure.
- Long-term decline in the workforce.

## 7. Economic Growth and PPC

Economic growth occurs when an economy increases its capacity to produce goods and services over time.

An outward shift of the PPC illustrates this increase in productive potential.

For example, investments in education, infrastructure and technology can enable an economy to produce greater quantities of both food and machinery.

## 8. Constant and Increasing Opportunity Cost

### Constant Opportunity Cost

If resources are equally suitable for producing two goods, opportunity cost may remain constant.

The PPC becomes a straight line.

### Increasing Opportunity Cost

If resources differ in suitability, producing more of one good requires sacrificing progressively greater quantities of the other.

The PPC becomes bowed outward.

## 9. Limitations of the PPC Model

Although useful, the PPC simplifies economic reality.

Its traditional assumptions include:

- Only two goods are considered.
- Available resources are fixed.
- Technology is constant.
- Resources are fully employed for points on the curve.
- The model examines production during a particular period.

Real economies produce many goods and experience continuous changes in resources and technology.

## Common Exam Traps

- A point on the PPC represents productive efficiency.
- A point inside the PPC indicates underutilisation or inefficiency.
- A point outside the PPC is currently unattainable under existing resources and technology.
- An outward shift indicates increased productive capacity.
- An inward shift indicates decreased productive capacity.
- A bowed-out PPC represents increasing opportunity cost.
- A straight-line PPC represents constant opportunity cost.
- Movement along a PPC represents a change in the production combination, not necessarily economic growth.
