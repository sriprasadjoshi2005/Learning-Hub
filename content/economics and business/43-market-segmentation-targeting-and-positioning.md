Market segmentation, targeting, and positioning form a central framework for developing effective marketing strategies.

Businesses rarely serve every customer in exactly the same way. Customers differ in their needs, purchasing power, preferences, lifestyles, and buying behaviour.

The STP framework helps organisations divide markets into meaningful groups, select the customers they want to serve, and establish a distinctive position in customers' minds.

## 1. Meaning of Market Segmentation

Market segmentation is the process of dividing a broad market into smaller groups of customers who share similar characteristics or needs.

Customers within a segment may have similar:

- Demographic characteristics.
- Geographical locations.
- Interests and lifestyles.
- Purchasing behaviour.
- Product preferences.
- Price sensitivity.

**Example:**

A clothing company may divide its market into:

- Children.
- Teenagers.
- Young adults.
- Working professionals.
- Older consumers.

It can then develop suitable products and marketing messages for each group.

## 2. Importance of Market Segmentation

Market segmentation helps businesses understand differences among customers.

Its benefits include:

- Better understanding of customer needs.
- More relevant products.
- Improved marketing communication.
- Efficient allocation of marketing resources.
- Identification of attractive market opportunities.
- Better customer satisfaction.
- Stronger competitive positioning.

Segmentation can help a business avoid spending resources on customers whose needs do not match its products.

## 3. Bases of Market Segmentation

Markets can be segmented using several major approaches.

### Geographic Segmentation

Geographic segmentation divides markets according to geographical characteristics.

Examples include:

- Country.
- State.
- City.
- Region.
- Climate.
- Urban or rural location.
- Population density.

Example:

A company may market winter clothing more heavily in colder regions.

### Demographic Segmentation

Demographic segmentation divides customers according to measurable population characteristics.

Examples include:

- Age.
- Gender.
- Income.
- Education.
- Occupation.
- Family size.
- Family life cycle.

Example:

A financial services company may develop different products for young earners, families, and retirees.

### Psychographic Segmentation

Psychographic segmentation divides consumers according to psychological and lifestyle characteristics.

Examples include:

- Personality.
- Values.
- Interests.
- Attitudes.
- Lifestyle.
- Social class.

Example:

A travel company may target customers interested in adventure tourism and outdoor activities.

### Behavioural Segmentation

Behavioural segmentation groups consumers according to their purchasing and usage behaviour.

Examples include:

- Purchase frequency.
- Brand loyalty.
- Usage rate.
- Benefits sought.
- Purchase occasion.
- Readiness to purchase.

Example:

A software company may offer different plans to occasional users and heavy professional users.

## 4. Requirements for Effective Segmentation

A useful market segment should satisfy several conditions.

### Measurable

The segment's size, purchasing power, and characteristics should be reasonably identifiable.

### Accessible

The business should be able to reach and serve the segment through suitable distribution and communication channels.

### Substantial

The segment should be large or valuable enough to justify serving it.

### Differentiable

The segment should respond differently to marketing activities compared with other segments.

### Actionable

The business should have the resources and capabilities to design effective strategies for the segment.

## 5. Market Targeting

Market targeting is the process of evaluating market segments and selecting one or more segments to serve.

After identifying different segments, a business evaluates their attractiveness and compatibility with organisational objectives.

Important evaluation factors include:

- Segment size.
- Expected growth.
- Profit potential.
- Competitive intensity.
- Customer accessibility.
- Resource requirements.
- Strategic compatibility.

## 6. Targeting Strategies

### Undifferentiated Marketing

Undifferentiated marketing treats the market as a broad group and uses a common marketing approach.

It focuses on shared customer needs rather than segment differences.

Example: Marketing a basic commodity product to a broad population.

Advantages:
- Economies of scale.
- Simpler marketing activities.
- Lower complexity.

Limitations:
- May not satisfy specialised needs.
- Can face strong competition from targeted offerings.

### Differentiated Marketing

Differentiated marketing targets several segments using separate marketing approaches.

Example:

A smartphone company offers different product ranges for budget, mid-range, and premium customers.

Advantages:
- Better satisfaction of different needs.
- Broader market coverage.
- Reduced dependence on one segment.

Limitations:
- Higher marketing and production costs.
- More complex product management.

### Concentrated Marketing

Concentrated marketing focuses on one particular market segment.

Example: A company specialising in professional climbing equipment.

Advantages:
- Strong understanding of a specialised market.
- Efficient use of limited resources.
- Opportunity to develop specialised expertise.

Limitations:
- Dependence on a limited customer group.
- Vulnerability if the segment declines.

### Micromarketing

Micromarketing adapts marketing activities to the needs of very small customer groups or individual customers.

It may include local marketing and individual personalisation.

Examples include customised product recommendations and location-specific promotions.

## 7. Market Positioning

Market positioning is the process of establishing a distinctive and desirable image of a product or brand in the minds of target customers.

It answers the question:

Why should customers choose this product instead of competing alternatives?

Positioning may be based on:

- Price.
- Quality.
- Performance.
- Design.
- Convenience.
- Innovation.
- Customer service.
- Brand personality.
- Specific product benefits.

**Example:**

A laptop brand may position itself around portability and long battery life, while another brand may emphasise gaming performance.

## 8. Positioning Strategies

### Price-Based Positioning

The product is positioned around affordability, premium pricing, or value for money.

### Quality-Based Positioning

The brand emphasises durability, reliability, craftsmanship, or superior materials.

### Benefit-Based Positioning

The product is positioned according to a particular customer benefit.

Example: A toothpaste brand focusing on cavity protection.

### Competitor-Based Positioning

The business differentiates its offering by comparing its characteristics with those of competitors.

### Usage-Based Positioning

The product is associated with a particular situation or use.

Example: A beverage marketed as a refreshing drink during physical activity.

### User-Based Positioning

The product is associated with a particular customer group or lifestyle.

## 9. Unique Selling Proposition

A Unique Selling Proposition is a distinctive benefit or feature used to differentiate an offering from competing alternatives.

A strong USP should:

- Be relevant to target customers.
- Communicate a clear benefit.
- Be meaningfully different.
- Be credible.
- Be difficult for competitors to imitate quickly.

Example:

A delivery company may differentiate itself through reliable same-day delivery in selected locations.

A USP should communicate genuine value rather than unsupported promotional claims.

## 10. Perceptual Mapping

A perceptual map is a visual tool that represents how consumers perceive competing brands or products across selected dimensions.

For example, a market may be mapped according to:

- Low price to high price.
- Basic features to advanced features.

Each brand is positioned according to customer perceptions rather than necessarily its actual technical specifications.

Perceptual maps help identify:

- Competitive clusters.
- Market gaps.
- Differentiation opportunities.
- Potential repositioning strategies.

## 11. Repositioning

Repositioning involves changing the way a product or brand is perceived by customers.

A business may reposition an offering because of:

- Changing customer preferences.
- New competitors.
- Declining brand relevance.
- Product improvements.
- Changes in market conditions.
- Entry into a new target segment.

Repositioning may involve changes to product features, pricing, packaging, promotion, or distribution.

## 12. STP Marketing Process

The STP framework follows three connected stages.

### Segmentation

Divide the market into groups with similar characteristics or needs.

### Targeting

Evaluate the groups and select the most appropriate customer segments.

### Positioning

Develop a distinctive value proposition and brand image for the selected customers.

**Example: Sports Footwear Business**

Segmentation:
- Casual users.
- Professional athletes.
- Fitness enthusiasts.
- Outdoor sports participants.

Targeting:
- Select fitness enthusiasts and recreational runners.

Positioning:
- Offer comfortable, durable footwear designed for regular exercise.

## 13. Customer Persona

A customer persona is a fictional but research-based representation of a typical target customer.

It may include:

- Age range.
- Occupation.
- Goals.
- Challenges.
- Purchasing motivations.
- Preferred communication channels.
- Product expectations.

Personas help marketing teams understand customer needs and develop more relevant communication.

A persona should be based on research rather than unsupported assumptions.

## 14. Limitations of STP

Although segmentation, targeting, and positioning are useful, they have limitations.

- Market research may be inaccurate.
- Customer preferences can change.
- Segments may overlap.
- Serving many segments can increase costs.
- Competitors may imitate successful positioning.
- Excessive segmentation may reduce economies of scale.
- Customer data may raise privacy concerns.

Businesses should regularly review their segmentation and positioning decisions.

## Common Exam Traps

- Segmentation divides a market into groups; targeting selects which groups to serve.
- Positioning concerns how customers perceive a product or brand.
- Geographic segmentation uses location-related characteristics.
- Demographic segmentation uses population characteristics such as age and income.
- Psychographic segmentation focuses on lifestyles, values, and personality.
- Behavioural segmentation focuses on purchasing and usage patterns.
- Concentrated marketing focuses on a limited segment.
- Differentiated marketing uses different approaches for multiple segments.
- A perceptual map represents customer perceptions, not necessarily objective product measurements.
- A customer persona should be supported by research.
