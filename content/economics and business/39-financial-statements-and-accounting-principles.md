Financial statements are structured reports that communicate the financial position and performance of an organisation.

They provide information about assets, liabilities, equity, income, expenses, and cash flows.

Business owners, managers, investors, lenders, regulators, and other stakeholders use financial statements to evaluate an organisation's financial condition and make decisions.

## 1. Meaning of Financial Statements

Financial statements summarise the financial activities of an organisation over a particular period or at a particular date.

Their main purposes include:

- Measuring financial performance.
- Reporting financial position.
- Evaluating liquidity and solvency.
- Supporting investment decisions.
- Assessing management performance.
- Providing information to lenders and creditors.
- Supporting regulatory compliance.

Financial statements are generally prepared using an applicable accounting framework and reporting standards.

## 2. Main Financial Statements

The major financial statements include:

1. Income Statement.
2. Balance Sheet or Statement of Financial Position.
3. Cash Flow Statement.
4. Statement of Changes in Equity.

These statements provide different but connected information about the organisation.

## 3. Basic Accounting Equation

The accounting equation expresses the relationship between assets, liabilities, and owners' equity.

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

### Assets

Assets are economic resources controlled by an organisation as a result of past events.

Examples include:

- Cash.
- Inventory.
- Buildings.
- Machinery.
- Accounts receivable.
- Vehicles.

### Liabilities

Liabilities are present obligations of an organisation arising from past events.

Examples include:

- Bank loans.
- Accounts payable.
- Outstanding salaries.
- Taxes payable.
- Other financial obligations.

### Equity

Equity represents the residual interest in the assets of an organisation after deducting liabilities.

Equity = Assets - Liabilities

For a company, equity may include share capital, retained earnings, and other reserves.

### Example

A business has:

- Assets = Rs. 800,000.
- Liabilities = Rs. 300,000.

Equity = 800,000 - 300,000

Equity = Rs. 500,000.

The accounting equation is:

800,000 = 300,000 + 500,000.

## 4. Income Statement

An income statement reports an organisation's financial performance over a specified period.

It summarises income, expenses, and the resulting profit or loss.

A simplified structure is:

Revenue - Expenses = Profit or Loss

### Revenue

Revenue represents income generated from an organisation's ordinary activities.

Examples include:

- Sales of goods.
- Service fees.
- Subscription income.
- Commission income.

### Cost of Goods Sold

Cost of Goods Sold represents the direct cost associated with goods sold during a period.

For a merchandising business:

Cost of Goods Sold = Opening Inventory + Net Purchases - Closing Inventory

### Gross Profit

Gross Profit = Revenue - Cost of Goods Sold

Gross profit measures the amount remaining after deducting the cost of goods sold.

### Operating Expenses

Operating expenses are costs incurred in conducting ordinary business operations.

Examples include:

- Salaries.
- Rent.
- Advertising.
- Utilities.
- Administrative expenses.
- Depreciation.

### Operating Profit

Operating Profit = Gross Profit - Operating Expenses

This simplified calculation excludes non-operating income and expenses.

### Net Profit

Net profit is the profit remaining after relevant expenses, finance costs, and taxes are accounted for.

The exact presentation depends on the accounting framework and nature of the business.

## 5. Example of an Income Statement

Consider the following simplified figures for a business.

| Particulars | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Revenue | Rs. 500,000 |
| Cost of Goods Sold | Rs. 300,000 |
| Gross Profit | Rs. 200,000 |
| Operating Expenses | Rs. 100,000 |
| Operating Profit | Rs. 100,000 |
| Finance Costs | Rs. 10,000 |
| Profit Before Tax | Rs. 90,000 |
| Income Tax Expense | Rs. 20,000 |
| Net Profit | Rs. 70,000 |

The business generates Rs. 70,000 in net profit for the period.

## 6. Balance Sheet

A balance sheet, also known as a statement of financial position, reports the assets, liabilities, and equity of an organisation at a specific date.

It is based on the accounting equation:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

### Current Assets

Current assets are assets expected to be realised, sold, or consumed within the normal operating cycle or another applicable current-asset classification period.

Examples include:

- Cash.
- Inventory.
- Trade receivables.
- Short-term investments.

### Non-Current Assets

Non-current assets are resources held for long-term use.

Examples include:

- Buildings.
- Machinery.
- Land.
- Vehicles.
- Certain intangible assets.

### Current Liabilities

Current liabilities are obligations classified as current under the applicable accounting framework.

Examples include:

- Trade payables.
- Short-term borrowings.
- Outstanding expenses.
- Certain current portions of long-term debt.

### Non-Current Liabilities

Non-current liabilities are obligations classified as long-term.

Examples include:

- Long-term bank loans.
- Long-term bonds.
- Certain lease liabilities.

### Example of a Simplified Balance Sheet

| Particulars | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Cash | Rs. 50,000 |
| Inventory | Rs. 100,000 |
| Equipment | Rs. 250,000 |
| Total Assets | Rs. 400,000 |
| Current Liabilities | Rs. 80,000 |
| Long-Term Loan | Rs. 120,000 |
| Total Liabilities | Rs. 200,000 |
| Equity | Rs. 200,000 |
| Total Liabilities and Equity | Rs. 400,000 |

The accounting equation is satisfied because total assets equal total liabilities plus equity.

## 7. Cash Flow Statement

A cash flow statement reports cash and cash-equivalent movements during a particular period.

It classifies cash flows into three major categories.

### Operating Activities

Operating activities relate to the main revenue-generating activities of the business.

Examples include:

- Cash received from customers.
- Cash paid to suppliers.
- Employee payments.
- Certain operating expenses.

### Investing Activities

Investing activities concern the acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and certain investments.

Examples include:

- Purchasing machinery.
- Selling equipment.
- Acquiring certain investments.
- Selling long-term assets.

### Financing Activities

Financing activities relate to changes in the organisation's capital and borrowings.

Examples include:

- Issuing shares.
- Borrowing money.
- Repaying loans.
- Paying dividends.

### Importance of the Cash Flow Statement

It helps users understand:

- Whether operations generate cash.
- Whether the organisation can meet obligations.
- How much is invested in long-term assets.
- How activities are financed.
- Whether cash reserves are increasing or decreasing.

A profitable business may still face cash shortages if customers delay payments or substantial cash is invested in inventory and assets.

## 8. Statement of Changes in Equity

The statement of changes in equity explains movements in owners' equity during a reporting period.

These movements may include:

- New share issues.
- Profit or loss for the period.
- Dividends.
- Other comprehensive income.
- Transfers between reserves.
- Other changes recognised under the applicable accounting framework.

It helps explain why the equity balance differs between the beginning and end of a period.

## 9. Accounting Principles and Concepts

Accounting principles provide a framework for recording and reporting financial transactions.

### Business Entity Concept

The business is treated as separate from its owners for accounting purposes.

Personal transactions of owners are generally distinguished from business transactions.

### Going Concern Concept

Financial statements are generally prepared on the assumption that the organisation will continue operating for the foreseeable future unless evidence suggests otherwise.

### Accrual Basis of Accounting

Transactions are generally recorded when the related economic events occur rather than only when cash is received or paid.

For example, revenue may be recognised when goods are delivered and the relevant recognition requirements are satisfied, even if payment is received later.

### Consistency

Accounting policies should be applied consistently across periods unless a justified change is required or permitted.

Consistency improves comparability.

### Prudence

Prudence involves exercising caution when making judgements under conditions of uncertainty.

It does not permit deliberate understatement of assets or income or deliberate overstatement of liabilities and expenses.

### Materiality

Information is material when omitting, misstating, or obscuring it could reasonably influence decisions made by users of financial statements.

### Historical Cost

Under the historical cost approach, an asset is initially recorded at the amount paid or the relevant transaction value, subject to applicable accounting rules.

Some assets may subsequently be measured using other permitted bases.

### Matching

The matching concept is traditionally associated with recognising expenses in the period in which the related revenue is recognised, where appropriate.

Modern accounting standards apply specific recognition and measurement requirements rather than relying on matching alone.

## 10. Accounting Cycle

The accounting cycle is the sequence of activities involved in recording and reporting financial transactions.

Major stages include:

1. Identifying financial transactions.
2. Preparing source documents.
3. Recording transactions in journals.
4. Posting entries to ledger accounts.
5. Preparing an unadjusted trial balance.
6. Recording adjusting entries.
7. Preparing an adjusted trial balance.
8. Preparing financial statements.
9. Completing closing entries where applicable.

The cycle supports systematic and consistent financial reporting.

## 11. Double-Entry Bookkeeping

Double-entry bookkeeping records each transaction using at least two corresponding account entries.

The total debits and credits for each transaction must be equal.

### Basic Debit and Credit Rules

| Account Type | Increase | Decrease |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assets | Debit | Credit |
| Expenses | Debit | Credit |
| Liabilities | Credit | Debit |
| Equity | Credit | Debit |
| Revenue | Credit | Debit |

These rules help maintain the accounting equation.

### Example

A business purchases equipment for Rs. 20,000 using cash.

- Debit Equipment: Rs. 20,000.
- Credit Cash: Rs. 20,000.

Equipment increases, while cash decreases.

Total debits equal total credits.

## 12. Depreciation

Depreciation is the systematic allocation of the depreciable amount of a tangible asset over its useful life.

It reflects the consumption of the asset's economic benefits.

### Straight-Line Method

Annual Depreciation = (Cost - Residual Value) / Useful Life

Example:

- Asset Cost = Rs. 100,000.
- Residual Value = Rs. 10,000.
- Useful Life = 5 years.

Annual Depreciation = (100,000 - 10,000) / 5

Annual Depreciation = Rs. 18,000.

### Reducing Balance Method

The reducing balance method calculates depreciation by applying a specified rate to the asset's carrying amount at the beginning of each period.

Depreciation = Opening Carrying Amount × Depreciation Rate

This method generally results in higher depreciation in earlier periods.

## 13. Limitations of Financial Statements

Financial statements provide important information but have limitations.

- Many figures are based on estimates.
- Historical information may not reflect current market values.
- Inflation can affect comparisons over time.
- Some valuable resources, such as employee experience, are not directly recognised as assets.
- Different accounting policies may affect comparability.
- Financial statements do not fully explain qualitative factors.
- They may not predict future performance.

Financial statements should therefore be interpreted alongside other relevant information.

## Common Exam Traps

- The balance sheet reports financial position at a specific date.
- The income statement reports financial performance over a period.
- The cash flow statement classifies cash movements into operating, investing, and financing activities.
- Assets equal liabilities plus equity.
- Profit is not the same as cash generated.
- Accrual accounting recognises transactions based on economic events rather than only cash movements.
- Double-entry bookkeeping requires corresponding debit and credit entries.
- Depreciation allocates an asset's depreciable amount over its useful life.
- A trial balance can agree even when certain accounting errors exist.
