title: Business Ownership and Corporate Structures
level: intermediate
---

Business ownership determines who holds the legal rights and responsibilities associated with a business. Corporate structures determine how ownership, management, control, and accountability are organised.

Understanding ownership structures is important when starting a business, attracting investors, raising capital, and planning expansion.

## 1. Meaning of Business Ownership

Business ownership refers to the legal possession and control of a business or an interest in it.

Ownership determines:

- Who has rights over business assets.
- Who receives profits or distributions.
- Who makes important ownership decisions.
- Who bears financial and legal responsibilities.
- How ownership can be transferred.
- How the business continues when ownership changes.

The exact rights depend on the legal form of the organisation.

## 2. Ownership and Management

Ownership and management are related but distinct concepts.

**Ownership**

Ownership refers to the legal interest held by individuals or entities in a business.

**Management**

Management refers to planning, organising, directing, and controlling business operations.

In a small sole proprietorship, the owner often manages the business directly.

In a large company, shareholders own the company while directors and professional managers oversee its activities.

## 3. Share Capital

Share capital is the capital raised by a company through the issue of shares.

A share represents a unit of ownership in a company.

Shareholders may receive certain rights depending on the class of shares and applicable law.

Common rights may include:

- Voting rights.
- Rights to dividends when declared.
- Rights to receive information.
- Rights to participate in certain company decisions.
- Rights to receive a share of remaining assets after creditors are paid during liquidation, according to legal priority.

Owning shares does not mean that a shareholder directly owns individual company assets.

## 4. Types of Shares

### A. Equity Shares

Equity shares represent ownership in a company.

Equity shareholders generally:

- Have voting rights, subject to applicable provisions.
- May receive dividends when declared.
- Benefit from increases in company value.
- Bear investment risk.
- Receive distributions after creditors and other prior claims during liquidation.

Dividend payments are not guaranteed.

### B. Preference Shares

Preference shares generally carry preferential rights regarding dividend payments and repayment of capital during winding up.

Depending on their terms, they may be:

- Cumulative or non-cumulative.
- Participating or non-participating.
- Convertible or non-convertible.
- Redeemable, subject to applicable law.

Preference shareholders generally have restricted voting rights compared with equity shareholders, although certain circumstances provide voting rights under company law.

## 5. Private and Public Ownership

### Privately Held Company

A privately held company has ownership concentrated among private individuals or entities.

Its shares are not offered to the general public through a stock exchange listing.

Advantages may include:

- Greater ownership control.
- More flexibility in certain decisions.
- Less exposure to public-market pressures.
- Greater privacy in some business matters.

Challenges may include:

- Limited access to public equity markets.
- Dependence on private investors.
- Restrictions on share transfers.
- Difficulty attracting large amounts of capital.

### Publicly Listed Company

A publicly listed company has securities admitted to trading on a recognised stock exchange.

Advantages may include:

- Access to a wider investor base.
- Greater potential for raising equity capital.
- Market visibility.
- Liquidity for eligible investors.
- Opportunities for business expansion.

Challenges may include:

- Greater disclosure requirements.
- Regulatory compliance costs.
- Shareholder scrutiny.
- Market pressure.
- Potential changes in ownership and control.

A public company under company law is not necessarily a publicly listed company.

## 6. Corporate Governance

Corporate governance refers to the system of rules, processes, and relationships through which a company is directed and controlled.

It establishes accountability among shareholders, directors, management, and other stakeholders.

Important principles include:

### A. Accountability

Directors and managers should be answerable for their decisions and actions.

### B. Transparency

Companies should provide accurate and timely information to relevant stakeholders.

### C. Fairness

The rights and interests of shareholders and other relevant stakeholders should be treated fairly.

### D. Responsibility

Companies should comply with applicable laws and act responsibly.

### E. Independence

Appropriate independent oversight can reduce conflicts of interest and improve decision-making.

## 7. Board of Directors

The board of directors is responsible for overseeing the strategic direction and governance of a company.

Its responsibilities commonly include:

- Approving major strategies.
- Monitoring management performance.
- Overseeing financial reporting.
- Reviewing risk management.
- Protecting shareholder interests.
- Ensuring legal compliance.
- Appointing or evaluating senior management.

The board generally does not perform every routine operational task. Day-to-day activities are usually handled by management.

## 8. Types of Directors

Depending on the applicable company law and corporate structure, directors may include:

**Executive Directors**

Directors who participate in the company's day-to-day management.

**Non-Executive Directors**

Directors who provide oversight and guidance without being responsible for daily operations.

**Independent Directors**

Directors expected to exercise independent judgement and provide objective oversight according to legal requirements.

**Managing Director**

A director entrusted with substantial management powers under the company's legal and organisational arrangements.

**Nominee Director**

A director appointed to represent particular interests, subject to applicable duties and legal obligations.

## 9. Corporate Governance Mechanisms

Companies use different mechanisms to improve accountability.

Examples include:

- Board committees.
- Independent audits.
- Internal controls.
- Risk management systems.
- Disclosure requirements.
- Shareholder meetings.
- Regulatory supervision.
- Codes of conduct.

Common board committees include:

### Audit Committee

Oversees financial reporting, auditing, and internal control matters.

### Nomination and Remuneration Committee

Deals with director appointments, remuneration policies, and related matters.

### Risk Management Committee

Oversees the identification, assessment, and management of significant risks where required or established.

## 10. Separation of Ownership and Control

In large corporations, shareholders may own the company without directly managing its daily activities.

This separation can create an agency problem.

An agency problem occurs when the interests of those managing a business differ from the interests of its owners.

Example:

Managers may focus on increasing their own compensation or expanding the organisation even when those decisions do not maximise shareholder value.

Methods of reducing agency problems include:

- Performance-based compensation.
- Independent board oversight.
- Audits.
- Transparent reporting.
- Shareholder voting.
- Strong internal controls.

## 11. Corporate Social Responsibility and Accountability

Corporate accountability involves taking responsibility for business decisions and their effects.

Companies may be expected to consider:

- Financial performance.
- Legal compliance.
- Employee welfare.
- Consumer protection.
- Environmental impact.
- Community interests.

Responsible corporate governance recognises that sustainable performance depends on trust, ethical conduct, and effective oversight.

## 12. Business Ownership in India

Different ownership structures operate under different legal frameworks in India.

| Structure | Main Legal Framework |
| --- | --- |
| Traditional partnership | Indian Partnership Act, 1932 |
| Limited Liability Partnership | Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008 |
| Company | Companies Act, 2013 |
| Cooperative society | Applicable central or state cooperative laws |
| One Person Company | Companies Act, 2013 |

The appropriate structure depends on the business's objectives, ownership requirements, capital needs, and applicable legal conditions.

## Common Exam Traps

- Ownership and management are not the same.
- Shareholders own shares, not individual company assets.
- Dividends are not guaranteed payments.
- Preference shareholders generally receive priority in specified matters over equity shareholders.
- A public company is not necessarily listed on a stock exchange.
- Corporate governance concerns direction, control, accountability, and oversight.
- The board oversees company strategy and governance, while managers generally handle daily operations.
- Agency problems arise when managers' interests differ from owners' interests.
- Independent directors are intended to provide objective oversight.
