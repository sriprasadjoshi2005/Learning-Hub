title: Organisational Structure and Departmentalisation
level: intermediate
---

Organisational structure defines how responsibilities, authority, communication, and reporting relationships are arranged within a business.

Departmentalisation is the process of grouping related activities and jobs into departments to improve coordination and operational efficiency.

An appropriate organisational structure helps businesses coordinate employees, establish accountability, and achieve their objectives.

## 1. Meaning of Organisational Structure

Organisational structure is the formal arrangement of roles, responsibilities, authority, and relationships within an organisation.

It determines:

- Who performs particular tasks.
- Who reports to whom.
- How decisions are made.
- How departments coordinate.
- How information flows.
- Who is responsible for organisational results.

A clear structure reduces confusion and supports accountability.

## 2. Importance of Organisational Structure

Organisational structure provides several benefits.

- Clarifies responsibilities.
- Establishes authority.
- Improves communication.
- Supports coordination.
- Reduces duplication of work.
- Helps allocate resources.
- Facilitates growth.
- Improves accountability.

A poorly designed structure may create communication delays, conflicts, and inefficient decision-making.

## 3. Departmentalisation

Departmentalisation involves grouping similar activities into organisational units.

Businesses may use different methods depending on their size, products, customers, and operations.

## 4. Functional Structure

A functional structure groups employees according to specialised business functions.

Common departments include:

- Finance.
- Marketing.
- Human resources.
- Production.
- Operations.
- Research and development.

### Advantages

- Encourages specialisation.
- Develops functional expertise.
- Reduces duplication within departments.
- Creates clear functional responsibilities.
- Can improve operational efficiency.

### Disadvantages

- Departments may become isolated.
- Coordination between functions can be difficult.
- Decision-making may become centralised.
- Employees may focus on departmental goals rather than overall organisational objectives.

## 5. Divisional Structure

A divisional structure groups activities according to products, geographical regions, or customer categories.

Each division may have its own functional departments.

### A. Product-Based Divisional Structure

Departments are organised around different product lines.

Example:

A company may have separate divisions for:

- Electronics.
- Home appliances.
- Personal care products.

### B. Geographical Divisional Structure

Departments are organised according to geographical regions.

Example:

A business may operate separate divisions for:

- Northern India.
- Southern India.
- Western India.
- Eastern India.

### C. Customer-Based Divisional Structure

Departments are organised according to customer groups.

Example:

A financial services company may create separate divisions for:

- Individual customers.
- Small businesses.
- Large corporations.

### Advantages

- Greater focus on individual products or markets.
- Better responsiveness to local conditions.
- Clear divisional accountability.
- Easier performance evaluation.

### Disadvantages

- Duplication of resources.
- Higher operating costs.
- Competition between divisions.
- Inconsistent organisational practices.

## 6. Matrix Structure

A matrix structure combines functional departments with project-based or product-based teams.

Employees may report to both a functional manager and a project manager.

Example:

An engineer may belong to the engineering department while also working under the manager of a specific product-development project.

### Advantages

- Encourages collaboration.
- Improves resource sharing.
- Supports innovation.
- Provides flexibility.
- Encourages communication across departments.

### Disadvantages

- Dual reporting can create confusion.
- Conflicting priorities may arise.
- Coordination can become complex.
- Employees may experience increased workload.

## 7. Flat and Tall Structures

### Tall Organisational Structure

A tall structure has several management levels between top management and operational employees.

Characteristics include:

- Multiple reporting levels.
- Narrower spans of control.
- More formal supervision.
- Longer communication chains.

Advantages:

- Closer supervision.
- Clear hierarchical authority.
- More opportunities for managerial positions.

Disadvantages:

- Slower communication.
- Higher administrative costs.
- More layers of approval.
- Possible delays in decision-making.

### Flat Organisational Structure

A flat structure has fewer management levels.

Characteristics include:

- Fewer hierarchical layers.
- Wider spans of control.
- Greater employee autonomy.
- Shorter communication channels.

Advantages:

- Faster communication.
- Lower administrative costs.
- Greater employee responsibility.
- Quicker decision-making in suitable organisations.

Disadvantages:

- Managers may supervise too many employees.
- Responsibilities may become unclear.
- Employees may receive less individual supervision.

## 8. Authority, Responsibility and Accountability

These concepts are essential to organisational structure.

**Authority**

Authority is the formal right to make decisions, give instructions, and use organisational resources within assigned limits.

**Responsibility**

Responsibility is the obligation to perform assigned duties.

**Accountability**

Accountability means being answerable for the results of assigned responsibilities.

Authority should be sufficient to perform the responsibilities assigned to an individual.

## 9. Delegation of Authority

Delegation is the process through which a manager assigns tasks and grants appropriate authority to subordinates.

It generally involves:

1. Assigning duties.
2. Granting authority.
3. Establishing accountability.

### Advantages of Delegation

- Reduces managerial workload.
- Develops employee skills.
- Improves decision-making speed.
- Encourages responsibility.
- Supports organisational growth.

### Limitations of Delegation

- Managers may hesitate to share authority.
- Employees may lack the required skills.
- Poor communication may create confusion.
- Excessive delegation without control may create coordination problems.

Delegation does not completely remove the manager's responsibility for overall organisational performance.

## 10. Centralisation and Decentralisation

### Centralisation

Centralisation involves concentrating decision-making authority at higher levels of management.

Advantages:

- Consistent policies.
- Greater control.
- Uniform organisational decisions.
- Easier coordination in some situations.

Disadvantages:

- Slower local decisions.
- Heavy dependence on senior management.
- Limited employee participation.
- Reduced flexibility.

### Decentralisation

Decentralisation involves distributing decision-making authority to lower levels or different organisational units.

Advantages:

- Faster local decisions.
- Greater employee participation.
- Better responsiveness.
- Development of managerial skills.

Disadvantages:

- Risk of inconsistent decisions.
- Possible duplication of work.
- More complex coordination.
- Reduced central control.

## 11. Span of Control

Span of control refers to the number of employees who report directly to a manager.

A narrow span means a manager supervises relatively few employees.

A wide span means a manager supervises many employees.

Factors influencing span of control include:

- Nature of work.
- Employee experience.
- Managerial ability.
- Geographical distribution.
- Complexity of tasks.
- Communication systems.
- Degree of standardisation.

There is no universally correct span of control. It depends on organisational conditions.

## 12. Formal and Informal Organisation

**Formal Organisation**

A formal organisation is the officially established structure of roles, responsibilities, and reporting relationships.

**Informal Organisation**

An informal organisation develops naturally through personal relationships, social interaction, and shared interests among employees.

Informal networks can improve communication and cooperation, but they may also spread inaccurate information or create resistance to formal decisions.

## Common Exam Traps

- Organisational structure defines formal authority and reporting relationships.
- Functional structure groups employees according to specialised activities.
- Divisional structure groups activities by products, regions, or customers.
- Matrix structures commonly involve dual reporting relationships.
- Tall structures have more hierarchical levels, while flat structures have fewer.
- Authority is the right to make decisions, while responsibility is the obligation to perform duties.
- Accountability means being answerable for results.
- Delegation assigns duties and appropriate authority but does not eliminate managerial accountability.
- Centralisation concentrates decision-making, while decentralisation distributes it.
- Span of control measures the number of direct subordinates supervised by a manager.
