Financial management involves planning, organising, directing, and controlling the financial resources of a business. It ensures that an organisation obtains funds when required and uses them effectively to achieve its objectives.

Every business needs finance to establish operations, purchase assets, pay employees, manage daily expenses, expand activities, and respond to unexpected situations.

## 1. Meaning of Financial Management

Financial management is the process of making decisions concerning the acquisition, allocation, and utilisation of financial resources.

Its major objectives include:

- Ensuring adequate availability of funds.
- Using financial resources efficiently.
- Maintaining sufficient liquidity.
- Improving profitability.
- Controlling financial risks.
- Supporting business growth.
- Increasing the long-term value of the organisation.

Financial management connects business operations with financial planning and strategic decision-making.

## 2. Importance of Financial Management

Financial management is essential because almost every business activity requires financial resources.

Its importance includes:

- Investment decisions: Selecting suitable assets and projects.
- Financing decisions: Determining how funds should be obtained.
- Dividend decisions: Deciding how much profit should be distributed to shareholders.
- Liquidity management: Ensuring that short-term obligations can be paid.
- Risk management: Identifying and controlling financial uncertainty.
- Performance evaluation: Measuring financial results.
- Growth planning: Providing funds for expansion and development.

## 3. Major Financial Decisions

Financial management involves three major categories of decisions.

### Investment Decisions

Investment decisions determine how a business should allocate its funds among different assets and projects.

Examples include:

- Purchasing machinery.
- Constructing a new factory.
- Developing new products.
- Acquiring another company.
- Investing in technology.

Investment decisions generally consider expected returns, costs, risks, and the time required to recover the investment.

### Financing Decisions

Financing decisions determine the sources through which a business obtains the funds required for its activities.

Common sources include:

- Equity capital.
- Retained earnings.
- Bank loans.
- Bonds.
- Trade credit.

The choice depends on cost, risk, repayment obligations, ownership considerations, and the financial position of the business.

### Dividend Decisions

Dividend decisions determine how much profit should be distributed to shareholders and how much should be retained within the business.

A company may distribute profits as dividends or retain them to finance future growth.

## 4. Profit Maximisation and Wealth Maximisation

### Profit Maximisation

Profit maximisation focuses on increasing the difference between revenue and costs.

Profit = Total Revenue - Total Cost

It is an important business objective, but focusing only on immediate profit may encourage decisions that damage long-term performance.

For example, reducing product quality may lower costs temporarily but damage customer trust.

### Wealth Maximisation

Wealth maximisation focuses on increasing the long-term value of the business for its owners.

For a publicly traded company, shareholder wealth is commonly associated with the market value of its shares.

Wealth maximisation considers:

- Expected future cash flows.
- Investment risk.
- Timing of returns.
- Long-term business sustainability.
- Cost of capital.

It provides a broader perspective than short-term profit maximisation.

## 5. Time Value of Money

The time value of money is the principle that money available today is generally worth more than the same amount received in the future.

This is because money available today can potentially be invested to earn a return.

### Future Value

Future value calculates what a present amount will become after earning interest over a specified period.

FV = PV × (1 + r)^n

Where:

- FV = Future Value.
- PV = Present Value.
- r = Interest rate per period.
- n = Number of periods.

**Example:**

A business invests Rs. 10,000 at an annual interest rate of 10% for two years.

FV = 10,000 × (1.10)^2

FV = Rs. 12,100.

### Present Value

Present value calculates the current worth of a future amount.

PV = FV / (1 + r)^n

**Example:**

A business expects to receive Rs. 12,100 after two years. If the discount rate is 10% per year:

PV = 12,100 / (1.10)^2

PV = Rs. 10,000.

Present value allows financial managers to compare cash flows occurring at different times.

## 6. Cost of Capital

Cost of capital is the required rate of return that a business must earn on its investments to satisfy its providers of finance.

Different sources of finance have different costs.

Examples include:

- Cost of equity: The return expected by shareholders.
- Cost of debt: The effective borrowing cost after considering relevant tax effects.
- Cost of preference shares: The return required by preference shareholders.

The Weighted Average Cost of Capital combines the costs of different financing sources according to their proportions in the capital structure.

WACC = (E / V × Re) + (D / V × Rd × (1 - T))

Where:

- E = Market value of equity.
- D = Market value of debt.
- V = Total market value of financing.
- Re = Cost of equity.
- Rd = Cost of debt before tax.
- T = Corporate tax rate.

WACC is commonly used as a discount rate when evaluating projects with risk characteristics similar to those of the business.

## 7. Capital Budgeting

Capital budgeting is the process of evaluating long-term investment projects.

Examples include purchasing machinery, constructing facilities, and introducing major production systems.

Important capital budgeting methods include:

### Payback Period

The payback period measures how long an investment takes to recover its initial cost through cash inflows.

Payback Period = Initial Investment / Annual Cash Inflow

This simple formula applies when annual cash inflows are equal.

**Example:**

A project costs Rs. 100,000 and generates Rs. 25,000 annually.

Payback Period = 100,000 / 25,000

Payback Period = 4 years.

The method is simple but ignores cash flows received after the payback period and generally does not account for the time value of money.

### Net Present Value

Net Present Value calculates the difference between the present value of future cash inflows and the initial investment.

NPV = Present Value of Cash Inflows - Initial Investment

A positive NPV indicates that a project is expected to generate value above the required return, based on the assumptions used.

### Internal Rate of Return

Internal Rate of Return is the discount rate at which a project's NPV becomes zero.

It represents the project's implied rate of return.

It is commonly compared with the required rate of return when evaluating investment opportunities.

## 8. Financial Risk and Return

Financial decisions involve a relationship between risk and expected return.

Risk refers to the possibility that actual financial outcomes may differ from expected outcomes.

Common financial risks include:

- Market risk: Losses caused by changes in market prices.
- Credit risk: The possibility that borrowers or customers fail to make payments.
- Liquidity risk: Difficulty meeting financial obligations or converting assets into cash.
- Interest rate risk: Losses resulting from changes in interest rates.
- Foreign exchange risk: Losses caused by currency fluctuations.
- Operational risk: Losses caused by failures in processes, systems, or people.

Businesses manage financial risks through diversification, insurance, appropriate borrowing, cash reserves, and internal controls.

## 9. Financial Leverage

Financial leverage refers to the use of borrowed funds to finance business activities.

Debt can increase returns to shareholders when the business earns more from its investments than the effective cost of borrowing.

However, debt also creates fixed repayment and interest obligations.

High financial leverage can increase financial risk, especially when revenue or cash flow declines.

## 10. Working Capital and Financial Stability

Working capital supports everyday business activities.

Net Working Capital = Current Assets - Current Liabilities

A business needs sufficient short-term resources to pay employees, suppliers, and other obligations.

Financial managers must balance liquidity and profitability.

Holding too much cash may reduce investment opportunities, while holding too little may create payment difficulties.

## 11. Financial Control

Financial control ensures that financial resources are used according to organisational plans and policies.

Important activities include:

- Comparing actual results with budgets.
- Monitoring cash flows.
- Reviewing expenses.
- Evaluating financial performance.
- Identifying unusual transactions.
- Maintaining accurate financial records.
- Conducting internal and external audits.

Financial control supports accountability and reduces the possibility of financial errors and misuse of resources.

## Common Exam Traps

- Investment decisions concern the allocation of funds; financing decisions concern obtaining funds.
- Dividend decisions determine the distribution and retention of profits.
- Wealth maximisation considers long-term value, risk, and the timing of cash flows.
- Present value discounts future money; future value compounds present money.
- Payback period measures recovery time but does not fully account for the time value of money.
- NPV compares the present value of future cash flows with the initial investment.
- High financial leverage can increase both potential returns and financial risk.
- Cost of debt and cost of equity are different components of financing cost.
