# Lesson 3: States of Matter

Matter exists in different physical states depending on the arrangement, motion, and interactions of its particles. The three familiar states are solids, liquids, and gases. Under suitable conditions, matter can also exist as plasma and other specialized states.

Understanding the states of matter helps explain melting, freezing, evaporation, condensation, sublimation, diffusion, and the behaviour of substances when temperature and pressure change. These concepts form the foundation for studying gas laws, thermodynamics, and physical chemistry.

## 1. The Particulate Model of Matter

### 1.1 Basic Idea

The particulate model explains the behaviour of matter by considering its microscopic particles.

According to this model:

- Matter consists of tiny particles.
- Particles are in continuous motion.
- There are spaces between particles.
- Particles attract one another.
- Particle motion generally increases with temperature.

The physical state of a substance depends on the balance between particle motion and intermolecular attractions.

### 1.2 Arrangement of Particles

The arrangement of particles differs in each state.

**Solids**
- Particles are closely packed.
- Particles occupy relatively fixed positions.
- Particles mainly vibrate about their positions.
- Strong cohesive forces generally maintain their structure.

**Liquids**
- Particles remain relatively close together.
- Particles can move past one another.
- Attractive forces maintain a definite volume.
- Their arrangement is less ordered than in solids.

**Gases**
- Particles are widely separated.
- Particles move rapidly and randomly.
- Interparticle attractions are generally weak under ordinary conditions.
- Particles occupy the available container volume.

## 2. Solid State

### 2.1 Definition

A solid is a state of matter characterized by a definite shape and definite volume.

### 2.2 Properties of Solids

- Have a definite shape.
- Have a definite volume.
- Are generally almost incompressible.
- Usually possess relatively high density.
- Do not flow like ordinary liquids.
- Particles vibrate around relatively fixed positions.

### 2.3 Types of Solids

Solids can be broadly classified into crystalline and amorphous solids.

**Crystalline Solids**

Crystalline solids have a regular and repeating arrangement of particles.

Characteristics:
- Long-range structural order.
- Generally possess a definite melting point.
- Often have geometrical crystal shapes.
- May show anisotropy in physical properties.

Examples:
- Sodium chloride.
- Quartz.
- Diamond.
- Copper sulfate crystals.

**Amorphous Solids**

Amorphous solids do not possess long-range regular arrangement of particles.

Characteristics:
- Lack long-range structural order.
- Generally soften over a temperature range.
- Often show isotropic behaviour.
- May fracture irregularly.

Examples:
- Glass.
- Rubber.
- Many plastics.
- Some resins.

### 2.4 Crystalline and Amorphous Solids: Comparison

| Crystalline solids | Amorphous solids |
|---|---|
| Regular long-range arrangement. | No long-range regular arrangement. |
| Generally have a sharp melting point. | Generally soften over a temperature range. |
| Often anisotropic. | Generally isotropic. |
| Possess crystal structures. | Lack regular crystal structures. |
| Example: NaCl. | Example: ordinary glass. |

## 3. Liquid State

### 3.1 Definition

A liquid is a state of matter that has a definite volume but no definite shape.

A liquid takes the shape of its container while maintaining approximately constant volume under ordinary conditions.

### 3.2 Properties of Liquids

- Have a definite volume.
- Do not have a definite shape.
- Flow and can be poured.
- Are only slightly compressible.
- Have particles that move past one another.
- Possess a free surface when placed in an open container.

### 3.3 Important Properties of Liquids

**Viscosity**

Viscosity is the resistance of a fluid to flow.

- High viscosity means slower flow.
- Low viscosity means easier flow.

Examples:
- Honey has relatively high viscosity.
- Water has relatively low viscosity.

**Surface Tension**

Surface tension is the tendency of a liquid surface to minimize its surface area due to cohesive intermolecular forces.

It explains:
- Formation of spherical liquid droplets.
- Floating of some small insects on water.
- Capillary effects.

**Vapour Pressure**

Vapour pressure is the pressure exerted by vapour in equilibrium with its liquid at a specified temperature.

The vapour pressure of a liquid generally increases with temperature.

**Boiling Point**

The boiling point is the temperature at which the vapour pressure of a liquid becomes equal to the external pressure.

## 4. Gaseous State

### 4.1 Definition

A gas is a state of matter that has neither a definite shape nor a definite volume.

A gas expands to occupy the available space in its container.

### 4.2 Properties of Gases

- No definite shape.
- No definite volume.
- Highly compressible.
- Generally have low density.
- Diffuse rapidly.
- Exert pressure on container walls.
- Expand when heated at constant pressure.

### 4.3 Gas Pressure

Gas pressure arises from collisions of gas particles with the walls of their container.

Pressure is defined as force per unit area.

\[
\boxed{P=\frac{F}{A}}
\]

Where:

- \(P\) = Pressure.
- \(F\) = Force.
- \(A\) = Area.

The SI unit of pressure is the pascal (Pa).

\[
1\text{ Pa}=1\text{ N/m}^2
\]

Other commonly used units include:
- Atmosphere (atm).
- Bar.
- Millimetre of mercury (mmHg).
- Torr.

### 4.4 Compressibility

Compressibility describes how much the volume of a substance changes when pressure is applied.

Gases are highly compressible because their particles have large spaces between them.

Liquids and solids are much less compressible because their particles are closely packed.

## 5. Plasma and Other States of Matter

### 5.1 Plasma

Plasma is an ionized state of matter containing free electrons and positively charged ions.

Characteristics:
- Contains charged particles.
- Conducts electricity.
- Responds to electric and magnetic fields.
- Often occurs at very high temperatures.

Examples:
- Stars.
- Lightning.
- Neon discharge lamps.
- Fluorescent lamps.

Plasma is often described as the fourth state of matter.

### 5.2 Bose-Einstein Condensate

A Bose-Einstein condensate is a state formed when certain bosonic particles are cooled to extremely low temperatures near absolute zero.

At these temperatures, many particles occupy the same quantum state, producing collective quantum behaviour.

### 5.3 Other Specialized States

Other states and phases include:
- Superfluid phases.
- Superconducting states.
- Degenerate matter.
- Quark-gluon plasma.

These occur under specialized physical conditions and are studied in advanced physics and materials science.

## 6. Changes of State

Matter can change from one physical state to another when temperature or pressure changes.

### 6.1 Melting

Melting is the conversion of a solid into a liquid by absorbing heat.

\[
\text{Solid}\rightarrow\text{Liquid}
\]

Example:

\[
H_2O(s)\rightarrow H_2O(l)
\]

The temperature at which a solid melts at a specified pressure is its melting point.

### 6.2 Freezing

Freezing is the conversion of a liquid into a solid by releasing heat.

\[
\text{Liquid}\rightarrow\text{Solid}
\]

Example:

\[
H_2O(l)\rightarrow H_2O(s)
\]

### 6.3 Vaporization

Vaporization is the conversion of a liquid into a gas.

It occurs through evaporation or boiling.

\[
\text{Liquid}\rightarrow\text{Gas}
\]

**Evaporation**

Evaporation occurs at the surface of a liquid and can take place below its boiling point.

Factors affecting evaporation:
- Temperature.
- Surface area.
- Humidity.
- Air movement.
- Nature of the liquid.

**Boiling**

Boiling occurs throughout a liquid when its vapour pressure equals the external pressure.

### 6.4 Condensation

Condensation is the conversion of a gas or vapour into a liquid.

\[
\text{Gas}\rightarrow\text{Liquid}
\]

Example: Formation of water droplets on the outside of a cold glass.

### 6.5 Sublimation

Sublimation is the direct conversion of a solid into a gas without passing through the liquid state.

\[
\text{Solid}\rightarrow\text{Gas}
\]

Examples:
- Dry ice.
- Iodine.
- Naphthalene.

### 6.6 Deposition

Deposition is the direct conversion of a gas into a solid.

\[
\text{Gas}\rightarrow\text{Solid}
\]

Example: Formation of frost from water vapour.

### 6.7 Summary of State Changes

| Process | Change |
|---|---|
| Melting | Solid → Liquid |
| Freezing | Liquid → Solid |
| Vaporization | Liquid → Gas |
| Condensation | Gas → Liquid |
| Sublimation | Solid → Gas |
| Deposition | Gas → Solid |

## 7. Heating and Cooling Curves

### 7.1 Heating Curve

A heating curve represents the temperature of a substance as heat is supplied.

For a typical pure substance at constant pressure:

1. The solid warms.
2. The solid melts at a constant temperature.
3. The liquid warms.
4. The liquid boils at a constant temperature.
5. The gas warms.

During melting and boiling, the supplied energy is used to overcome intermolecular attractions rather than increase the average kinetic energy of the particles.

### 7.2 Latent Heat

Latent heat is the energy absorbed or released during a change of state without a corresponding change in temperature for a pure substance at constant pressure.

The heat involved in a phase change can be calculated using:

\[
\boxed{Q=mL}
\]

Where:

- \(Q\) = Heat energy.
- \(m\) = Mass.
- \(L\) = Specific latent heat.

Two important types are:

- Latent heat of fusion.
- Latent heat of vaporization.

### 7.3 Numerical Example

Calculate the heat required to melt 0.5 kg of ice if its specific latent heat of fusion is \(334\,000\text{ J/kg}\).

Given:

\[
m=0.5\text{ kg}
\]

\[
L=334000\text{ J/kg}
\]

Using:

\[
Q=mL
\]

\[
Q=0.5\times334000
\]

\[
\boxed{Q=167000\text{ J}}
\]

## 8. Diffusion and Brownian Motion

### 8.1 Diffusion

Diffusion is the spontaneous movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration due to random particle motion.

Examples:
- Perfume spreading through a room.
- Food colouring spreading in water.
- Oxygen moving from the lungs into the blood.

Diffusion generally occurs faster in gases than in liquids because gas particles move more freely.

### 8.2 Brownian Motion

Brownian motion is the continuous random movement of small particles suspended in a fluid due to collisions with the rapidly moving molecules of the surrounding medium.

It provides evidence for the particulate nature of matter.

## 9. Important Comparisons and Observations

### 9.1 Three Common States of Matter

| Property | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | Definite | Not definite | Not definite |
| Volume | Definite | Definite | Not definite |
| Compressibility | Very low | Low | High |
| Particle arrangement | Closely packed | Close but mobile | Widely separated |
| Particle motion | Mainly vibration | Sliding and translation | Rapid random motion |
| Density | Generally high | Generally moderate to high | Generally low |
| Diffusion | Very slow | Moderate | Fast |

### 9.2 Effect of Temperature

Increasing temperature generally increases the average kinetic energy of particles.

As temperature rises:
- Particle motion becomes more energetic.
- Solids may melt.
- Liquids may vaporize.
- Gases may expand if pressure is constant.

### 9.3 Effect of Pressure

Increasing pressure can favour states with smaller volume.

For example, increasing pressure can promote liquefaction of a gas when the temperature is suitable.

## 10. Common Exam Traps

1. Liquids have definite volume but no definite shape.
2. Gases occupy the entire available container volume.
3. Evaporation occurs at the surface and can take place below the boiling point.
4. Boiling occurs throughout the liquid.
5. Sublimation does not involve the liquid state.
6. During a phase change of a pure substance at constant pressure, temperature remains constant.
7. Plasma contains charged particles and is electrically conducting.
8. Brownian motion results from collisions with surrounding fluid particles.
9. Gases are more compressible because of large interparticle spaces.
10. Vapour pressure increases with increasing temperature.
