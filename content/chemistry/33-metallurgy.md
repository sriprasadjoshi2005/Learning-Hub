**Metallurgy** is the extraction of metals from their **ores**. It follows three broad stages: concentration, extraction (reduction), and refining.

## Concentration

- **Hydraulic washing** uses density differences (heavy oxide ores).
- **Magnetic separation** is used when one of the components is magnetic (e.g. $\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}$).
- **Froth flotation** separates sulfide ores: pine oil makes the mineral particles stick to the froth, while depressants such as NaCN can be used to separate two sulfides.
- **Leaching** dissolves the ore chemically: bauxite in hot NaOH gives sodium aluminate, and gold and silver are leached with NaCN.

## Roasting and calcination

**Roasting** heats a sulfide ore in excess air to give the oxide ($\mathrm{2ZnS + 3O_2 \rightarrow 2ZnO + 2SO_2}$). **Calcination** heats carbonate or hydrated ores in limited air to drive off $\mathrm{CO_2}$ or water ($\mathrm{ZnCO_3 \rightarrow ZnO + CO_2}$).

## Thermodynamics: the Ellingham diagram

An Ellingham diagram plots $\Delta G^\circ$ for forming oxides against temperature. A metal can reduce the oxide of another metal if its line lies **below** it. Most lines slope upward, since forming a solid oxide from a gas ($\mathrm{O_2}$) lowers entropy, so $\Delta S < 0$ and $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$ rises with $T$. The line for $\mathrm{C \rightarrow CO}$ slopes **downward**, because one mole of gas gives two moles of gas ($\Delta S > 0$). That is why carbon can reduce many oxides at high enough temperature.

## Key industrial processes

- **Blast furnace (iron):** $\mathrm{CO}$ reduces $\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}$. Limestone decomposes to $\mathrm{CaO}$, which combines with silica impurities to form a fusible **slag**, $\mathrm{CaSiO_3}$.
- **Copper:** roasted ore is smelted to a matte of $\mathrm{Cu_2S}$ and FeS; self-reduction in a Bessemer converter gives blister copper: $\mathrm{2Cu_2O + Cu_2S \rightarrow 6Cu + SO_2}$.
- **Aluminium (Hall-Héroult):** purified $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$ is electrolysed in molten **cryolite**, $\mathrm{Na_3AlF_6}$, which lowers the melting point and improves conductivity. Graphite anodes are slowly consumed.

## Refining

Electrolytic refining (Cu, Zn, Ag), zone refining (very pure Si, Ge), vapour-phase refining (Mond process for Ni, van Arkel for Ti and Zr), and distillation (Zn, Hg).

## Quiz

1. How many kg of iron can be obtained from $1000$ kg of pure $\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}$? ($\mathrm{Fe} = 55.85$, $\mathrm{O} = 16.00$)
   - [ ] 350 kg
   - [ ] 466 kg
   - [x] 699 kg
   - [ ] 1000 kg
   > $M(\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}) = 159.7$ and it contains $2\times55.85 = 111.7$ of iron, so the iron fraction is $0.699$. The value 350 kg wrongly takes only one iron atom per formula unit.

2. Why does the Ellingham line for $\mathrm{C + \tfrac12 O_2 \rightarrow CO}$ slope downward, unlike most metal-oxide lines?
   - [ ] Carbon monoxide is very stable at low temperature.
   - [x] The reaction increases the number of moles of gas, so $\Delta S$ is positive.
   - [ ] The reaction is endothermic.
   - [ ] Carbon is a better conductor than metals.
   > $\Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ$ has slope $-\Delta S^\circ$. Here half a mole of gas becomes one mole, so $\Delta S^\circ > 0$ and $\Delta G^\circ$ falls with temperature.

3. In the electrolytic extraction of aluminium, cryolite ($\mathrm{Na_3AlF_6}$) is added to $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$ mainly to:
   - [x] lower the melting point of the mixture and increase its conductivity
   - [ ] reduce the alumina chemically
   - [ ] act as the anode
   - [ ] remove silica as slag
   > Pure alumina melts at about $2000\,^\circ$C and is a poor conductor. With cryolite, electrolysis works at about $1000\,^\circ$C, saving a great deal of energy.
