# Lesson 10: Periodic Table and Periodic Trends

The periodic table is one of the most important organizing principles in chemistry. It arranges chemical elements according to their atomic numbers and reveals repeating patterns in their physical and chemical properties.

The periodic classification of elements makes it possible to predict chemical behaviour, understand electron configurations, compare atomic sizes, and explain trends in ionization energy, electron affinity, electronegativity, and metallic character.

This lesson introduces the historical development of the periodic table, the modern periodic law, periods, groups, blocks, electronic configuration, and the major periodic trends.

## 1. Development of the Periodic Table

### 1.1 Early Classification of Elements

As more elements were discovered, scientists attempted to organize them according to their properties.

Several classification systems were proposed before the modern periodic table was developed.

### 1.2 Dobereiner's Triads

Johann Wolfgang Döbereiner proposed grouping certain elements into sets of three called triads.

In each triad:
- The elements had similar chemical properties.
- The atomic mass of the middle element was approximately the arithmetic mean of the other two.

Example:

Lithium, sodium, and potassium.

Approximate atomic masses:

\[
Li=7,\quad Na=23,\quad K=39
\]

Mean of the first and third:

\[
\frac{7+39}{2}=23
\]

This is approximately the atomic mass of sodium.

**Limitation:** Only a limited number of elements could be arranged into suitable triads.

### 1.3 Newlands' Law of Octaves

John Newlands arranged elements in increasing order of atomic mass.

He observed that every eighth element showed similarities in properties when the elements were arranged in this way.

This observation was called the law of octaves.

**Limitation:**

- It worked reasonably well for some lighter elements.
- It failed for many heavier elements.
- It did not leave adequate gaps for undiscovered elements.

### 1.4 Mendeleev's Periodic Table

Dmitri Mendeleev arranged elements primarily in increasing order of atomic mass and grouped elements with similar chemical properties.

His periodic law stated that the properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic masses.

**Major achievements:**

1. Elements with similar properties were placed together.
2. Gaps were left for undiscovered elements.
3. Properties of some undiscovered elements were predicted.
4. Incorrectly determined atomic masses could sometimes be identified and corrected.

### 1.5 Limitations of Mendeleev's Table

- Hydrogen did not have a completely satisfactory position.
- Isotopes could not be explained naturally using atomic mass ordering.
- Some elements appeared out of strict increasing atomic mass order.
- The underlying reason for periodicity was not fully explained.

## 2. Modern Periodic Law

### 2.1 Statement

The modern periodic law states:

"The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers."

This means that when elements are arranged in increasing order of atomic number, similar properties recur at regular intervals.

### 2.2 Importance of Atomic Number

Atomic number determines the number of protons in an atom and, for a neutral atom, the number of electrons.

The electronic configuration depends on the atomic number.

Since chemical behaviour is strongly influenced by electronic configuration, periodic properties are related to atomic number.

## 3. Structure of the Modern Periodic Table

### 3.1 Periods

Horizontal rows in the periodic table are called periods.

There are seven periods.

| Period | Number of elements |
|---|---:|
| 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 8 |
| 3 | 8 |
| 4 | 18 |
| 5 | 18 |
| 6 | 32 |
| 7 | 32 |

The first period contains hydrogen and helium.

The sixth and seventh periods include the lanthanoids and actinoids, respectively, which are commonly displayed separately below the main body of the table.

### 3.2 Groups

Vertical columns are called groups.

The modern periodic table contains 18 groups.

Elements in the same group often have similar valence-shell electronic configurations and related chemical properties.

### 3.3 Important Groups

| Group | Common name | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Alkali metals | Li, Na, K |
| 2 | Alkaline earth metals | Be, Mg, Ca |
| 13 | Boron group | B, Al, Ga |
| 14 | Carbon group | C, Si, Ge |
| 15 | Nitrogen group | N, P, As |
| 16 | Chalcogens | O, S, Se |
| 17 | Halogens | F, Cl, Br |
| 18 | Noble gases | He, Ne, Ar |

### 3.4 Important Families

**Alkali Metals**

Group 1 elements other than hydrogen are called alkali metals.

Examples:
- Lithium.
- Sodium.
- Potassium.

They generally have one valence electron and commonly form +1 ions.

**Alkaline Earth Metals**

Group 2 elements are called alkaline earth metals.

Examples:
- Beryllium.
- Magnesium.
- Calcium.

They generally have two valence electrons and commonly form +2 ions.

**Halogens**

Group 17 elements are called halogens.

Examples:
- Fluorine.
- Chlorine.
- Bromine.
- Iodine.

They generally have seven valence electrons and commonly form -1 ions.

**Noble Gases**

Group 18 elements are called noble gases.

Examples:
- Helium.
- Neon.
- Argon.
- Krypton.

They have relatively stable valence-shell configurations and generally exhibit low chemical reactivity.

## 4. Blocks of the Periodic Table

The periodic table is divided into four blocks according to the subshell into which the differentiating electron enters.

### 4.1 s-Block

The s-block consists mainly of Groups 1 and 2.

Characteristics:
- The differentiating electron enters an s orbital.
- General outer electronic configurations are ns¹ and ns².
- Includes highly reactive alkali and alkaline earth metals.

Helium has an s² configuration but is placed in Group 18 because of its noble gas properties.

### 4.2 p-Block

The p-block consists of Groups 13–18.

Characteristics:
- The differentiating electron enters a p orbital.
- Contains metals, non-metals, and metalloids.
- Includes halogens and noble gases.

General outer electronic configuration:

\[
ns^2np^{1-6}
\]

### 4.3 d-Block

The d-block consists of Groups 3–12.

Characteristics:
- The differentiating electron generally enters a d subshell.
- Contains transition elements.
- Many elements show variable oxidation states.
- Many form coloured ions and coordination compounds.

General configuration:

\[
(n-1)d^{1-10}ns^{0-2}
\]

### 4.4 f-Block

The f-block consists of the lanthanoids and actinoids.

Characteristics:
- The differentiating electron enters an f subshell.
- Many elements show similar chemical properties.
- Lanthanoids are associated with filling of the 4f subshell.
- Actinoids are associated with filling of the 5f subshell.

## 5. Electronic Configuration and Periodic Position

### 5.1 Relationship Between Period and Electronic Configuration

For many elements, the highest principal quantum number in the ground-state electronic configuration indicates the period.

Example:

Sodium:

\[
Na:1s^22s^22p^63s^1
\]

The highest principal quantum number is 3.

Therefore, sodium belongs to Period 3.

### 5.2 Relationship Between Group and Valence Electrons

For main-group elements, the number of valence electrons helps determine the group.

Examples:

- Group 1: One valence electron.
- Group 2: Two valence electrons.
- Group 13: Three valence electrons.
- Group 14: Four valence electrons.
- Group 15: Five valence electrons.
- Group 16: Six valence electrons.
- Group 17: Seven valence electrons.
- Group 18: Eight valence electrons, except helium with two.

### 5.3 Determining the Block

The subshell receiving the differentiating electron determines the block.

Examples:

- Sodium: s-block.
- Carbon: p-block.
- Iron: d-block.
- Cerium: f-block.

## 6. Atomic Radius

### 6.1 Definition

Atomic radius is a measure of the size of an atom.

Because the electron cloud has no sharp outer boundary, atomic radius is defined using specific experimental conventions.

Common types include:
- Covalent radius.
- Metallic radius.
- van der Waals radius.

### 6.2 Covalent Radius

The covalent radius is approximately half the distance between the nuclei of two identical atoms joined by a single covalent bond.

### 6.3 Metallic Radius

The metallic radius is approximately half the distance between the nuclei of neighbouring atoms in a metallic crystal.

### 6.4 van der Waals Radius

The van der Waals radius is related to the closest approach of non-bonded atoms.

### 6.5 Periodic Trend in Atomic Radius

Across a period from left to right:

\[
\boxed{\text{Atomic radius generally decreases}}
\]

Reason:
- Nuclear charge increases.
- Electrons are added to the same principal shell.
- Shielding does not increase enough to cancel the increase in nuclear attraction.
- Electrons are pulled closer to the nucleus.

Down a group:

\[
\boxed{\text{Atomic radius generally increases}}
\]

Reason:
- Additional electron shells are occupied.
- Shielding increases.
- Outer electrons are located farther from the nucleus.

## 7. Ionic Radius

### 7.1 Definition

Ionic radius is a measure of the effective size of an ion in an ionic structure.

### 7.2 Cations

Cations are generally smaller than their parent atoms.

Reasons:
- Electrons are removed.
- Electron-electron repulsion may decrease.
- The effective nuclear attraction per remaining electron generally increases.

Example:

\[
Na^+<Na
\]

### 7.3 Anions

Anions are generally larger than their parent atoms.

Reasons:
- Electrons are added.
- Electron-electron repulsion increases.
- The electron cloud expands.

Example:

\[
Cl^->Cl
\]

### 7.4 Isoelectronic Species

Isoelectronic species contain the same number of electrons.

For an isoelectronic series, ionic radius generally decreases as nuclear charge increases.

Example:

\[
O^{2-},\quad F^-,\quad Na^+,\quad Mg^{2+}
\]

All contain ten electrons.

Their radii generally follow:

\[
\boxed{O^{2-}>F^->Na^+>Mg^{2+}}
\]

This is because the nuclear charge increases from oxygen to magnesium while the electron count remains constant.

## 8. Ionization Enthalpy

### 8.1 Definition

Ionization enthalpy is the enthalpy change required to remove an electron from an isolated gaseous atom or ion.

The first ionization process is:

\[
X(g)\rightarrow X^+(g)+e^-
\]

The first ionization enthalpy is represented by:

\[
IE_1
\]

### 8.2 Successive Ionization Enthalpies

The removal of successive electrons requires additional energy.

\[
X(g)\rightarrow X^+(g)+e^-
\]

\[
X^+(g)\rightarrow X^{2+}(g)+e^-
\]

\[
X^{2+}(g)\rightarrow X^{3+}(g)+e^-
\]

Generally:

\[
\boxed{IE_1<IE_2<IE_3}
\]

The removal of each successive electron becomes more difficult because the ion becomes increasingly positively charged.

### 8.3 Periodic Trend

Across a period:

Ionization enthalpy generally increases.

Reasons:
- Effective nuclear charge generally increases.
- Atomic radius generally decreases.
- Electrons are held more strongly.

Down a group:

Ionization enthalpy generally decreases.

Reasons:
- Atomic radius increases.
- Shielding increases.
- The outermost electron is generally easier to remove.

### 8.4 Important Exceptions

The trend is not perfectly regular.

Examples include:
- Beryllium has a higher first ionization enthalpy than boron.
- Nitrogen has a higher first ionization enthalpy than oxygen.

These exceptions are related to subshell energies and electron-pairing effects.

## 9. Electron Gain Enthalpy

### 9.1 Definition

Electron gain enthalpy is the enthalpy change when an isolated gaseous atom gains an electron to form a gaseous anion.

\[
X(g)+e^-\rightarrow X^-(g)
\]

It is represented by:

\[
\Delta_{eg}H
\]

### 9.2 Sign Convention

When energy is released, electron gain enthalpy is negative.

When energy is absorbed, electron gain enthalpy is positive.

### 9.3 Periodic Trend

Across a period, electron gain enthalpy generally becomes more negative, although there are important exceptions.

Down a group, it generally becomes less negative, but the trend is not perfectly regular.

Halogens generally have strongly negative first electron gain enthalpies.

### 9.4 Important Exception: Chlorine and Fluorine

Chlorine has a more negative first electron gain enthalpy than fluorine.

The small size of fluorine produces significant electron-electron repulsion when an additional electron enters its compact 2p subshell.

## 10. Electronegativity

### 10.1 Definition

Electronegativity is the tendency of an atom in a chemical bond to attract the shared electron pair toward itself.

It is commonly expressed using the Pauling scale.

### 10.2 Periodic Trend

Across a period:

\[
\boxed{\text{Electronegativity generally increases}}
\]

Down a group:

\[
\boxed{\text{Electronegativity generally decreases}}
\]

### 10.3 Important Observations

- Fluorine is the most electronegative element.
- Caesium and francium are among the least electronegative elements on commonly used scales.
- Noble gases are often excluded from simple electronegativity comparisons.
- Electronegativity helps predict bond polarity.

## 11. Metallic and Non-Metallic Character

### 11.1 Metallic Character

Metallic character describes the tendency of an element to lose electrons and form positive ions.

Across a period:

\[
\boxed{\text{Metallic character generally decreases}}
\]

Down a group:

\[
\boxed{\text{Metallic character generally increases}}
\]

### 11.2 Non-Metallic Character

Non-metallic character describes the tendency of an element to gain or share electrons.

Across a period, non-metallic character generally increases.

Down a group, it generally decreases.

### 11.3 Metalloids

Metalloids show properties intermediate between metals and non-metals.

Examples:
- Boron.
- Silicon.
- Germanium.
- Arsenic.
- Antimony.

## 12. Valency and Oxidation States

### 12.1 Valency

Valency describes the combining capacity of an element in a compound.

For simple main-group elements, it can often be related to the number of electrons lost, gained, or shared during bonding.

### 12.2 Oxidation State

Oxidation state is the hypothetical charge assigned to an atom when bonding electrons are allocated according to specified rules.

Examples:
- Sodium in NaCl: +1.
- Chlorine in NaCl: -1.
- Oxygen in H₂O: -2.
- Hydrogen in H₂O: +1.

### 12.3 Variable Oxidation States

Many transition elements exhibit multiple oxidation states.

Examples:
- Iron: +2 and +3.
- Copper: +1 and +2.
- Manganese: several oxidation states.
- Chromium: +2, +3, and +6.

## 13. Periodic Trends: Summary

| Property | Across a period (left to right) | Down a group |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic radius | Generally decreases | Generally increases |
| Ionic radius | Depends on ion type and electron configuration | Generally increases for comparable ions |
| First ionization enthalpy | Generally increases | Generally decreases |
| Electron gain enthalpy | Generally becomes more negative, with exceptions | Generally becomes less negative, with exceptions |
| Electronegativity | Generally increases | Generally decreases |
| Metallic character | Generally decreases | Generally increases |
| Non-metallic character | Generally increases | Generally decreases |

## 14. Practical Applications

### 14.1 Predicting Chemical Reactivity

The periodic table helps predict which elements are likely to lose, gain, or share electrons.

### 14.2 Materials Science

Periodic trends help explain the properties of metals, semiconductors, and non-metals.

### 14.3 Industrial Chemistry

Knowledge of periodic properties helps select suitable elements and compounds for industrial reactions and materials.

### 14.4 Understanding Chemical Bonding

Electronegativity and electronic configuration help explain ionic, covalent, and polar bonds.

### 14.5 Environmental Chemistry

The periodic table helps understand the chemical behaviour, toxicity, and environmental distribution of different elements.

## 15. Common Exam Traps

1. The modern periodic law is based on atomic number, not atomic mass.
2. The modern periodic table contains 7 periods and 18 groups.
3. Periods are horizontal rows; groups are vertical columns.
4. The block is determined by the subshell receiving the differentiating electron.
5. Atomic radius generally decreases across a period and increases down a group.
6. Cations are generally smaller than their parent atoms.
7. Anions are generally larger than their parent atoms.
8. Isoelectronic species become smaller as nuclear charge increases.
9. Successive ionization enthalpies generally increase.
10. Ionization enthalpy generally increases across a period.
11. Chlorine has a more negative first electron gain enthalpy than fluorine.
12. Fluorine is the most electronegative element.
13. Metallic character generally increases down a group.
14. Periodic trends contain exceptions and should not be treated as perfectly uniform rules.
15. Noble gases generally have stable valence-shell configurations.
