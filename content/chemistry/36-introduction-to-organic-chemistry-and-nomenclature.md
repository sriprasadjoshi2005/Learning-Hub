# Lesson 36: Introduction to Organic Chemistry and Nomenclature

Organic chemistry is the study of carbon-containing compounds, particularly compounds containing carbon-hydrogen bonds and their derivatives.

Carbon forms an exceptionally large number of compounds because of its tetravalency, ability to form strong carbon-carbon bonds, and capacity to create chains, branches and rings.

This lesson introduces the structural basis of organic compounds, their classification, functional groups, homologous series, representations and systematic IUPAC nomenclature.

## 1. Why Carbon Forms So Many Compounds

### 1.1 Tetravalency

Carbon has atomic number 6.

Its electronic configuration is:

\[
1s^22s^22p^2
\]

It has four valence electrons and generally forms four covalent bonds.

This property is called tetravalency.

Examples:

\[
CH_4,\quad CH_3Cl,\quad CH_2O,\quad CO_2
\]

### 1.2 Catenation

Catenation is the ability of an element to form covalent bonds with atoms of the same element.

Carbon shows extensive catenation because carbon-carbon bonds are strong and stable.

It can form:

- Straight chains.
- Branched chains.
- Cyclic structures.
- Single, double and triple bonds.

### 1.3 Multiple Bond Formation

Carbon can form:

- Single bonds: \(C-C\)
- Double bonds: \(C=C\)
- Triple bonds: \(C\equiv C\)

This creates a large variety of molecular structures.

## 2. Classification of Organic Compounds

Organic compounds can be classified according to their carbon skeletons and functional groups.

### 2.1 Acyclic or Open-Chain Compounds

These contain carbon atoms arranged in straight or branched chains.

Examples:

\[
CH_3CH_2CH_3
\]

\[
CH_3CH(CH_3)CH_3
\]

### 2.2 Cyclic Compounds

These contain ring structures.

They may be:

- Homocyclic: rings containing only carbon atoms.
- Heterocyclic: rings containing carbon and other atoms.

Examples include cyclohexane, pyridine and furan.

### 2.3 Alicyclic Compounds

These are cyclic compounds that are not aromatic.

Cyclopropane and cyclohexane are examples.

### 2.4 Aromatic Compounds

Aromatic compounds contain a conjugated cyclic electron system satisfying the conditions for aromaticity.

Benzene is the simplest common example.

## 3. Functional Groups

A functional group is an atom or group of atoms responsible for characteristic chemical reactions of an organic compound.

| Functional group | General representation | Class |
|---|---|---|
| Hydroxyl | \(-OH\) | Alcohol |
| Aldehyde | \(-CHO\) | Aldehyde |
| Ketone | \(>C=O\) | Ketone |
| Carboxyl | \(-COOH\) | Carboxylic acid |
| Amino | \(-NH_2\) | Amine |
| Ether | \(-O-\) | Ether |
| Ester | \(-COO-\) | Ester |
| Halo | \(-X\) | Haloalkane |
| Nitrile | \(-C\equiv N\) | Nitrile |
| Amide | \(-CONH_2\) | Amide |

Functional groups strongly influence polarity, boiling point, solubility and chemical reactivity.

## 4. Homologous Series

A homologous series is a family of organic compounds having:

- The same functional group.
- The same general formula.
- Similar chemical properties.
- A gradual change in physical properties.
- Successive members differing by a \(-CH_2-\) unit.

Examples include alkanes:

\[
CH_4,\quad C_2H_6,\quad C_3H_8,\quad C_4H_{10}
\]

Their general formula is:

\[
C_nH_{2n+2}
\]

The molecular mass increases by approximately 14 units between successive members.

## 5. Representation of Organic Compounds

Organic molecules can be represented in different ways.

### 5.1 Molecular Formula

It gives the number of each type of atom.

Example:

\[
C_2H_6O
\]

### 5.2 Condensed Structural Formula

It shows groups of atoms in sequence.

Example:

\[
CH_3CH_2OH
\]

### 5.3 Expanded Structural Formula

It shows individual bonds between atoms.

### 5.4 Skeletal Formula

Carbon atoms are represented by line ends and vertices. Hydrogen atoms attached to carbon are usually omitted.

Heteroatoms and hydrogens attached to heteroatoms are shown explicitly.

Skeletal formulas make it easier to represent large organic structures.

## 6. IUPAC Nomenclature

The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry provides systematic rules for naming chemical compounds.

A basic IUPAC name generally contains:

\[
\text{Prefix}+\text{Word root}+\text{Primary suffix}+\text{Secondary suffix}
\]

The exact arrangement depends on the compound and the functional group.

### 6.1 Word Roots

The word root indicates the number of carbon atoms in the parent chain.

| Carbon atoms | Word root |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Meth |
| 2 | Eth |
| 3 | Prop |
| 4 | But |
| 5 | Pent |
| 6 | Hex |
| 7 | Hept |
| 8 | Oct |
| 9 | Non |
| 10 | Dec |

### 6.2 Primary Suffix

The primary suffix indicates the type of carbon-carbon bonding.

| Bond type | Suffix |
|---|---|
| Single | -ane |
| Double | -ene |
| Triple | -yne |

Examples:

\[
CH_4:\text{Methane}
\]

\[
CH_2=CH_2:\text{Ethene}
\]

\[
HC\equiv CH:\text{Ethyne}
\]

### 6.3 Choosing the Parent Chain

Select the longest suitable continuous carbon chain containing the principal functional group and as many multiple bonds as possible.

The parent chain should be numbered to give the lowest possible locants according to the applicable IUPAC priority rules.

### 6.4 Naming Substituents

Groups attached to the parent chain are named as prefixes.

Examples:

- \(-CH_3\): methyl
- \(-C_2H_5\): ethyl
- \(-Cl\): chloro
- \(-Br\): bromo

Repeated substituents use prefixes such as di-, tri- and tetra-.

Example:

\[
CH_3CH(CH_3)CH_3
\]

Name: 2-methylpropane.

### 6.5 Numbering the Chain

Number the parent chain from the end that gives the lowest locant to the principal characteristic group, then the multiple bonds and substituents according to the applicable priority rules.

Example:

\[
CH_3CH_2CH=CH_2
\]

Name: but-1-ene.

### 6.6 Naming Alcohols

The hydroxyl group is indicated by the suffix -ol when it is the principal functional group.

\[
CH_3CH_2OH
\]

Name: ethanol.

\[
CH_3CH(OH)CH_3
\]

Name: propan-2-ol.

### 6.7 Naming Aldehydes and Ketones

Aldehydes use the suffix -al.

\[
CH_3CHO
\]

Name: ethanal.

Ketones use the suffix -one.

\[
CH_3COCH_3
\]

Name: propanone.

### 6.8 Naming Carboxylic Acids

Carboxylic acids use the suffix -oic acid.

\[
CH_3COOH
\]

Name: ethanoic acid.

## 7. Basic Nomenclature Practice

| Structure | IUPAC name |
|---|---|
| \(CH_3CH_2CH_3\) | Propane |
| \(CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_3\) | Butane |
| \(CH_3CH(CH_3)CH_3\) | 2-Methylpropane |
| \(CH_2=CHCH_3\) | Prop-1-ene |
| \(CH_3C\equiv CH\) | Prop-1-yne |
| \(CH_3CH_2OH\) | Ethanol |
| \(CH_3COCH_3\) | Propanone |
| \(CH_3COOH\) | Ethanoic acid |

## 8. Common Mistakes and Exam Points

- Carbon is tetravalent and generally forms four covalent bonds.
- Catenation is the formation of bonds between atoms of the same element.
- A functional group determines characteristic chemical behaviour.
- Consecutive members of a homologous series differ by \(CH_2\).
- The longest suitable parent chain must be selected.
- Numbering should follow functional-group and multiple-bond priority rules.
- Skeletal formulas omit carbon symbols and most carbon-bound hydrogens.
- A molecular formula alone may not identify a unique structural compound.

## 9. Lesson Recap

- Organic chemistry studies carbon compounds.
- Tetravalency and catenation explain carbon's structural diversity.
- Organic compounds can be acyclic, cyclic, alicyclic or aromatic.
- Functional groups determine characteristic chemical behaviour.
- Homologous series contain related compounds differing successively by \(CH_2\).
- IUPAC nomenclature provides systematic names based on parent chains, substituents, bonding and functional groups.
