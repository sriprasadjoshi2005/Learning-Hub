# Lesson 18: Chemical Equations and Stoichiometry

Chemical reactions involve the rearrangement of atoms to form new substances. Chemical equations represent these reactions using chemical formulae and coefficients. Stoichiometry is the quantitative study of the relationships between reactants and products in balanced chemical equations.

It allows chemists to calculate the amount of reactants required, the amount of products formed, and the limiting reactant in a reaction. These calculations are essential in laboratory experiments, industrial production, and chemical manufacturing.

## 1. Chemical Equations

### 1.1 Definition

A chemical equation is a symbolic representation of a chemical reaction.

It shows:
- The reactants that undergo the reaction.
- The products that are formed.
- The relative quantities of substances involved.
- Relevant reaction conditions, when specified.

General representation:

\[
\text{Reactants}\rightarrow\text{Products}
\]

For example:

\[
2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O
\]

Hydrogen and oxygen react to form water.

### 1.2 Reactants and Products

**Reactants** are the starting substances consumed during a chemical reaction.

**Products** are the substances formed by the reaction.

In the equation:

\[
Zn+2HCl\rightarrow ZnCl_2+H_2
\]

Reactants:
- Zinc.
- Hydrochloric acid.

Products:
- Zinc chloride.
- Hydrogen gas.

### 1.3 Symbols Used in Chemical Equations

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| (s) | Solid |
| (l) | Liquid |
| (g) | Gas |
| (aq) | Aqueous solution |
| \(\rightarrow\) | Produces |
| \(\rightleftharpoons\) | Reversible reaction |
| \(\Delta\) | Heating |
| ↑ | Gas evolved |
| ↓ | Precipitate formed |

Example:

\[
CaCO_3(s)\xrightarrow{\Delta}CaO(s)+CO_2(g)
\]

Calcium carbonate decomposes on heating to form calcium oxide and carbon dioxide.

## 2. Balancing Chemical Equations

### 2.1 Law of Conservation of Mass

The law of conservation of mass states that mass is conserved during an ordinary chemical reaction.

Atoms are rearranged, but they are not created or destroyed.

Therefore, the number of atoms of each element must be equal on both sides of a balanced chemical equation.

### 2.2 Why Equations Must Be Balanced

Consider:

\[
H_2+O_2\rightarrow H_2O
\]

The left side contains:
- 2 hydrogen atoms.
- 2 oxygen atoms.

The right side contains:
- 2 hydrogen atoms.
- 1 oxygen atom.

The equation is not balanced.

The balanced equation is:

\[
\boxed{2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O}
\]

Both sides now contain:
- 4 hydrogen atoms.
- 2 oxygen atoms.

### 2.3 Rules for Balancing Equations

1. Write the correct chemical formulae for all reactants and products.
2. Count the atoms of each element on both sides.
3. Adjust coefficients to equalize the atom counts.
4. Do not change the subscripts in chemical formulae.
5. Reduce coefficients to the smallest whole-number ratio.
6. Verify that all atoms and charges are balanced when applicable.

### 2.4 Worked Example: Formation of Ammonia

Unbalanced equation:

\[
N_2+H_2\rightarrow NH_3
\]

**Step 1: Balance nitrogen.**

There are two nitrogen atoms on the left.

Place coefficient 2 before NH₃.

\[
N_2+H_2\rightarrow2NH_3
\]

**Step 2: Balance hydrogen.**

The right side contains six hydrogen atoms.

Place coefficient 3 before H₂.

\[
\boxed{N_2+3H_2\rightarrow2NH_3}
\]

The equation is balanced.

### 2.5 Balancing Equations with Polyatomic Ions

When a polyatomic ion remains unchanged on both sides, it can often be balanced as a single unit.

Example:

\[
Al_2(SO_4)_3+Ca(OH)_2
\rightarrow Al(OH)_3+CaSO_4
\]

Balanced equation:

\[
\boxed{Al_2(SO_4)_3+3Ca(OH)_2
\rightarrow2Al(OH)_3+3CaSO_4}
\]

The sulfate ion is treated as a unit because it remains unchanged.

## 3. Stoichiometry

### 3.1 Definition

Stoichiometry is the quantitative relationship between reactants and products in a balanced chemical equation.

The coefficients in a balanced equation represent mole ratios.

For example:

\[
2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O
\]

The equation indicates:

- 2 mol of hydrogen react with 1 mol of oxygen.
- 2 mol of water are produced.

The coefficients do not directly represent masses.

### 3.2 Mole Ratios

For the reaction:

\[
N_2+3H_2\rightarrow2NH_3
\]

The mole ratios are:

\[
N_2:H_2=1:3
\]

\[
N_2:NH_3=1:2
\]

\[
H_2:NH_3=3:2
\]

These ratios are used to calculate unknown quantities.

### 3.3 General Stoichiometric Relationship

For a reaction:

\[
aA+bB\rightarrow cC+dD
\]

The mole relationship is:

\[
\boxed{\frac{n_A}{a}=\frac{n_B}{b}=\frac{n_C}{c}=\frac{n_D}{d}}
\]

This relationship applies to the quantities participating in the balanced reaction.

## 4. Mass-to-Mass Stoichiometry

### 4.1 Calculation Procedure

To calculate the mass of a product from the mass of a reactant:

1. Balance the chemical equation.
2. Convert the given reactant mass into moles.
3. Use the balanced equation's mole ratio.
4. Convert the product moles into mass.

### 4.2 Worked Example

Calculate the mass of water produced when 4 g of hydrogen reacts completely with excess oxygen.

Balanced equation:

\[
2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O
\]

Molar masses:
- H₂ = 2 g mol⁻¹.
- H₂O = 18 g mol⁻¹.

**Step 1: Calculate moles of hydrogen.**

\[
n(H_2)=\frac{4}{2}=2\,mol
\]

**Step 2: Apply the mole ratio.**

From the balanced equation:

\[
2H_2:2H_2O
\]

Therefore:

\[
n(H_2O)=2\,mol
\]

**Step 3: Calculate the mass of water.**

\[
m=nM
\]

\[
m=2(18)
\]

\[
\boxed{m=36\,g}
\]

## 5. Limiting and Excess Reactants

### 5.1 Limiting Reactant

The limiting reactant is the reactant that is completely consumed first in a chemical reaction.

It determines the maximum amount of product that can form.

### 5.2 Excess Reactant

An excess reactant is present in a quantity greater than the amount required by the balanced equation.

Some of it remains after the limiting reactant is completely consumed.

### 5.3 Identifying the Limiting Reactant

For a balanced equation:

\[
aA+bB\rightarrow\text{Products}
\]

Calculate:

\[
\frac{n_A}{a}
\]

and:

\[
\frac{n_B}{b}
\]

The reactant with the smaller value is the limiting reactant.

This method assumes the reaction proceeds according to the balanced equation and that the reactants are available for reaction.

### 5.4 Worked Example

Consider:

\[
2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O
\]

Suppose we have:
- 3 mol H₂.
- 2 mol O₂.

Calculate:

\[
\frac{n(H_2)}{2}=\frac{3}{2}=1.5
\]

\[
\frac{n(O_2)}{1}=\frac{2}{1}=2
\]

The smaller value is 1.5.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{H_2\text{ is the limiting reactant}}
\]

### 5.5 Calculating Product from the Limiting Reactant

From the equation:

\[
2H_2\rightarrow2H_2O
\]

Two moles of hydrogen produce two moles of water.

Therefore, 3 mol H₂ produce:

\[
\boxed{3\,mol\ H_2O}
\]

### 5.6 Calculating Excess Reactant Remaining

The equation requires:

\[
2\,mol\ H_2:1\,mol\ O_2
\]

For 3 mol H₂, oxygen required is:

\[
n(O_2)=\frac{3}{2}=1.5\,mol
\]

Initial oxygen:

\[
2\,mol
\]

Oxygen remaining:

\[
2-1.5=0.5\,mol
\]

Therefore, 0.5 mol of oxygen remains unreacted.

## 6. Stoichiometry Involving Gases

For gases at the same temperature and pressure, their volumes are proportional to their mole quantities.

This follows from Avogadro's law.

For example:

\[
N_2+3H_2\rightarrow2NH_3
\]

At the same temperature and pressure, the reacting gas-volume ratio is:

\[
1:3:2
\]

Thus, one volume of nitrogen reacts with three volumes of hydrogen to form two volumes of ammonia gas, assuming ideal-gas behaviour and suitable reaction conditions.

## 7. Ionic Equations and Net Ionic Equations

### 7.1 Complete Ionic Equation

In aqueous solutions, soluble ionic compounds can be represented as separate ions.

For example:

\[
AgNO_3(aq)+NaCl(aq)\rightarrow AgCl(s)+NaNO_3(aq)
\]

Complete ionic equation:

\[
Ag^++NO_3^-+Na^++Cl^-
\rightarrow AgCl(s)+Na^++NO_3^-
\]

### 7.2 Spectator Ions

Spectator ions are ions that remain unchanged during the reaction.

In the above reaction:
- Na⁺ is a spectator ion.
- NO₃⁻ is a spectator ion.

### 7.3 Net Ionic Equation

Removing the spectator ions gives:

\[
\boxed{Ag^+(aq)+Cl^-(aq)\rightarrow AgCl(s)}
\]

The net ionic equation shows only the particles directly involved in the chemical change.

## 8. Applications of Stoichiometry

### 8.1 Industrial Production

Stoichiometry determines the quantities of raw materials required to manufacture chemicals.

### 8.2 Pharmaceutical Manufacturing

Accurate reactant quantities are important for preparing medicines and chemical formulations.

### 8.3 Environmental Chemistry

Stoichiometric calculations help estimate emissions, pollutant formation, and neutralization requirements.

### 8.4 Laboratory Analysis

Stoichiometry is essential in titrations, precipitation reactions, and quantitative chemical analysis.

## 9. Common Exam Traps

1. **Never balance an equation by changing subscripts.** Change coefficients only.
2. **Coefficients represent mole ratios, not mass ratios.**
3. **Always balance the equation before performing stoichiometric calculations.**
4. **The limiting reactant determines the theoretical amount of product.**
5. **An excess reactant is not necessarily completely consumed.**
6. **Use the limiting reactant when calculating the maximum product quantity.**
7. **Gas-volume ratios apply to gases measured at the same temperature and pressure.**
8. **Spectator ions are omitted from net ionic equations.**
9. **Check both atom counts and total charge when balancing ionic equations.**
