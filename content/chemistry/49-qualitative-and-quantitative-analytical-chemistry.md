# Lesson 49: Qualitative and Quantitative Analytical Chemistry

Analytical chemistry develops methods for identifying substances and determining how much of each substance is present. Qualitative analysis identifies chemical species, while quantitative analysis measures their amounts or concentrations.

## 1. Types of Chemical Analysis

### 1.1 Qualitative Analysis

Qualitative analysis answers:

- Which ions are present?
- Which functional group is present?
- Is a particular element or compound present?

Examples include flame tests, precipitation reactions, and functional-group tests.

### 1.2 Quantitative Analysis

Quantitative analysis answers:

- How much substance is present?
- What is the concentration of a solution?
- What percentage of a sample is a particular component?

Examples include titration, gravimetric analysis, and instrumental measurements.

## 2. Sampling and Sample Preparation

Reliable analysis begins with a representative sample.

Important steps include:
1. Selecting a representative portion.
2. Avoiding contamination.
3. Homogenising the sample when required.
4. Dissolving or extracting the analyte.
5. Removing interfering substances.
6. Performing calibration and measurement.

An analyte is the substance being measured.

A matrix is the other material present in the sample.

## 3. Qualitative Analysis of Cations

Cations can be identified through characteristic precipitation reactions.

### 3.1 Flame Tests

Some metal ions produce characteristic flame colours.

| Ion | Typical flame colour |
|---|---|
| Na⁺ | Intense yellow |
| K⁺ | Lilac |
| Ca²⁺ | Brick red |
| Ba²⁺ | Apple green |
| Cu²⁺ | Blue-green |

Flame tests are useful screening methods, but they may not provide definitive identification in complex mixtures.

### 3.2 Precipitation Reactions

A precipitate is an insoluble solid formed when suitable ions react in solution.

Examples:

\[
Ag^++Cl^-\rightarrow AgCl(s)
\]

Silver chloride is a white precipitate.

\[
Ba^{2+}+SO_4^{2-}\rightarrow BaSO_4(s)
\]

Barium sulfate is a white precipitate.

\[
Cu^{2+}+2OH^-\rightarrow Cu(OH)_2(s)
\]

Copper(II) hydroxide is a blue precipitate.

## 4. Qualitative Analysis of Anions

### 4.1 Carbonate Ions

Carbonates react with dilute acids to release carbon dioxide.

\[
CO_3^{2-}+2H^+\rightarrow CO_2+H_2O
\]

The gas turns limewater milky due to calcium carbonate formation.

\[
CO_2+Ca(OH)_2\rightarrow CaCO_3+H_2O
\]

### 4.2 Sulfate Ions

Sulfate ions produce a white barium sulfate precipitate with suitable barium salt solutions.

\[
Ba^{2+}+SO_4^{2-}\rightarrow BaSO_4(s)
\]

Acidification before testing helps reduce interference from carbonate ions.

### 4.3 Halide Ions

Acidified silver nitrate can distinguish common halide ions.

| Ion | Silver salt | Appearance |
|---|---|---|
| Cl⁻ | AgCl | White |
| Br⁻ | AgBr | Cream |
| I⁻ | AgI | Yellow |

The precipitates have different solubilities in aqueous ammonia.

- AgCl dissolves in dilute ammonia.
- AgBr dissolves in concentrated ammonia more readily than in dilute ammonia.
- AgI is insoluble in ammonia.

## 5. Identification of Selected Metal Ions

Some hydroxide precipitates show characteristic colours and behaviour in excess alkali.

| Ion | Hydroxide precipitate | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Cu²⁺ | Blue | Generally insoluble in excess NaOH |
| Fe²⁺ | Green | Generally insoluble in excess NaOH |
| Fe³⁺ | Brown | Generally insoluble in excess NaOH |
| Al³⁺ | White | Dissolves in excess NaOH |
| Zn²⁺ | White | Dissolves in excess NaOH |

These tests should be interpreted together with other observations because mixtures and interfering ions can complicate identification.

## 6. Volumetric Analysis

Volumetric analysis measures the volume of a solution required to react with a known amount of another substance.

Titration is a common volumetric method.

### 6.1 Important Terms

- Titrant: solution delivered from the burette.
- Analyte: substance being analysed.
- Indicator: substance that changes colour near the endpoint.
- Endpoint: observed point at which the indicator changes.
- Equivalence point: point at which the reacting substances are present in stoichiometrically equivalent amounts.

The endpoint should be close to the equivalence point.

### 6.2 Acid–Base Titration

For a monoprotic acid reacting with a monobasic base:

\[
HA+BOH\rightarrow BA+H_2O
\]

At equivalence:

\[
n(HA)=n(BOH)
\]

For general reactions, use the balanced equation and its stoichiometric coefficients rather than assuming equal mole amounts.

### 6.3 Titration Calculation

Suppose 25.0 mL of hydrochloric acid is neutralised by 20.0 mL of 0.100 mol L⁻¹ sodium hydroxide.

Reaction:

\[
HCl+NaOH\rightarrow NaCl+H_2O
\]

Moles of sodium hydroxide:

\[
n=CV
\]

\[
n=0.100\times0.0200
=0.00200\ mol
\]

The mole ratio is 1:1, so the acid also contains 0.00200 mol.

Concentration of acid:

\[
C=\frac{0.00200}{0.0250}
\]

\[
\boxed{C=0.0800\ mol\,L^{-1}}
\]

## 7. Indicators

Indicators are selected according to the pH change around the equivalence point.

| Indicator | Approximate transition range |
|---|---|
| Methyl orange | pH 3.1–4.4 |
| Methyl red | pH 4.4–6.2 |
| Bromothymol blue | pH 6.0–7.6 |
| Phenolphthalein | pH 8.2–10.0 |

The best indicator is one whose transition range lies within the steep pH change near the equivalence point.

## 8. Redox Titrations

Redox titrations are based on electron-transfer reactions.

### 8.1 Permanganate Titration

In acidic solution, permanganate ions are reduced to manganese(II) ions.

\[
MnO_4^-+8H^++5e^-\rightarrow Mn^{2+}+4H_2O
\]

Potassium permanganate is often self-indicating because its purple colour disappears when it is reduced.

A faint permanent pink colour can indicate the endpoint when permanganate is used as the titrant.

### 8.2 Iodometric Methods

Iodine can be titrated using sodium thiosulfate.

\[
I_2+2S_2O_3^{2-}\rightarrow2I^-+S_4O_6^{2-}
\]

Starch is used as an indicator because it forms a deep blue complex with iodine.

## 9. Gravimetric Analysis

Gravimetric analysis determines the amount of an analyte by converting it into a compound of known composition and measuring its mass.

General procedure:
1. Dissolve the sample.
2. Add a suitable precipitating reagent.
3. Allow the precipitate to form.
4. Filter and wash the precipitate.
5. Dry or ignite it as required.
6. Weigh it accurately.
7. Calculate the analyte amount using stoichiometry.

### 9.1 Example

Suppose sulfate ions are precipitated as barium sulfate.

\[
Ba^{2+}+SO_4^{2-}\rightarrow BaSO_4(s)
\]

If 2.33 g of BaSO₄ is obtained:

\[
n(BaSO_4)=\frac{2.33}{233.4}
\approx0.00998\ mol
\]

The mole ratio of sulfate to barium sulfate is 1:1.

\[
n(SO_4^{2-})\approx0.00998\ mol
\]

## 10. Accuracy, Precision and Errors

### 10.1 Accuracy

Accuracy describes how close a measured result is to the true or accepted value.

### 10.2 Precision

Precision describes the agreement among repeated measurements.

### 10.3 Systematic Errors

Systematic errors shift measurements consistently in one direction.

Examples:
- Incorrectly calibrated equipment.
- Consistent parallax error.
- An indicator that changes outside the ideal endpoint range.

### 10.4 Random Errors

Random errors cause unpredictable variations.

Examples:
- Small reading variations.
- Uncontrolled fluctuations in temperature.
- Minor differences in endpoint judgement.

## 11. Significant Figures and Uncertainty

Results should reflect the precision of the measurements.

For multiplication and division, the result is generally reported to the number of significant figures of the least precise input.

For addition and subtraction, the result is generally reported to the least precise decimal place.

Percentage error relative to an accepted value is:

\[
\%\text{ error}=
\frac{|\text{measured}-\text{accepted}|}
{|\text{accepted}|}\times100
\]

## 12. Common Exam Traps

- Endpoint and equivalence point are related but not identical.
- Titration calculations must use the balanced chemical equation.
- Equal volumes do not necessarily mean equal mole amounts.
- Flame colours are useful clues but may be affected by contamination.
- Silver chloride, bromide, and iodide have different colours and ammonia solubilities.
- Gravimetric analysis depends on accurate precipitation, washing, drying, and weighing.
- Accuracy and precision are different concepts.
- Significant figures must reflect measurement precision.

## 13. Summary

- Qualitative analysis identifies chemical species.
- Quantitative analysis determines amounts and concentrations.
- Precipitation and flame tests help identify ions.
- Titration uses reaction stoichiometry to determine concentration.
- Gravimetric analysis determines composition through mass measurement.
- Calibration, uncertainty, and significant figures are essential for reliable results.
