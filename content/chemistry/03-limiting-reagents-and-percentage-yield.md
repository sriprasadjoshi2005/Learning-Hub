title: Limiting Reagents and Percentage Yield
level: foundation
---

A balanced equation gives reacting amounts in **moles**, never in grams. In $\mathrm{N_2 + 3H_2 \rightarrow 2NH_3}$, one mole of nitrogen needs three moles of hydrogen and produces two moles of ammonia.

## Method for any stoichiometry problem

1. Write and balance the equation.
2. Convert each given mass to moles with $n = m/M$.
3. Compare the moles with the equation's ratio to find the **limiting reagent**, the one that runs out first.
4. Use the limiting reagent's moles and the mole ratio to find the product's moles, then convert to grams.

The limiting reagent is **not** simply the one with the smaller mass, and not the one with the smaller number of moles either. It is the one that is scarcest *relative to what the equation demands*. The other reagent is **in excess**, and some of it is left over.

## Percentage yield

The **theoretical yield** is the mass of product predicted by the calculation, assuming the reaction goes to completion with no losses. In practice, reactions are incomplete, side reactions occur, and product is lost during separation. The **actual yield** is what you really collect:

$$\text{percentage yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100\%$$

A percentage yield above 100% signals an error, usually an impure or wet product, or an arithmetic slip.

## Worked pattern

For $\mathrm{CaCO_3 \rightarrow CaO + CO_2}$, the mole ratio is $1:1:1$. Heating 1.00 mol of $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$ (100.09 g) can give at most 1.00 mol of $\mathrm{CaO}$ (56.08 g), so the theoretical yield is $56.08$ g, whatever the heating time.

## Quiz

1. $28.0$ g of $\mathrm{N_2}$ reacts with $8.00$ g of $\mathrm{H_2}$ to form ammonia ($M$: $\mathrm{N_2}$ 28.02, $\mathrm{H_2}$ 2.016, $\mathrm{NH_3}$ 17.04 g mol$^{-1}$). What mass of $\mathrm{NH_3}$ can form?
   - [x] 34.1 g
   - [ ] 17.0 g
   - [ ] 45.1 g
   - [ ] 68.1 g
   > Moles: $\mathrm{N_2}$ $=1.00$, $\mathrm{H_2}$ $=3.97$. One mole of $\mathrm{N_2}$ needs only $3.00$ mol of $\mathrm{H_2}$, so $\mathrm{N_2}$ is limiting. It gives $2.00$ mol of $\mathrm{NH_3}$, which is $34.1$ g. The value 45.1 g wrongly treats hydrogen as limiting.

2. $50.0$ g of $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$ ($M=100.09$) is heated until it decomposes to $\mathrm{CaO}$ ($M=56.08$) and $\mathrm{CO_2}$. Only $24.1$ g of $\mathrm{CaO}$ is collected. What is the percentage yield?
   - [ ] 48.2%
   - [ ] 92.0%
   - [ ] 116%
   - [x] 86.0%
   > Theoretical: $(50.0/100.09)\times56.08=28.0$ g. Percentage yield $=24.1/28.0\times100\approx86.0\%$. The value 48.2% divides by the mass of the starting material, which is not the theoretical yield of the product.

3. Which statement about a limiting reagent is correct?
   - [ ] It is always the reactant with the smaller mass.
   - [ ] It is always the reactant with the smaller number of moles.
   - [x] It is the reactant that would produce the smaller amount of product.
   - [ ] It is the reactant with the smaller molar mass.
   > Compare each reactant's moles with the equation's ratio. Whichever supports the least product runs out first. Mass and raw mole counts alone can be misleading because the coefficients matter.
