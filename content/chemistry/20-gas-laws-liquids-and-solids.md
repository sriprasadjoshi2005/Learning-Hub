# Lesson 20: Gas Laws, Liquids and Solids

Matter exists in different physical states depending on temperature, pressure, and the nature of the particles and their interactions. Gases have no fixed shape or volume, liquids have a fixed volume but no fixed shape, and solids generally have both fixed shape and fixed volume.

The behaviour of gases can be described using gas laws and kinetic molecular theory. Liquids are characterized by properties such as viscosity, surface tension, and vapour pressure. Solids are classified according to their internal particle arrangement, including crystalline and amorphous structures.

## 1. The Gaseous State

### 1.1 General Characteristics

Gases have the following properties:

- No fixed shape.
- No fixed volume.
- High compressibility.
- Low density compared with liquids and solids.
- Rapid molecular motion.
- Ability to diffuse and mix.
- Pressure exerted on the walls of their container.

Gas particles are widely separated compared with their molecular sizes under ordinary low-pressure conditions.

### 1.2 Gas Pressure

Gas pressure is the force exerted per unit area by gas particles colliding with the walls of a container.

\[
\boxed{P=\frac{F}{A}}
\]

SI unit:

\[
Pa=N\,m^{-2}
\]

Other commonly used units include:
- Atmosphere (atm).
- Bar.
- Millimetres of mercury (mmHg).
- Torr.

Useful conversions:

\[
1\,atm=101325\,Pa
\]

\[
1\,atm=760\,mmHg
\]

\[
1\,bar=10^5\,Pa
\]

## 2. Boyle's Law

### 2.1 Statement

Boyle's law states that for a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature, pressure is inversely proportional to volume.

\[
P\propto\frac{1}{V}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{PV=\text{constant}}
\]

For two states:

\[
\boxed{P_1V_1=P_2V_2}
\]

### 2.2 Explanation

When a gas is compressed at constant temperature:

- The volume decreases.
- Gas particles collide with the container walls more frequently per unit area.
- Pressure increases.

### 2.3 Graph

A graph of pressure against volume is a decreasing curve called a rectangular hyperbola.

A graph of pressure against reciprocal volume is a straight line through the origin.

### 2.4 Worked Example

A gas occupies 4 L at a pressure of 2 atm. It is compressed to 2 L at constant temperature.

Calculate the final pressure.

\[
P_1V_1=P_2V_2
\]

\[
2(4)=P_2(2)
\]

\[
P_2=\frac{8}{2}
\]

\[
\boxed{P_2=4\,atm}
\]

## 3. Charles's Law

### 3.1 Statement

Charles's law states that for a fixed amount of gas at constant pressure, volume is directly proportional to its absolute temperature.

\[
V\propto T
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\frac{V}{T}=\text{constant}}
\]

For two states:

\[
\boxed{\frac{V_1}{T_1}=\frac{V_2}{T_2}}
\]

Temperature must be expressed in kelvin.

### 3.2 Celsius and Kelvin

The relationship between Celsius and Kelvin temperature is:

\[
\boxed{T(K)=t(^\circ C)+273.15}
\]

For most introductory calculations, 273 may be used as an approximation.

### 3.3 Explanation

When a gas is heated at constant pressure:
- The average kinetic energy of its particles increases.
- The particles move faster.
- The gas expands until the pressure returns to the specified constant value.

### 3.4 Worked Example

A gas occupies 2 L at 300 K. Calculate its volume at 450 K if pressure remains constant.

\[
\frac{V_1}{T_1}=\frac{V_2}{T_2}
\]

\[
\frac{2}{300}=\frac{V_2}{450}
\]

\[
V_2=\frac{2(450)}{300}
\]

\[
\boxed{V_2=3\,L}
\]

## 4. Gay-Lussac's Pressure Law

### 4.1 Statement

For a fixed amount of gas at constant volume, pressure is directly proportional to absolute temperature.

\[
P\propto T
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\frac{P}{T}=\text{constant}}
\]

For two states:

\[
\boxed{\frac{P_1}{T_1}=\frac{P_2}{T_2}}
\]

### 4.2 Explanation

When a gas is heated in a rigid container:
- The average kinetic energy increases.
- Particles move faster.
- Collisions with the container walls become more forceful.
- Pressure increases.

### 4.3 Worked Example

A gas has a pressure of 1 atm at 300 K. Calculate its pressure at 600 K if volume remains constant.

\[
\frac{P_1}{T_1}=\frac{P_2}{T_2}
\]

\[
\frac{1}{300}=\frac{P_2}{600}
\]

\[
\boxed{P_2=2\,atm}
\]

## 5. Avogadro's Law

### 5.1 Statement

Avogadro's law states that equal volumes of all ideal gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.

For a fixed temperature and pressure:

\[
V\propto n
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\frac{V}{n}=\text{constant}}
\]

For two states:

\[
\boxed{\frac{V_1}{n_1}=\frac{V_2}{n_2}}
\]

### 5.2 Explanation

Increasing the number of moles of gas increases the number of particles.

At constant temperature and pressure, the volume must increase proportionally to accommodate the additional particles.

## 6. Combined Gas Law

The combined gas law combines Boyle's law, Charles's law, and Gay-Lussac's law for a fixed amount of gas.

\[
\boxed{\frac{P_1V_1}{T_1}=\frac{P_2V_2}{T_2}}
\]

This equation is useful when pressure, volume, and temperature all change.

The amount of gas must remain constant.

### 6.1 Worked Example

A gas occupies 3 L at 2 atm and 300 K. Calculate its volume at 1 atm and 400 K.

\[
\frac{P_1V_1}{T_1}=\frac{P_2V_2}{T_2}
\]

Substitute:

\[
\frac{2(3)}{300}=\frac{1(V_2)}{400}
\]

Rearranging:

\[
V_2=\frac{2(3)(400)}{300}
\]

\[
\boxed{V_2=8\,L}
\]

## 7. Ideal Gas Equation

### 7.1 Derivation

Boyle's law:

\[
V\propto\frac{1}{P}
\]

Charles's law:

\[
V\propto T
\]

Avogadro's law:

\[
V\propto n
\]

Combining these relationships:

\[
V\propto\frac{nT}{P}
\]

Introducing a proportionality constant \(R\):

\[
V=\frac{nRT}{P}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{PV=nRT}
\]

This is the ideal gas equation.

### 7.2 Meaning of the Terms

- \(P\) = Pressure.
- \(V\) = Volume.
- \(n\) = Number of moles.
- \(R\) = Universal gas constant.
- \(T\) = Absolute temperature.

Common value of the gas constant:

\[
R=8.314\,J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}
\]

Another commonly used value is:

\[
R=0.082057\,L\,atm\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}
\]

The value and units of \(R\) must match the units used for pressure and volume.

### 7.3 Worked Example

Calculate the volume occupied by 2 mol of an ideal gas at 300 K and 1 atm.

Use:

\[
R=0.082057\,L\,atm\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}
\]

\[
PV=nRT
\]

\[
V=\frac{nRT}{P}
\]

\[
V=\frac{2(0.082057)(300)}{1}
\]

\[
\boxed{V\approx49.23\,L}
\]

## 8. Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures

### 8.1 Statement

Dalton's law states that the total pressure exerted by a mixture of non-reacting gases is equal to the sum of the partial pressures of the individual gases.

\[
\boxed{P_{\text{total}}=P_1+P_2+\cdots+P_n}
\]

### 8.2 Partial Pressure

The partial pressure of a gas is the pressure it would exert if it alone occupied the entire volume of the mixture at the same temperature.

For an ideal-gas mixture:

\[
\boxed{P_i=X_iP_{\text{total}}}
\]

Where:
- \(P_i\) = Partial pressure of gas i.
- \(X_i\) = Mole fraction of gas i.
- \(P_{\text{total}}\) = Total pressure.

### 8.3 Worked Example

A gas mixture contains:
- Nitrogen partial pressure = 0.7 atm.
- Oxygen partial pressure = 0.2 atm.
- Argon partial pressure = 0.1 atm.

Total pressure:

\[
P_{\text{total}}=0.7+0.2+0.1
\]

\[
\boxed{P_{\text{total}}=1.0\,atm}
\]

## 9. Kinetic Molecular Theory of Gases

### 9.1 Main Assumptions

The kinetic molecular theory explains the macroscopic behaviour of gases using the motion of individual particles.

The ideal-gas model assumes:

1. A gas consists of a very large number of particles.
2. The particles are in continuous random motion.
3. The volume of individual particles is negligible compared with the container volume.
4. Intermolecular forces are negligible except during collisions.
5. Collisions between particles and container walls are perfectly elastic.
6. The average translational kinetic energy is proportional to absolute temperature.

### 9.2 Molecular Kinetic Energy

The average translational kinetic energy per molecule is:

\[
\boxed{\overline{E_k}=\frac{3}{2}k_BT}
\]

Where:
- \(k_B\) = Boltzmann constant.
- \(T\) = Absolute temperature.

For one mole of gas:

\[
\boxed{\overline{E_k}=\frac{3}{2}RT}
\]

This shows that average translational kinetic energy depends only on absolute temperature for an ideal gas.

### 9.3 Root Mean Square Speed

The root mean square speed is:

\[
\boxed{u_{rms}=\sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}}
\]

Where:
- \(R\) = Universal gas constant.
- \(T\) = Absolute temperature.
- \(M\) = Molar mass in kg mol⁻¹.

At the same temperature, lighter gas molecules have higher root mean square speeds.

### 9.4 Effect of Temperature

Increasing temperature increases the average kinetic energy of gas particles.

Consequently:
- Molecular speeds increase.
- Gas diffusion generally becomes faster.
- Pressure increases at constant volume.
- Volume increases at constant pressure.

## 10. Real Gases

### 10.1 Why Real Gases Deviate from Ideal Behaviour

Real gas particles have:
- Finite molecular volumes.
- Intermolecular attractions and repulsions.

The ideal-gas model neglects these effects.

Real gases therefore deviate from ideal behaviour, particularly at high pressure and low temperature.

### 10.2 Conditions for Nearly Ideal Behaviour

Real gases behave more ideally when:
- Pressure is low.
- Temperature is high.
- Particles are far apart.
- Intermolecular attractions are relatively unimportant.

### 10.3 Compressibility Factor

The compressibility factor is:

\[
\boxed{Z=\frac{PV}{nRT}}
\]

For an ideal gas:

\[
Z=1
\]

For real gases:
- \(Z<1\) indicates that attractive interactions dominate under the specified conditions.
- \(Z>1\) indicates that repulsive or excluded-volume effects dominate.

### 10.4 van der Waals Equation

The van der Waals equation modifies the ideal gas equation to account approximately for molecular attractions and finite molecular volume.

\[
\boxed{\left(P+\frac{an^2}{V^2}\right)(V-nb)=nRT}
\]

Where:
- \(a\) corrects for intermolecular attractions.
- \(b\) corrects for the finite volume occupied by gas particles.
- \(n\) = Number of moles.
- \(V\) = Volume.
- \(P\) = Pressure.
- \(T\) = Absolute temperature.

The equation is more realistic than the ideal-gas equation for many real gases, although it remains an approximate model.

## 11. The Liquid State

### 11.1 General Characteristics

Liquids have:
- Definite volume.
- No fixed shape.
- Relatively high density compared with gases.
- Limited compressibility.
- Particles that remain close together but can move past one another.

Intermolecular forces play an important role in determining liquid properties.

### 11.2 Vapour Pressure

Vapour pressure is the pressure exerted by a vapour in equilibrium with its liquid in a closed system at a specified temperature.

When a liquid is placed in a closed container:
- Some molecules escape into the vapour phase.
- Some vapour molecules return to the liquid.
- Eventually, a dynamic equilibrium may be established.

At equilibrium, the rates of evaporation and condensation are equal.

### 11.3 Factors Affecting Vapour Pressure

**1. Temperature**

Vapour pressure increases as temperature increases because a greater fraction of molecules can escape into the vapour phase.

**2. Intermolecular forces**

Stronger intermolecular attractions generally lower the vapour pressure at a given temperature.

**3. Nature of the liquid**

Different liquids have different vapour pressures because of differences in molecular structure and intermolecular interactions.

### 11.4 Boiling Point

A liquid boils when its vapour pressure becomes equal to the external pressure.

\[
\boxed{P_{\text{vapour}}=P_{\text{external}}}
\]

The normal boiling point is the temperature at which the vapour pressure equals one standard atmosphere.

A liquid boils at a lower temperature when the external pressure is reduced.

### 11.5 Viscosity

Viscosity is the resistance of a liquid to flow.

It is influenced by:
- Intermolecular forces.
- Molecular size and shape.
- Temperature.

For many liquids, viscosity decreases as temperature increases.

### 11.6 Surface Tension

Surface tension is the energy required to increase the surface area of a liquid per unit area.

It arises from the cohesive attractions between liquid molecules.

Strong cohesive forces generally produce higher surface tension.

### 11.7 Capillary Action

Capillary action is the movement of a liquid through a narrow tube or porous material due to surface tension and the balance of adhesive and cohesive forces.

Water rises in a narrow glass tube because water-glass adhesion can exceed the cohesive attraction between water molecules.

Mercury behaves differently in glass because its cohesive interactions are stronger than its adhesive interactions with glass.

## 12. The Solid State

### 12.1 General Characteristics

Solids generally have:
- Definite shape.
- Definite volume.
- High density.
- Very low compressibility.
- Particles arranged closely together.

Particles in a solid vibrate about relatively fixed positions rather than moving freely throughout the material.

### 12.2 Crystalline Solids

Crystalline solids have a regular, repeating arrangement of particles over long distances.

Examples:
- Sodium chloride.
- Quartz.
- Diamond.
- Many metals.

Characteristics:
- Long-range structural order.
- Definite geometrical arrangement.
- Usually a sharp melting temperature for pure crystalline substances.
- Anisotropic properties in many crystals.

Anisotropy means that certain physical properties vary with direction.

### 12.3 Amorphous Solids

Amorphous solids lack long-range periodic order.

Examples:
- Glass.
- Many plastics.
- Some forms of rubber.

Characteristics:
- No regular long-range arrangement.
- Soften over a range of temperatures rather than having a sharp melting point.
- Generally isotropic at the macroscopic scale.

Isotropy means that physical properties are approximately the same in all directions.

### 12.4 Comparison

| Feature | Crystalline solids | Amorphous solids |
|---|---|---|
| Particle arrangement | Long-range regular order | No long-range periodic order |
| Melting behaviour | Usually sharp melting point | Soften over a temperature range |
| Shape | Definite geometrical arrangement | No regular crystal geometry |
| Directional properties | Often anisotropic | Generally isotropic |
| Examples | NaCl, quartz | Glass, many plastics |

## 13. Classification of Crystalline Solids

### 13.1 Ionic Solids

Ionic solids contain cations and anions held together by electrostatic attractions.

Examples:
- NaCl.
- MgO.
- CaF₂.

Properties:
- Generally high melting points.
- Brittle.
- Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved if mobile ions are present.

### 13.2 Molecular Solids

Molecular solids consist of molecules held together by intermolecular forces.

Examples:
- Ice.
- Solid iodine.
- Dry ice.

Properties:
- Generally lower melting points than ionic or network covalent solids.
- Often poor electrical conductors.
- Mechanical properties depend on the intermolecular interactions.

### 13.3 Covalent Network Solids

Covalent network solids contain atoms connected through an extended network of covalent bonds.

Examples:
- Diamond.
- Silicon carbide.
- Silicon dioxide.

Properties:
- Generally high hardness.
- High melting temperatures.
- Usually poor electrical conductivity, with important exceptions such as graphite.

### 13.4 Metallic Solids

Metallic solids consist of metal atoms arranged in a lattice with delocalized electrons.

Examples:
- Copper.
- Iron.
- Aluminium.

Properties:
- Good electrical conductivity.
- Good thermal conductivity.
- Malleability.
- Ductility.
- Metallic lustre.

## 14. Crystal Lattices and Unit Cells

### 14.1 Crystal Lattice

A crystal lattice is a regular three-dimensional arrangement of points representing the repeating positions of particles in a crystalline material.

The smallest repeating unit that reproduces the crystal structure through translation is called the unit cell.

### 14.2 Unit Cell

A unit cell is described by:
- Edge lengths \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\).
- Interaxial angles \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), and \(\gamma\).

Repeating the unit cell in three dimensions generates the crystal lattice.

### 14.3 Cubic Unit Cells

The three common cubic unit cells are:

1. Simple cubic.
2. Body-centred cubic.
3. Face-centred cubic.

**Simple Cubic (SC)**

Atoms are located at the eight corners of a cube.

Each corner atom contributes one-eighth to the unit cell.

\[
Z=8\left(\frac18\right)
\]

\[
\boxed{Z=1}
\]

**Body-Centred Cubic (BCC)**

Atoms occupy the eight corners and one position at the centre of the cube.

\[
Z=8\left(\frac18\right)+1
\]

\[
\boxed{Z=2}
\]

**Face-Centred Cubic (FCC)**

Atoms occupy the eight corners and the centres of the six faces.

\[
Z=8\left(\frac18\right)+6\left(\frac12\right)
\]

\[
\boxed{Z=4}
\]

### 14.4 Atoms per Unit Cell

| Unit cell | Corner contribution | Face contribution | Body contribution | Total atoms |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Simple cubic | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 |
| Body-centred cubic | 1 | 0 | 1 | 2 |
| Face-centred cubic | 1 | 3 | 0 | 4 |

### 14.5 Coordination Number

Coordination number is the number of nearest neighbouring particles surrounding a particle in a crystal structure.

| Structure | Coordination number |
|---|---:|
| Simple cubic | 6 |
| Body-centred cubic | 8 |
| Face-centred cubic | 12 |

### 14.6 Relationship Between Edge Length and Atomic Radius

For ideal hard-sphere models:

**Simple cubic:**

Atoms touch along the edge.

\[
\boxed{a=2r}
\]

**Body-centred cubic:**

Atoms touch along the body diagonal.

\[
\sqrt{3}a=4r
\]

\[
\boxed{a=\frac{4r}{\sqrt3}}
\]

**Face-centred cubic:**

Atoms touch along the face diagonal.

\[
\sqrt{2}a=4r
\]

\[
\boxed{a=2\sqrt2r}
\]

Where:
- \(a\) = Unit-cell edge length.
- \(r\) = Atomic radius.

## 15. Packing Efficiency

### 15.1 Definition

Packing efficiency is the percentage of the unit-cell volume occupied by the particles represented in the hard-sphere model.

\[
\boxed{\%\text{ packing efficiency}=
\frac{\text{Volume occupied by particles}}{\text{Total unit-cell volume}}\times100}
\]

### 15.2 Simple Cubic

Number of atoms per unit cell:

\[
Z=1
\]

Atomic radius:

\[
r=\frac{a}{2}
\]

Volume occupied by atoms:

\[
V_a=1\left(\frac43\pi r^3\right)
\]

Unit-cell volume:

\[
V_c=a^3=8r^3
\]

Therefore:

\[
\%\text{ packing}=
\frac{\frac43\pi r^3}{8r^3}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{52.4\%}
\]

### 15.3 Body-Centred Cubic

Number of atoms:

\[
Z=2
\]

Relationship:

\[
a=\frac{4r}{\sqrt3}
\]

Packing efficiency:

\[
\boxed{68.0\%}
\]

### 15.4 Face-Centred Cubic

Number of atoms:

\[
Z=4
\]

Relationship:

\[
a=2\sqrt2r
\]

Packing efficiency:

\[
\boxed{74.0\%}
\]

### 15.5 Comparison

| Structure | Atoms per unit cell | Coordination number | Packing efficiency |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Simple cubic | 1 | 6 | 52.4% |
| Body-centred cubic | 2 | 8 | 68.0% |
| Face-centred cubic | 4 | 12 | 74.0% |

## 16. Density of a Unit Cell

### 16.1 Formula

Density is mass divided by volume.

For a cubic unit cell:

\[
\boxed{\rho=\frac{ZM}{N_Aa^3}}
\]

Where:
- \(\rho\) = Density.
- \(Z\) = Number of atoms or formula units per unit cell, as appropriate.
- \(M\) = Molar mass.
- \(N_A\) = Avogadro constant.
- \(a\) = Unit-cell edge length.

The units must be consistent.

For example, if \(M\) is in kg mol⁻¹ and \(a\) is in metres, density is obtained in kg m⁻³.

### 16.2 Worked Example

A simple cubic crystal has:
- Molar mass = 60 g mol⁻¹.
- Unit-cell edge length = 300 pm.
- Number of particles per unit cell = 1.

Calculate its density.

Convert edge length:

\[
300\,pm=3.0\times10^{-8}\,cm
\]

Unit-cell volume:

\[
a^3=(3.0\times10^{-8})^3
\]

\[
a^3=2.7\times10^{-23}\,cm^3
\]

Mass of one unit cell:

\[
m=\frac{ZM}{N_A}
\]

\[
m=\frac{1(60)}{6.022\times10^{23}}
\]

\[
m\approx9.96\times10^{-23}\,g
\]

Density:

\[
\rho=\frac{9.96\times10^{-23}}{2.7\times10^{-23}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\rho\approx3.69\,g\,cm^{-3}}
\]

## 17. Imperfections and Defects in Solids

### 17.1 Point Defects

Real crystals are not perfectly ordered. They contain imperfections called crystal defects.

Point defects involve one or a few lattice positions.

### 17.2 Vacancy Defect

A vacancy defect occurs when an atom or ion is missing from its normal lattice position.

### 17.3 Interstitial Defect

An interstitial defect occurs when an additional particle occupies a space between normal lattice positions.

### 17.4 Schottky Defect

A Schottky defect involves the absence of equal numbers of cations and anions from their normal lattice positions in an ionic solid.

Electrical neutrality is maintained.

The density generally decreases because particles are missing from the crystal.

Examples include NaCl and KCl.

### 17.5 Frenkel Defect

A Frenkel defect occurs when an ion, usually a smaller cation, leaves its normal lattice position and occupies an interstitial position.

The number of ions in the crystal remains unchanged.

Therefore, the density is generally unchanged.

An example is AgCl.

### 17.6 Comparison

| Feature | Schottky defect | Frenkel defect |
|---|---|---|
| Main change | Missing cation-anion pairs | Ion moves to an interstitial site |
| Electrical neutrality | Maintained | Maintained |
| Density | Generally decreases | Generally unchanged |
| Example | NaCl | AgCl |

## 18. Applications of the Study of States of Matter

### 18.1 Gas Technology

Gas laws are used in:
- Gas storage.
- Industrial gas processing.
- Pressure systems.
- Chemical reactors.

### 18.2 Refrigeration and Climate Systems

Changes in pressure, temperature, and phase are central to refrigeration and air-conditioning systems.

### 18.3 Materials Science

Crystal structures help explain the hardness, conductivity, density, and mechanical behaviour of materials.

### 18.4 Pharmaceutical Science

The physical form and crystal structure of a substance can influence its solubility, stability, and processing.

### 18.5 Environmental Science

Gas laws help explain atmospheric behaviour, gas emissions, and the movement of gases in the environment.

## 19. Common Exam Traps

1. **Always use kelvin in gas-law calculations involving temperature.**
2. **Boyle's law applies at constant temperature and fixed amount of gas.**
3. **Charles's law applies at constant pressure and fixed amount of gas.**
4. **Gay-Lussac's law applies at constant volume and fixed amount of gas.**
5. **The ideal gas equation requires consistent units.**
6. **Real gases deviate most strongly from ideal behaviour at high pressure and low temperature.**
7. **A liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals external pressure.**
8. **Viscosity is resistance to flow, while surface tension relates to the energy required to increase surface area.**
9. **Crystalline solids have long-range order; amorphous solids do not.**
10. **A corner atom contributes one-eighth to a cubic unit cell.**
11. **A face-centred atom contributes one-half to a unit cell.**
12. **A body-centred atom contributes fully to a unit cell.**
13. **Schottky defects generally reduce density, while Frenkel defects generally do not change density.**
