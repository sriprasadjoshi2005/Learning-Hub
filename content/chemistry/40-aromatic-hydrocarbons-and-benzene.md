# Lesson 40: Aromatic Hydrocarbons and Benzene

Aromatic compounds are an important class of organic molecules characterised by cyclic, conjugated electron systems with special stability.

Benzene is the fundamental example. Although it contains three formal double bonds in many structural representations, its six pi electrons are delocalised around the ring.

This delocalisation explains benzene's unusual stability and its preference for substitution reactions rather than ordinary addition reactions.

## 1. Introduction to Aromaticity

Aromaticity is a special stabilisation associated with certain cyclic, planar, conjugated systems containing a specific number of pi electrons.

A compound is generally aromatic when it satisfies four conditions:

1. It is cyclic.
2. It is approximately planar.
3. It has continuous conjugation around the ring.
4. It contains \(4n+2\) pi electrons, where \(n\) is a non-negative integer.

This is called Hückel's rule.

## 2. Hückel's Rule

The number of pi electrons in an aromatic system must satisfy:

\[
4n+2
\]

Possible values include:

| \(n\) | Number of pi electrons |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | 2 |
| 1 | 6 |
| 2 | 10 |
| 3 | 14 |

Benzene has six pi electrons.

\[
4(1)+2=6
\]

Therefore, benzene satisfies Hückel's rule.

## 3. Benzene

Benzene has the molecular formula:

\[
C_6H_6
\]

It contains six carbon atoms arranged in a planar hexagonal ring.

Each carbon atom is approximately sp²-hybridised.

Each carbon forms:

- Two sigma bonds with neighbouring carbon atoms.
- One sigma bond with hydrogen.
- One unhybridised p-orbital.

The six p-orbitals overlap sideways to form a delocalised pi-electron system above and below the plane of the ring.

Benzene is commonly represented using two equivalent Kekulé structures, but the actual benzene molecule is a resonance hybrid.

## 4. Structure and Stability of Benzene

### 4.1 Delocalised Pi Electrons

Each carbon contributes one electron to the delocalised pi system.

The six electrons are spread across the entire ring rather than being restricted to three separate double bonds.

### 4.2 Equal Carbon-Carbon Bond Lengths

All six carbon-carbon bonds in benzene have equal lengths.

Their lengths are intermediate between typical single and double carbon-carbon bonds.

### 4.3 Resonance Stability

The delocalised electron system gives benzene additional stability.

This is often called aromatic stabilisation or resonance stabilisation.

Benzene therefore does not behave like a typical alkene, even though it is frequently drawn with alternating single and double bonds.

## 5. Aromaticity Beyond Benzene

Other cyclic systems can also be aromatic if they satisfy Hückel's rule.

Examples include:

- Cyclopropenyl cation: 2 pi electrons.
- Cyclopentadienyl anion: 6 pi electrons.
- Tropylium cation: 6 pi electrons.
- Naphthalene: 10 pi electrons.

A cyclic, planar and fully conjugated system with \(4n\) pi electrons is generally antiaromatic.

If a system is not planar or lacks continuous conjugation, it may be non-aromatic rather than antiaromatic.

## 6. Nomenclature of Aromatic Compounds

Benzene is used as the parent structure for many aromatic compounds.

Examples:

| Structure | Name |
|---|---|
| \(C_6H_5CH_3\) | Methylbenzene |
| \(C_6H_5OH\) | Phenol |
| \(C_6H_5NH_2\) | Aniline |
| \(C_6H_5Cl\) | Chlorobenzene |
| \(C_6H_5NO_2\) | Nitrobenzene |

Methylbenzene is commonly called toluene.

For disubstituted benzene rings, the relative positions can be described using numerical locants or ortho, meta and para terminology.

- Ortho: 1,2-substitution.
- Meta: 1,3-substitution.
- Para: 1,4-substitution.

## 7. Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution

Benzene commonly undergoes electrophilic aromatic substitution.

In this reaction, an electrophile replaces a hydrogen atom on the benzene ring.

The aromatic ring temporarily loses aromaticity during formation of an intermediate, then regains it when a proton is removed.

The general reaction is:

\[
C_6H_6+E^+\rightarrow C_6H_5E+H^+
\]

## 8. Nitration of Benzene

Benzene reacts with concentrated nitric acid in the presence of concentrated sulfuric acid.

\[
C_6H_6+HNO_3
\xrightarrow{conc.\ H_2SO_4}
C_6H_5NO_2+H_2O
\]

The electrophile is the nitronium ion:

\[
NO_2^+
\]

It is generated through reactions involving nitric acid and sulfuric acid.

The product is nitrobenzene.

## 9. Halogenation of Benzene

Benzene reacts with chlorine in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst such as \(FeCl_3\).

\[
C_6H_6+Cl_2\xrightarrow{FeCl_3}C_6H_5Cl+HCl
\]

The product is chlorobenzene.

A similar reaction occurs with bromine using a suitable Lewis acid catalyst.

## 10. Sulfonation of Benzene

Benzene reacts with fuming sulfuric acid to produce benzenesulfonic acid.

\[
C_6H_6+SO_3
\xrightarrow{H_2SO_4}
C_6H_5SO_3H
\]

The reaction is reversible under suitable conditions.

## 11. Friedel-Crafts Alkylation

Benzene reacts with an alkyl halide in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst such as anhydrous aluminium chloride.

\[
C_6H_6+CH_3Cl
\xrightarrow{AlCl_3}
C_6H_5CH_3+HCl
\]

The product is methylbenzene.

The reaction introduces an alkyl group onto the aromatic ring.

## 12. Friedel-Crafts Acylation

Benzene reacts with an acyl halide in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst.

\[
C_6H_6+CH_3COCl
\xrightarrow{AlCl_3}
C_6H_5COCH_3+HCl
\]

The product is an aromatic ketone.

Friedel-Crafts acylation generally avoids the extensive rearrangement and repeated substitution problems associated with some alkylation reactions.

## 13. Mechanism of Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution

The mechanism can be divided into three major stages.

### 13.1 Generation of the Electrophile

A suitable reagent and catalyst produce a strong electrophile.

For nitration:

\[
NO_2^+
\]

### 13.2 Attack on the Aromatic Ring

The benzene pi-electron system attacks the electrophile.

A positively charged sigma complex, also called an arenium ion, forms.

Aromaticity is temporarily lost.

### 13.3 Removal of a Proton

A base removes a proton from the carbon attached to the electrophile.

The aromatic pi system is restored.

The final product is a substituted benzene derivative.

## 14. Activating and Deactivating Groups

Substituents already present on a benzene ring influence the rate and position of further substitution.

### 14.1 Activating Groups

Activating groups increase the electron density of the aromatic ring and generally make electrophilic substitution faster.

Examples include:

\[
-OH,\quad -NH_2,\quad -OR,\quad -R
\]

Many activating groups direct new substituents to the ortho and para positions.

### 14.2 Deactivating Groups

Deactivating groups withdraw electron density and generally make electrophilic substitution slower.

Examples include:

\[
-NO_2,\quad -CN,\quad -CHO,\quad -COOH,\quad -SO_3H
\]

Many deactivating groups direct new substituents to the meta position.

### 14.3 Halogens: An Important Exception

Halogens are deactivating because of their strong negative inductive effect.

However, they are generally ortho-para directing because their lone pairs can donate electron density through resonance.

This is a common examination point.

## 15. Directing Effects Summary

| Substituent | Effect on reaction rate | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| \(-OH\) | Activating | Ortho/para |
| \(-NH_2\) | Activating | Ortho/para |
| \(-CH_3\) | Activating | Ortho/para |
| \(-OCH_3\) | Activating | Ortho/para |
| \(-NO_2\) | Deactivating | Meta |
| \(-CHO\) | Deactivating | Meta |
| \(-COOH\) | Deactivating | Meta |
| \(-CN\) | Deactivating | Meta |
| \(-Cl\) | Deactivating | Ortho/para |
| \(-Br\) | Deactivating | Ortho/para |

## 16. Side-Chain Reactions of Aromatic Compounds

Aromatic compounds with alkyl side chains can undergo reactions at the side chain.

For example, methylbenzene can undergo free-radical halogenation at the benzylic position under suitable conditions.

\[
C_6H_5CH_3+Cl_2
\xrightarrow{h\nu}
C_6H_5CH_2Cl+HCl
\]

The benzylic radical is stabilised by resonance with the aromatic ring.

Under suitable strong oxidation conditions, an alkyl side chain containing a benzylic hydrogen can be oxidised to a carboxyl group.

## 17. Physical and Chemical Properties of Benzene

Benzene is:

- A colourless liquid.
- Volatile and flammable.
- Non-polar overall.
- Sparingly soluble in water.
- A good solvent for many non-polar substances.

Benzene is toxic and carcinogenic. Exposure should be avoided, and safer alternatives are preferred where practical.

## 18. Common Mistakes and Exam Points

- Benzene has six delocalised pi electrons.
- Aromaticity requires cyclic structure, planarity, continuous conjugation and Hückel's rule.
- Benzene generally undergoes substitution rather than ordinary addition.
- Electrophilic aromatic substitution temporarily interrupts aromaticity.
- The final deprotonation step restores aromaticity.
- Nitro groups are meta directing and deactivating.
- Alkyl and hydroxyl groups are generally ortho-para directing and activating.
- Halogens are deactivating but ortho-para directing.
- Ortho, meta and para correspond to 1,2-, 1,3- and 1,4-substitution.

## 19. Lesson Recap

- Aromatic compounds possess special stabilisation from cyclic electron delocalisation.
- Hückel's rule requires \(4n+2\) pi electrons in a suitable cyclic conjugated system.
- Benzene contains six delocalised pi electrons and equal carbon-carbon bond lengths.
- Benzene commonly undergoes electrophilic aromatic substitution.
- Important reactions include nitration, halogenation, sulfonation and Friedel-Crafts reactions.
- Substituents influence both reaction rate and substitution position.
