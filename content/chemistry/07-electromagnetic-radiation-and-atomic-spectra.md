# Lesson 7: Electromagnetic Radiation and Atomic Spectra

Atoms interact with electromagnetic radiation by absorbing and emitting energy. These interactions provide important information about atomic structure, electron energy levels, and the arrangement of electrons.

The study of electromagnetic radiation is essential for understanding atomic spectra, spectroscopy, quantum theory, and the behaviour of electrons in atoms.

This lesson introduces electromagnetic waves, wavelength, frequency, velocity, the electromagnetic spectrum, Planck's quantum hypothesis, the photoelectric effect, atomic emission spectra, and the mathematical relationships used in spectroscopy.

## 1. Electromagnetic Radiation

### 1.1 Definition

Electromagnetic radiation is a form of energy that travels through space as oscillating electric and magnetic fields.

Examples:
- Radio waves.
- Microwaves.
- Infrared radiation.
- Visible light.
- Ultraviolet radiation.
- X-rays.
- Gamma rays.

Electromagnetic radiation can travel through a vacuum without requiring a material medium.

### 1.2 Wave Nature

An electromagnetic wave consists of oscillating electric and magnetic fields.

The electric field and magnetic field:
- Are perpendicular to one another.
- Are perpendicular to the direction of propagation.
- Oscillate in phase in a plane electromagnetic wave.

### 1.3 Speed of Electromagnetic Radiation

In vacuum, all electromagnetic radiation travels at the speed of light.

\[
\boxed{c=2.998\times10^8\text{ m/s}}
\]

The approximate value commonly used is:

\[
c=3.0\times10^8\text{ m/s}
\]

## 2. Important Wave Parameters

### 2.1 Wavelength

Wavelength is the distance between two successive corresponding points of a wave.

It is represented by:

\[
\lambda
\]

SI unit: metre (m).

Common submultiples:
- Nanometre (nm).
- Micrometre (μm).
- Angstrom (Å).

Conversions:

\[
1\text{ nm}=10^{-9}\text{ m}
\]

\[
1\text{ \AA}=10^{-10}\text{ m}
\]

### 2.2 Frequency

Frequency is the number of complete wave cycles passing a fixed point per unit time.

It is represented by:

\[
\nu
\]

SI unit: hertz (Hz).

\[
1\text{ Hz}=1\text{ cycle/s}
\]

### 2.3 Time Period

The time period is the time required for one complete wave cycle.

It is represented by \(T\).

\[
\boxed{T=\frac{1}{\nu}}
\]

### 2.4 Amplitude

Amplitude is the maximum displacement of a wave from its equilibrium position.

For electromagnetic radiation, intensity is related to the energy carried by the wave and, under comparable conditions, increases with the square of the field amplitude.

## 3. Relationship Between Wavelength, Frequency, and Velocity

For a wave:

\[
\boxed{v=\nu\lambda}
\]

For electromagnetic radiation in vacuum:

\[
\boxed{c=\nu\lambda}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\nu=\frac{c}{\lambda}}
\]

And:

\[
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{c}{\nu}}
\]

### 3.1 Important Relationship

Wavelength and frequency are inversely proportional when the speed of the wave is constant.

\[
\nu\propto\frac{1}{\lambda}
\]

Therefore:
- Longer wavelength means lower frequency.
- Shorter wavelength means higher frequency.

### 3.2 Numerical Example

Calculate the frequency of electromagnetic radiation having a wavelength of 600 nm.

Given:

\[
\lambda=600\text{ nm}
\]

Convert to metres:

\[
\lambda=600\times10^{-9}
\]

\[
\lambda=6.0\times10^{-7}\text{ m}
\]

Using:

\[
\nu=\frac{c}{\lambda}
\]

\[
\nu=\frac{3.0\times10^8}{6.0\times10^{-7}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\nu=5.0\times10^{14}\text{ Hz}}
\]

## 4. Electromagnetic Spectrum

### 4.1 Definition

The electromagnetic spectrum is the complete range of electromagnetic radiation arranged according to wavelength or frequency.

The main regions are:

1. Radio waves.
2. Microwaves.
3. Infrared radiation.
4. Visible light.
5. Ultraviolet radiation.
6. X-rays.
7. Gamma rays.

### 4.2 Order of the Spectrum

From lowest frequency and longest wavelength to highest frequency and shortest wavelength:

Radio → Microwave → Infrared → Visible → Ultraviolet → X-ray → Gamma ray.

### 4.3 Visible Light

Visible light is the region of the electromagnetic spectrum detectable by the human eye.

Its approximate wavelength range is:

\[
400\text{ nm to }700\text{ nm}
\]

The colours progress approximately as:

Violet → Indigo → Blue → Green → Yellow → Orange → Red.

Violet has a shorter wavelength and higher frequency than red.

### 4.4 Applications of Electromagnetic Radiation

| Radiation | Important applications |
|---|---|
| Radio waves | Radio and television communication |
| Microwaves | Radar, communication, microwave heating |
| Infrared | Thermal imaging, remote controls |
| Visible light | Vision, photography, optical instruments |
| Ultraviolet | Sterilization, fluorescence |
| X-rays | Medical imaging, material analysis |
| Gamma rays | Cancer treatment, nuclear research |

## 5. Energy of Electromagnetic Radiation

### 5.1 Planck's Quantum Hypothesis

Max Planck proposed that energy is exchanged between matter and radiation in discrete packets called quanta.

The energy of one quantum is:

\[
\boxed{E=h\nu}
\]

Where:
- \(E\) = Energy.
- \(h\) = Planck's constant.
- \(\nu\) = Frequency.

Planck's constant:

\[
\boxed{h=6.626\times10^{-34}\text{ J s}}
\]

For electromagnetic radiation:

\[
E=h\nu
\]

Since:

\[
\nu=\frac{c}{\lambda}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{E=\frac{hc}{\lambda}}
\]

### 5.2 Important Observations

- Energy is directly proportional to frequency.
- Energy is inversely proportional to wavelength.
- Higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.
- Lower-frequency radiation carries less energy per photon.

### 5.3 Numerical Example

Calculate the energy of a photon with frequency \(5\times10^{14}\) Hz.

Given:

\[
h=6.626\times10^{-34}\text{ J s}
\]

\[
\nu=5\times10^{14}\text{ Hz}
\]

Using:

\[
E=h\nu
\]

\[
E=6.626\times10^{-34}\times5\times10^{14}
\]

\[
\boxed{E=3.313\times10^{-19}\text{ J}}
\]

## 6. Photons

### 6.1 Definition

A photon is a quantum or discrete packet of electromagnetic radiation.

Photons:
- Have zero rest mass.
- Carry energy.
- Carry momentum.
- Travel at the speed of light in vacuum.

### 6.2 Photon Energy

\[
\boxed{E=h\nu}
\]

### 6.3 Photon Momentum

The momentum of a photon is:

\[
\boxed{p=\frac{h}{\lambda}}
\]

Where:
- \(p\) = Momentum.
- \(h\) = Planck's constant.
- \(\lambda\) = Wavelength.

## 7. Photoelectric Effect

### 7.1 Definition

The photoelectric effect is the emission of electrons from a material surface when electromagnetic radiation of sufficiently high frequency falls on it.

The emitted electrons are called photoelectrons.

### 7.2 Experimental Observations

1. Photoelectric emission occurs only when the radiation frequency is above a minimum threshold frequency for the material.
2. Increasing light intensity increases the number of emitted electrons under suitable conditions.
3. Increasing frequency above the threshold increases the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons.
4. Emission occurs without a measurable delay under ordinary experimental conditions.

### 7.3 Einstein's Photoelectric Equation

Einstein explained the photoelectric effect using the photon concept.

\[
\boxed{h\nu=\phi+K_{\max}}
\]

Where:
- \(h\nu\) = Incident photon energy.
- \(\phi\) = Work function of the material.
- \(K_{\max}\) = Maximum kinetic energy of emitted electrons.

The work function is the minimum energy required to remove an electron from the material surface.

Since:

\[
\phi=h\nu_0
\]

Where \(\nu_0\) is the threshold frequency.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{K_{\max}=h(\nu-\nu_0)}
\]

### 7.4 Applications

- Photocells.
- Light sensors.
- Automatic lighting systems.
- Photoelectric detectors.
- Solar-energy technology, through related photon-induced electronic processes.

## 8. Atomic Spectra

### 8.1 Definition

An atomic spectrum is the pattern of wavelengths or frequencies of electromagnetic radiation absorbed or emitted by atoms.

Atomic spectra provide evidence that electron energies in atoms are quantized.

### 8.2 Continuous Spectrum

A continuous spectrum contains an uninterrupted range of wavelengths.

Example: Radiation emitted by a sufficiently hot dense object.

### 8.3 Line Spectrum

A line spectrum consists of separate discrete spectral lines.

It is produced when electrons transition between specific quantized energy levels.

Different elements have characteristic spectral line patterns.

### 8.4 Emission Spectrum

An emission spectrum is produced when excited atoms release energy as electrons move from higher energy levels to lower energy levels.

### 8.5 Absorption Spectrum

An absorption spectrum is produced when atoms absorb photons corresponding to energy differences between allowed energy levels.

Dark lines may appear in a continuous spectrum at the absorbed wavelengths.

## 9. Hydrogen Atomic Spectrum

### 9.1 Hydrogen Energy Levels

In the Bohr model, the energy of an electron in a hydrogen atom is:

\[
\boxed{E_n=-\frac{13.6}{n^2}\text{ eV}}
\]

Where \(n\) is the principal energy level.

Examples:

For \(n=1\):

\[
E_1=-13.6\text{ eV}
\]

For \(n=2\):

\[
E_2=-3.4\text{ eV}
\]

For \(n=3\):

\[
E_3=-1.51\text{ eV}
\]

The energy levels become closer together as \(n\) increases.

### 9.2 Energy Transitions

When an electron moves from a higher energy level to a lower energy level, energy is emitted.

\[
\boxed{\Delta E=E_{\text{higher}}-E_{\text{lower}}}
\]

The emitted photon satisfies:

\[
\boxed{\Delta E=h\nu}
\]

When an electron moves from a lower level to a higher level, energy must be absorbed.

### 9.3 Hydrogen Spectral Series

The major spectral series are classified according to the final energy level.

| Series | Final energy level | Spectral region |
|---|---:|---|
| Lyman | \(n=1\) | Ultraviolet |
| Balmer | \(n=2\) | Mainly visible |
| Paschen | \(n=3\) | Infrared |
| Brackett | \(n=4\) | Infrared |
| Pfund | \(n=5\) | Infrared |

### 9.4 Rydberg Equation

The wavelength of a hydrogen spectral line can be calculated using:

\[
\boxed{\frac{1}{\lambda}
=R_H\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2}-\frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)}
\]

Where:
- \(R_H\) = Rydberg constant.
- \(n_1\) = Lower energy level.
- \(n_2\) = Higher energy level.
- \(n_2>n_1\).

For hydrogen:

\[
R_H\approx1.097\times10^7\text{ m}^{-1}
\]

## 10. Practical Applications of Spectroscopy

### 10.1 Element Identification

Each element has a characteristic emission or absorption spectrum, allowing scientists to identify elements in unknown samples.

### 10.2 Astronomy

Spectroscopy helps determine:
- Chemical composition of stars.
- Stellar temperatures.
- Radial velocities.
- Physical conditions in astronomical objects.

### 10.3 Chemical Analysis

Spectroscopic techniques are used to identify and quantify substances.

Examples:
- Atomic absorption spectroscopy.
- Atomic emission spectroscopy.
- UV-visible spectroscopy.
- Infrared spectroscopy.

## 11. Common Exam Traps

1. Wavelength and frequency are inversely proportional in vacuum.
2. The speed of electromagnetic radiation in vacuum is approximately \(3\times10^8\) m/s.
3. Higher-frequency photons have greater energy.
4. Planck's equation is \(E=h\nu\).
5. The photoelectric effect requires a threshold frequency.
6. Increasing intensity does not compensate for a frequency below the threshold.
7. Emission occurs when electrons move from higher to lower energy levels.
8. Absorption occurs when electrons move from lower to higher energy levels.
9. Hydrogen's Lyman series ends at \(n=1\).
10. The Balmer series ends at \(n=2\).
11. The Rydberg equation uses the lower and higher energy levels.
12. A line spectrum contains discrete wavelengths, not a continuous range.
