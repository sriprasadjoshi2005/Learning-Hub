# Lesson 21: Chemical Thermodynamics

Chemical thermodynamics studies the energy changes that accompany physical and chemical processes. It explains heat transfer, work, internal energy, enthalpy, entropy, and the conditions under which a process can occur spontaneously. These principles help us understand why some reactions release heat, why others absorb it, and how energy determines the direction of chemical change.

## 1. Introduction to Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics deals with the relationship between heat, work, temperature, and energy.

In chemistry, it is particularly useful for studying:
- Heat released or absorbed during reactions.
- Energy changes during phase transitions.
- Whether a reaction is thermodynamically favourable.
- The conversion of chemical energy into other forms of energy.

### 1.1 System and Surroundings

A **system** is the part of the universe selected for investigation.

The **surroundings** include everything outside the system that can interact with it.

The system and surroundings together form the universe.

### 1.2 Types of Systems

| System | Exchange of matter | Exchange of energy | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open | Yes | Yes | Water boiling in an open vessel |
| Closed | No | Yes | Gas in a sealed container |
| Isolated | No | No | An ideal perfectly insulated container |

### 1.3 State of a System

The state of a thermodynamic system is described by measurable properties such as:
- Pressure (P)
- Volume (V)
- Temperature (T)
- Amount of substance (n)
- Internal energy (U)

A change in these properties represents a change in the state of the system.

## 2. Properties of a Thermodynamic System

### 2.1 Extensive Properties

Extensive properties depend on the amount of substance present.

Examples:
- Mass
- Volume
- Internal energy
- Enthalpy
- Entropy

If the amount of a homogeneous system is doubled, its extensive properties generally double.

### 2.2 Intensive Properties

Intensive properties do not depend on the total amount of substance.

Examples:
- Temperature
- Pressure
- Density
- Concentration

### 2.3 State Functions and Path Functions

A **state function** depends only on the initial and final states, not on the path followed.

Examples include:
- Internal energy
- Enthalpy
- Entropy
- Gibbs free energy

A **path function** depends on the process followed.

Heat and work are path functions.

## 3. Internal Energy

Internal energy, represented by U, is the total microscopic energy of a system.

It includes contributions from:
- Molecular translational energy.
- Molecular rotational energy.
- Molecular vibrational energy.
- Electronic energy.
- Intermolecular interactions.

The change in internal energy is:

\[
\Delta U=U_{\text{final}}-U_{\text{initial}}
\]

### 3.1 First Law of Thermodynamics

The first law expresses the conservation of energy.

Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only be transferred or converted from one form to another.

Using the chemistry sign convention:

\[
\boxed{\Delta U=q+w}
\]

Where:
- \(\Delta U\) = change in internal energy.
- \(q\) = heat supplied to the system.
- \(w\) = work done on the system.

Sign convention:

| Process | Sign |
|---|---|
| Heat absorbed by system | \(q>0\) |
| Heat released by system | \(q<0\) |
| Work done on system | \(w>0\) |
| Work done by system | \(w<0\) |

### 3.2 Pressure-Volume Work

When a gas expands against an external pressure, it performs work on the surroundings.

For constant external pressure:

\[
\boxed{w=-P_{\text{ext}}\Delta V}
\]

Where:

\[
\Delta V=V_f-V_i
\]

During expansion, \(\Delta V>0\), so work is negative.

During compression, \(\Delta V<0\), so work is positive.

### 3.3 Worked Example

A gas expands from 2 L to 5 L against a constant external pressure of 2 atm.

Calculate the work done in L atm.

\[
w=-P_{\text{ext}}(V_f-V_i)
\]

\[
w=-2(5-2)
\]

\[
\boxed{w=-6\text{ L atm}}
\]

Using:

\[
1\text{ L atm}=101.325\text{ J}
\]

\[
w=-6(101.325)
\]

\[
\boxed{w=-607.95\text{ J}}
\]

## 4. Heat Capacity and Calorimetry

Heat capacity is the amount of heat required to increase the temperature of a substance by one kelvin.

\[
C=\frac{q}{\Delta T}
\]

Specific heat capacity is the heat required to raise the temperature of one gram of a substance by one kelvin.

\[
\boxed{q=mc\Delta T}
\]

Where:
- q = heat transferred.
- m = mass.
- c = specific heat capacity.
- \(\Delta T=T_f-T_i\).

Molar heat capacity is the heat required to raise the temperature of one mole of a substance by one kelvin.

### 4.1 Calorimetry

Calorimetry measures heat changes during physical and chemical processes.

In an ideal isolated calorimeter:

\[
q_{\text{system}}+q_{\text{surroundings}}=0
\]

Therefore:

\[
q_{\text{system}}=-q_{\text{surroundings}}
\]

A bomb calorimeter operates at constant volume and is commonly used to measure changes in internal energy.

A coffee-cup calorimeter operates approximately at constant pressure and is commonly used to measure enthalpy changes.

## 5. Enthalpy

Enthalpy is a thermodynamic state function defined as:

\[
\boxed{H=U+PV}
\]

The change in enthalpy is:

\[
\Delta H=\Delta U+\Delta(PV)
\]

At constant pressure, when only pressure-volume work is involved:

\[
\boxed{q_p=\Delta H}
\]

### 5.1 Exothermic Processes

An exothermic process releases heat to the surroundings.

\[
\Delta H<0
\]

Examples:
- Combustion of fuels.
- Neutralisation of strong acids and bases.
- Freezing of water.

### 5.2 Endothermic Processes

An endothermic process absorbs heat from the surroundings.

\[
\Delta H>0
\]

Examples:
- Melting of ice.
- Vaporisation of water.
- Thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate.

### 5.3 Enthalpy and Gaseous Reactions

For reactions involving ideal gases:

\[
\boxed{\Delta H=\Delta U+\Delta n_gRT}
\]

Where:

\[
\Delta n_g=n_{\text{gaseous products}}-n_{\text{gaseous reactants}}
\]

Only gaseous species are included in \(\Delta n_g\).

## 6. Enthalpy Changes in Chemical Reactions

### 6.1 Standard Enthalpy of Formation

The standard enthalpy of formation is the enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its constituent elements in their standard states.

It is represented by:

\[
\Delta_fH^\circ
\]

The standard enthalpy of formation of an element in its standard state is zero.

### 6.2 Standard Enthalpy of Combustion

The standard enthalpy of combustion is the enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely burned in oxygen under standard conditions.

Combustion reactions are generally exothermic.

### 6.3 Standard Enthalpy of Neutralisation

The enthalpy of neutralisation is the enthalpy change when an acid and a base react to form one mole of water under specified conditions.

For a strong acid reacting with a strong base in dilute aqueous solution, the value is approximately:

\[
-57.1\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}
\]

### 6.4 Hess's Law

Hess's law states that the total enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the pathway followed, provided the initial and final states are the same.

If a reaction occurs in multiple steps:

\[
\boxed{\Delta H_{\text{overall}}=\sum \Delta H_{\text{steps}}}
\]

This law is useful for calculating enthalpy changes that cannot be measured directly.

### 6.5 Enthalpy from Standard Formation Values

For a balanced chemical reaction:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta_rH^\circ=
\sum \nu\Delta_fH^\circ(\text{products})
-
\sum \nu\Delta_fH^\circ(\text{reactants})
}
\]

Here, \(\nu\) represents the stoichiometric coefficient.

## 7. Bond Enthalpy

Bond enthalpy is the enthalpy required to break one mole of a specified bond in gaseous molecules.

Breaking bonds requires energy, whereas forming bonds releases energy.

An approximate reaction enthalpy can be calculated using:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta H\approx
\sum E(\text{bonds broken})
-
\sum E(\text{bonds formed})
}
\]

This method generally uses average bond enthalpies and therefore provides an estimate.

## 8. Common Exam Traps

- Do not confuse heat and temperature. Heat is energy transferred; temperature measures thermal state.
- Heat and work are path functions, not state functions.
- Expansion work is negative under the chemistry sign convention.
- At constant pressure, heat transferred equals enthalpy change only under the appropriate conditions.
- The standard enthalpy of formation of an element in its standard state is zero.
- Hess's law depends on initial and final states, not the number of reaction steps.

## 9. Applications of Thermodynamics

- Designing engines and power-generation systems.
- Estimating energy released by fuels.
- Understanding industrial chemical processes.
- Evaluating the energy requirements of phase changes.
- Studying energy efficiency and chemical manufacturing.
