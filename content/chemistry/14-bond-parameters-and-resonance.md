# Lesson 14: Bond Parameters and Resonance

Chemical bonds can be described using measurable properties such as bond length, bond angle, bond enthalpy, bond order, and bond polarity. These properties help explain the strength, stability, geometry, and reactivity of molecules.

Some molecules cannot be represented accurately using a single Lewis structure. In such cases, multiple valid Lewis structures, called resonance contributors, are used to describe the distribution of electrons. Resonance explains electron delocalization and helps us understand molecular stability and the equivalence of certain bonds.

## 1. Introduction to Bond Parameters

Bond parameters are measurable or calculable quantities used to describe chemical bonds.

The important bond parameters are:

1. Bond length.
2. Bond angle.
3. Bond enthalpy.
4. Bond order.
5. Bond polarity.
6. Dipole moment.

These parameters are interconnected. For example, bond order generally influences bond length and bond strength, while molecular geometry influences the overall polarity of a molecule.

## 2. Bond Length

### 2.1 Definition

Bond length is the equilibrium distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms in a molecule.

It is usually measured in picometres (pm) or angstroms (Å).

\[
1\,\text{Å}=100\,\text{pm}
\]

Bond length is determined by the balance between attractive and repulsive forces acting between the atoms.

At the equilibrium bond distance, the potential energy of the bonded system is minimum.

### 2.2 Factors Affecting Bond Length

**1. Atomic size**

Larger atoms generally form longer bonds when compared with smaller atoms having similar bonding arrangements.

For example:

\[
H-F < H-Cl < H-Br < H-I
\]

The bond length increases as the halogen atom becomes larger.

**2. Bond order**

For bonds between the same pair of atoms, increasing bond order generally decreases bond length.

\[
\text{Single bond}>\text{Double bond}>\text{Triple bond}
\]

In terms of bond length:

\[
r_{\text{single}}>r_{\text{double}}>r_{\text{triple}}
\]

For example:

| Bond | Approximate bond length |
|---|---:|
| C—C | 154 pm |
| C=C | 134 pm |
| C≡C | 120 pm |

**3. Hybridization**

Greater s-character in the bonding orbital generally results in shorter bonds because s orbitals have greater electron density closer to the nucleus.

The common order is:

\[
sp<sp^2<sp^3
\]

This represents increasing bond length for comparable carbon bonds.

### 2.3 Importance of Bond Length

Bond length helps determine:

- Molecular dimensions.
- Bond strength trends.
- Molecular geometry.
- Changes in bonding during chemical reactions.

## 3. Bond Angle

### 3.1 Definition

Bond angle is the angle between two bonds that share a common central atom.

It is generally measured in degrees.

For example, the H—O—H bond angle in water is approximately:

\[
104.5^\circ
\]

Bond angles depend on the arrangement of electron domains around the central atom.

### 3.2 Factors Affecting Bond Angle

**1. Electron-pair repulsion**

Electron pairs repel each other because they contain negatively charged electrons.

The approximate order of repulsion is:

\[
LP-LP>LP-BP>BP-BP
\]

Where:

- LP = Lone pair.
- BP = Bonding pair.

Lone pairs occupy more space around the central atom and generally exert stronger repulsion than bonding pairs.

**2. Lone pairs**

Lone pairs can reduce bond angles by pushing bonding pairs closer together.

For example:

| Molecule | Shape | Approximate bond angle |
|---|---|---:|
| CH₄ | Tetrahedral | 109.5° |
| NH₃ | Trigonal pyramidal | 107° |
| H₂O | Bent | 104.5° |

The decreasing bond angle is associated with increasing lone-pair repulsion.

**3. Hybridization**

Hybridization also influences the expected bond angles.

| Hybridization | Ideal geometry | Ideal angle |
|---|---|---:|
| sp | Linear | 180° |
| sp² | Trigonal planar | 120° |
| sp³ | Tetrahedral | 109.5° |

Actual bond angles can differ from ideal values because of lone pairs, different surrounding atoms, and other molecular effects.

## 4. Bond Enthalpy

### 4.1 Definition

Bond enthalpy is the enthalpy required to break a particular bond in gaseous molecules.

For a bond-breaking process:

\[
A-B(g)\rightarrow A(g)+B(g)
\]

Energy is absorbed, so bond breaking is endothermic.

Bond enthalpy is commonly expressed in kJ mol⁻¹.

### 4.2 Factors Affecting Bond Enthalpy

**1. Bond order**

For comparable bonds between the same atoms, higher bond order generally corresponds to greater bond enthalpy.

\[
E_{\text{triple}}>E_{\text{double}}>E_{\text{single}}
\]

For example:

\[
E_{C\equiv C}>E_{C=C}>E_{C-C}
\]

**2. Bond length**

Shorter bonds are generally stronger because the bonded atoms are held more closely together.

Therefore, bond length and bond enthalpy are generally inversely related for comparable bonds.

**3. Atomic size**

Bonding between smaller atoms can produce shorter and stronger bonds, although the exact trend depends on orbital overlap and the identities of the atoms.

### 4.3 Average Bond Enthalpy

Some molecules contain several bonds of the same type in different chemical environments.

The energy required to break these bonds may differ.

Average bond enthalpy is the average energy required to break one mole of a particular type of bond in gaseous molecules.

For example, the average C—H bond enthalpy is obtained from a range of gaseous compounds rather than being a universal value for every C—H bond.

### 4.4 Estimating Enthalpy Changes Using Bond Enthalpies

The enthalpy change of a gaseous reaction can be estimated using:

\[
\boxed{\Delta H\approx\sum BE_{\text{broken}}-\sum BE_{\text{formed}}}
\]

Where:

- \(BE_{\text{broken}}\) = Bond enthalpies of bonds broken.
- \(BE_{\text{formed}}\) = Bond enthalpies of bonds formed.

Breaking bonds requires energy, while forming bonds releases energy.

**Worked Example**

Consider the reaction:

\[
H_2+Cl_2\rightarrow 2HCl
\]

Given:

- H—H bond enthalpy = 436 kJ mol⁻¹.
- Cl—Cl bond enthalpy = 243 kJ mol⁻¹.
- H—Cl bond enthalpy = 431 kJ mol⁻¹.

Energy required to break the reactant bonds:

\[
436+243=679
\]

Energy released when two H—Cl bonds form:

\[
2(431)=862
\]

Therefore:

\[
\Delta H=679-862
\]

\[
\boxed{\Delta H=-183\,\text{kJ mol}^{-1}}
\]

The negative value indicates that the reaction is exothermic.

## 5. Bond Order

### 5.1 Definition

Bond order represents the number of chemical bonds between two atoms in a Lewis structure.

For simple Lewis structures:

- Single bond: Bond order = 1.
- Double bond: Bond order = 2.
- Triple bond: Bond order = 3.

Bond order provides a useful indication of bond strength and bond length.

### 5.2 Bond Order in Molecular Orbital Theory

In molecular orbital theory, bond order is calculated using:

\[
\boxed{BO=\frac{N_b-N_a}{2}}
\]

Where:

- \(N_b\) = Number of electrons in bonding molecular orbitals.
- \(N_a\) = Number of electrons in antibonding molecular orbitals.

A higher bond order generally indicates a stronger and shorter bond.

A bond order of zero indicates that the simple molecular orbital description predicts no net bond stabilization.

### 5.3 Bond Order and Bond Stability

For comparable bonds:

- Higher bond order generally means greater bond strength.
- Higher bond order generally means shorter bond length.
- Lower bond order generally means weaker bonding.

However, exact bond strengths also depend on the atoms involved and the surrounding molecular environment.

## 6. Bond Polarity

### 6.1 Definition

Bond polarity describes the unequal distribution of shared electrons between two bonded atoms.

It occurs when the atoms have different electronegativities.

The more electronegative atom attracts the bonding electrons more strongly and develops a partial negative charge.

The less electronegative atom develops a partial positive charge.

### 6.2 Non-Polar Covalent Bond

When two atoms have equal or nearly equal electronegativities, the bonding electrons are shared equally or almost equally.

Examples:

\[
H-H
\]

\[
Cl-Cl
\]

These bonds are non-polar.

### 6.3 Polar Covalent Bond

When two bonded atoms have different electronegativities, the electron distribution becomes unequal.

For hydrogen chloride:

\[
H^{\delta+}-Cl^{\delta-}
\]

Chlorine attracts the shared electron pair more strongly than hydrogen.

### 6.4 Bond Dipole

A bond dipole represents the separation of partial positive and negative charges in a polar bond.

The dipole arrow points towards the more electronegative atom.

For example:

```text
H  →  Cl
δ+     δ−
```

The arrow indicates the direction of increasing electron density.

## 7. Dipole Moment

### 7.1 Definition

Dipole moment is a measure of the separation of positive and negative charges in a system.

For a simple pair of equal and opposite charges:

\[
\boxed{\mu=q\times r}
\]

Where:

- \(\mu\) = Dipole moment.
- \(q\) = Magnitude of the separated charge.
- \(r\) = Distance between the centres of charge.

Its SI unit is coulomb metre (C m).

A commonly used chemical unit is the debye (D).

\[
1\,D\approx3.336\times10^{-30}\,C\,m
\]

### 7.2 Dipole Moment of a Molecule

The overall dipole moment of a molecule is the vector sum of its individual bond dipoles and other contributions to its charge distribution.

Therefore, molecular polarity depends on both:

1. Bond polarity.
2. Molecular geometry.

### 7.3 Examples of Molecular Dipole Moments

**Carbon dioxide (CO₂)**

Carbon dioxide has two polar C=O bonds.

However, the molecule is linear and symmetrical.

The two bond dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel.

\[
\boxed{\mu=0}
\]

Therefore, CO₂ is non-polar.

**Water (H₂O)**

Water contains two polar O—H bonds.

Its molecular geometry is bent, so the bond dipoles do not cancel.

Therefore, water has a net dipole moment and is polar.

**Ammonia (NH₃)**

Ammonia has a trigonal pyramidal geometry.

The N—H bond dipoles do not cancel completely.

Therefore, ammonia is polar.

**Boron trifluoride (BF₃)**

BF₃ has three polar B—F bonds arranged in a trigonal planar geometry.

The symmetrical arrangement causes the bond dipoles to cancel.

Therefore, BF₃ is non-polar.

### 7.4 Molecular Polarity Comparison

| Molecule | Geometry | Molecular polarity |
|---|---|---|
| CO₂ | Linear | Non-polar |
| H₂O | Bent | Polar |
| NH₃ | Trigonal pyramidal | Polar |
| BF₃ | Trigonal planar | Non-polar |
| CH₄ | Tetrahedral | Non-polar |
| SO₂ | Bent | Polar |
| CCl₄ | Tetrahedral | Non-polar |

A molecule can contain polar bonds and still be non-polar if its bond dipoles cancel because of symmetry.

## 8. Introduction to Resonance

### 8.1 Why Resonance Is Needed

Some molecules cannot be represented accurately by a single Lewis structure.

A single structure may suggest that certain bonds are different even though experimental evidence shows that they are equivalent.

Resonance provides a way to represent electron delocalization using multiple Lewis structures.

### 8.2 Definition of Resonance

Resonance occurs when a molecule or ion can be represented by two or more valid Lewis structures that differ only in the arrangement of electrons, not in the positions of the atoms.

These individual structures are called resonance contributors or canonical structures.

The actual molecule is described by a resonance hybrid.

### 8.3 Resonance Hybrid

A resonance hybrid is the overall electronic structure represented by the combination of all significant resonance contributors.

It is not a molecule rapidly switching between different structures.

Instead, the electrons are delocalized over the relevant atoms.

### 8.4 Conditions for Resonance

Resonance contributors must have:

1. The same arrangement of atomic nuclei.
2. The same total number of electrons.
3. The same overall charge.
4. Different arrangements of electrons, such as pi electrons or lone pairs.
5. Valid electron distributions according to the applicable bonding model.

Only electron positions change when moving from one resonance contributor to another.

## 9. Resonance in Common Molecules and Ions

### 9.1 Ozone (O₃)

Ozone contains three oxygen atoms and has 18 valence electrons.

It can be represented by two equivalent major resonance contributors.

```text
O=O⁺—O⁻  ↔  ⁻O—O⁺=O
```

In each contributor:

- The central oxygen carries a formal charge of +1.
- The singly bonded terminal oxygen carries a formal charge of −1.
- The double-bonded terminal oxygen has a formal charge of zero.

The actual ozone molecule has two equivalent O—O bonds.

Its average bond order is:

\[
BO=\frac{2+1}{2}=1.5
\]

This is a simplified way to describe the equivalence of its two bonds.

### 9.2 Carbonate Ion (CO₃²⁻)

The carbonate ion contains one carbon atom and three oxygen atoms.

Its total number of valence electrons is:

\[
4+3(6)+2=24
\]

It has three equivalent major resonance contributors.

In each contributor:

- One C—O bond is represented as a double bond.
- Two C—O bonds are represented as single bonds.
- The two singly bonded oxygen atoms each carry a formal charge of −1.
- Carbon has a formal charge of zero.
- The double-bonded oxygen has a formal charge of zero.

```text
       O
       ||
[ O⁻—C—O⁻ ]²⁻
```

The position of the double bond can be changed among the three oxygen atoms.

The actual carbonate ion has three equivalent C—O bonds.

Its average bond order is:

\[
BO=\frac{2+1+1}{3}
\]

\[
\boxed{BO=\frac{4}{3}}
\]

### 9.3 Nitrate Ion (NO₃⁻)

The nitrate ion has three equivalent resonance contributors.

Each contributor contains:

- One N=O double bond.
- Two N—O single bonds.
- A formal charge of +1 on nitrogen.
- A formal charge of −1 on each singly bonded oxygen.

The average N—O bond order is:

\[
BO=\frac{2+1+1}{3}
\]

\[
\boxed{BO=\frac{4}{3}}
\]

All three N—O bonds are equivalent in the resonance hybrid.

### 9.4 Benzene (C₆H₆)

Benzene is a six-membered carbon ring containing six hydrogen atoms.

Its conventional Lewis representations contain alternating single and double bonds.

Two equivalent major resonance contributors can be drawn by shifting the positions of the double bonds.

```text
   C=C              C—C
  /   \            /   \
 C     C    ↔     C     C
 ||   ||          ||   ||
 C     C          C     C
  \   /            \   /
   C—C              C=C
```

The actual benzene molecule does not contain three permanently fixed single bonds and three permanently fixed double bonds.

Its six pi electrons are delocalized over the carbon ring.

All six C—C bonds are equivalent and have lengths intermediate between typical single and double bonds.

## 10. Resonance Energy and Molecular Stability

### 10.1 Resonance Stabilization

Delocalization of electrons can lower the energy of a molecule or ion compared with a suitable localized bonding description.

This stabilization is called resonance stabilization.

### 10.2 Resonance Energy

Resonance energy is commonly described as the energy difference between the actual resonance hybrid and an appropriate hypothetical localized structure.

The actual resonance hybrid is more stable than any individual valid contributor considered separately.

The exact numerical value depends on the comparison model used.

### 10.3 Factors Affecting the Importance of Resonance Contributors

The more important contributors generally have:

1. Complete octets for atoms where possible.
2. Smaller magnitudes of formal charge.
3. Minimal separation of opposite charges.
4. Negative formal charges on more electronegative atoms.
5. Similar energy to other important contributors.

Equivalent resonance contributors contribute equally to the resonance hybrid.

Non-equivalent contributors do not necessarily contribute equally.

## 11. Resonance Versus Isomerism

Resonance and isomerism are different concepts.

| Feature | Resonance | Isomerism |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic arrangement | Same | May differ |
| Electron arrangement | Different | May differ |
| Nuclear positions | Same in contributors | Different in structural isomers |
| Representation | Multiple contributors describe one electronic structure | Different compounds with distinct structures |
| Example | Ozone resonance contributors | Ethanol and dimethyl ether |

Resonance contributors are not separate compounds that can normally be isolated from one another.

## 12. Common Exam Traps

1. **Bond length is not the same as bond angle.** Bond length is a distance, while bond angle is an angular measurement.
2. **Bond breaking requires energy.** Bond formation releases energy.
3. **Higher bond order generally means a shorter, stronger bond.**
4. **A polar bond does not guarantee a polar molecule.** Molecular geometry determines whether individual bond dipoles cancel.
5. **CO₂ is non-polar despite containing polar C=O bonds.**
6. **Resonance contributors do not represent rapidly interconverting structures.**
7. **Only electron positions change between resonance contributors.** The arrangement of atomic nuclei remains unchanged.
8. **Equivalent resonance contributors have equal importance.**
9. **The actual resonance hybrid is more stable than an individual contributor considered separately.**
10. **Average bond enthalpies are approximate values.** Individual bond strengths can depend on their molecular environments.
