# Lesson 17: Percentage Composition and Empirical and Molecular Formulae

The chemical formula of a compound describes the elements present and the relative number of atoms of each element. Quantitative analysis can determine the mass contribution of each element and help establish the formula of an unknown compound.

Percentage composition expresses the mass percentage of each element in a compound. The empirical formula gives the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms, while the molecular formula gives the actual number of atoms of each element in a molecule.

## 1. Percentage Composition

### 1.1 Definition

Percentage composition is the percentage by mass contributed by each element in a compound.

It is calculated by comparing the total mass of an element in one mole of the compound with the molar mass of the compound.

\[
\boxed{\%\text{ element}=\frac{\text{Mass of element in one mole}}{\text{Molar mass of compound}}\times100}
\]

The sum of the mass percentages of all elements in a pure compound is approximately 100%.

### 1.2 Worked Example: Percentage Composition of Water

The molecular formula of water is H₂O.

Atomic masses:
- H = 1 g mol⁻¹.
- O = 16 g mol⁻¹.

Molar mass:

\[
M(H_2O)=2(1)+16=18
\]

Percentage of hydrogen:

\[
\%H=\frac{2}{18}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{\%H=11.11\%}
\]

Percentage of oxygen:

\[
\%O=\frac{16}{18}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{\%O=88.89\%}
\]

Verification:

\[
11.11+88.89=100\%
\]

### 1.3 Worked Example: Percentage Composition of Calcium Carbonate

Formula:

\[
CaCO_3
\]

Atomic masses:
- Ca = 40.
- C = 12.
- O = 16.

Molar mass:

\[
M=40+12+3(16)=100
\]

Percentage of calcium:

\[
\%Ca=\frac{40}{100}\times100=40\%
\]

Percentage of carbon:

\[
\%C=\frac{12}{100}\times100=12\%
\]

Percentage of oxygen:

\[
\%O=\frac{48}{100}\times100=48\%
\]

Therefore:

| Element | Percentage by mass |
|---|---:|
| Calcium | 40% |
| Carbon | 12% |
| Oxygen | 48% |
| Total | 100% |

## 2. Empirical Formula

### 2.1 Definition

The empirical formula represents the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.

For example:

| Molecular formula | Empirical formula |
|---|---|
| H₂O₂ | HO |
| C₆H₁₂O₆ | CH₂O |
| C₂H₄ | CH₂ |
| C₆H₆ | CH |

The empirical formula does not necessarily represent the actual number of atoms in a molecule.

### 2.2 Steps to Determine an Empirical Formula

When percentage composition is given:

1. Assume 100 g of the compound if percentages are provided.
2. Convert the mass of each element into moles.
3. Divide all mole values by the smallest mole value.
4. Convert the resulting ratios into the smallest whole numbers.
5. Write the empirical formula.

### 2.3 Worked Example

A compound contains:

- Carbon = 40%.
- Hydrogen = 6.67%.
- Oxygen = 53.33%.

Determine its empirical formula.

**Step 1: Assume 100 g of the compound.**

Masses:
- C = 40 g.
- H = 6.67 g.
- O = 53.33 g.

**Step 2: Convert masses into moles.**

\[
n(C)=\frac{40}{12}=3.333
\]

\[
n(H)=\frac{6.67}{1}=6.67
\]

\[
n(O)=\frac{53.33}{16}=3.333
\]

**Step 3: Divide by the smallest value.**

\[
C:H:O=3.333:6.67:3.333
\]

Dividing by 3.333:

\[
C:H:O=1:2:1
\]

**Step 4: Write the empirical formula.**

\[
\boxed{CH_2O}
\]

### 2.4 When Ratios Are Not Whole Numbers

Sometimes the mole ratios contain fractions.

Multiply all ratios by a suitable integer to obtain whole numbers.

| Ratio obtained | Multiply by | Whole-number ratio |
|---|---:|---|
| 1 : 1.5 | 2 | 2 : 3 |
| 1 : 1.33 | 3 | 3 : 4 |
| 1 : 1.25 | 4 | 4 : 5 |
| 1 : 1.67 | 3 | 3 : 5 |

Small experimental deviations may occur because of rounding. Ratios should be interpreted using appropriate precision.

## 3. Molecular Formula

### 3.1 Definition

The molecular formula gives the actual number of atoms of each element present in one molecule of a compound.

It is related to the empirical formula through a whole-number multiplier.

\[
\boxed{\text{Molecular formula}=(\text{Empirical formula})_n}
\]

Where:

\[
\boxed{n=\frac{\text{Molecular molar mass}}{\text{Empirical formula mass}}}
\]

The value of \(n\) must be a positive whole number.

### 3.2 Worked Example

A compound has the empirical formula CH₂O and a molecular molar mass of 180 g mol⁻¹.

Determine its molecular formula.

**Step 1: Calculate empirical formula mass.**

\[
12+2(1)+16=30
\]

**Step 2: Calculate the multiplier.**

\[
n=\frac{180}{30}=6
\]

**Step 3: Multiply all subscripts by six.**

\[
(CH_2O)_6
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{C_6H_{12}O_6}
\]

### 3.3 Relationship Between Empirical and Molecular Formulae

The molecular formula is always a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.

Examples:

| Empirical formula | Multiplier | Molecular formula |
|---|---:|---|
| CH₂O | 6 | C₆H₁₂O₆ |
| CH | 6 | C₆H₆ |
| HO | 2 | H₂O₂ |
| CH₂ | 2 | C₂H₄ |

A compound's empirical and molecular formulae may also be identical when the multiplier is one.

## 4. Determining Formulae from Experimental Data

### 4.1 Mass Data

If the mass of each element in a compound is known, convert each mass into moles and determine the simplest ratio.

### 4.2 Percentage Data

If percentages are provided, assume a 100 g sample and use the percentage values directly as masses.

### 4.3 Combustion Analysis

Combustion analysis is used to determine the carbon and hydrogen content of many organic compounds.

A known mass of a compound is completely burned in excess oxygen.

- Carbon is converted into CO₂.
- Hydrogen is converted into H₂O.

The quantities of CO₂ and H₂O produced can be used to calculate the original amounts of carbon and hydrogen.

For carbon:

\[
n(C)=n(CO_2)
\]

For hydrogen:

\[
n(H)=2n(H_2O)
\]

If oxygen is also present in the compound, its mass can be determined by subtracting the masses of carbon and hydrogen from the original sample mass, assuming the compound contains only these three elements.

### 4.4 Worked Example: Combustion Data

A compound containing carbon and hydrogen produces 4.4 g of CO₂ and 1.8 g of H₂O on complete combustion.

Determine the masses of carbon and hydrogen in the original sample.

Molar mass of CO₂:

\[
M(CO_2)=44
\]

Moles of CO₂:

\[
n(CO_2)=\frac{4.4}{44}=0.1
\]

Therefore, moles of carbon:

\[
n(C)=0.1
\]

Mass of carbon:

\[
m(C)=0.1(12)=1.2\,g
\]

Molar mass of water:

\[
M(H_2O)=18
\]

Moles of water:

\[
n(H_2O)=\frac{1.8}{18}=0.1
\]

Each water molecule contains two hydrogen atoms.

\[
n(H)=2(0.1)=0.2
\]

Mass of hydrogen:

\[
m(H)=0.2(1)=0.2\,g
\]

Therefore, the sample contains:

- Carbon = 1.2 g.
- Hydrogen = 0.2 g.

## 5. Hydrated Salts

### 5.1 Definition

Hydrated salts are crystalline compounds containing a definite number of water molecules incorporated into their crystal structures.

The water is called water of crystallization.

For example:

\[
CuSO_4\cdot5H_2O
\]

This formula represents copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate.

It contains five water molecules per formula unit of copper(II) sulfate.

### 5.2 Anhydrous Salts

A salt without water of crystallization is called an anhydrous salt.

For example, heating hydrated copper(II) sulfate removes its water of crystallization:

\[
CuSO_4\cdot5H_2O
\xrightarrow{\text{heat}}
CuSO_4+5H_2O
\]

Hydrated copper(II) sulfate is blue, while anhydrous copper(II) sulfate is white.

### 5.3 Determining Water of Crystallization

The number of water molecules can be determined experimentally by heating a known mass of hydrated salt and measuring the mass lost.

\[
\text{Mass of water}=\text{Initial mass}-\text{Final mass}
\]

Then:

\[
n(H_2O)=\frac{\text{Mass of water}}{18}
\]

The moles of anhydrous salt are calculated using its molar mass.

The ratio gives the number of water molecules per formula unit.

## 6. Importance and Applications

### 6.1 Chemical Identification

Empirical and molecular formulae help identify unknown compounds from experimental composition data.

### 6.2 Pharmaceutical Chemistry

Composition calculations help determine the elemental and molecular composition of medicines and chemical substances.

### 6.3 Materials Chemistry

Percentage composition is useful for checking the composition of alloys, minerals, ceramics, and industrial materials.

### 6.4 Analytical Chemistry

Combustion analysis and quantitative elemental analysis help determine the formulae of unknown substances.

## 7. Common Exam Traps

1. **The empirical formula is the simplest ratio, not necessarily the actual molecular composition.**
2. **Molecular formulae are whole-number multiples of empirical formulae.**
3. **Always convert mass or percentage data into moles before determining an empirical formula.**
4. **Do not divide mole ratios by the largest value.** Divide by the smallest.
5. **Multiply fractional ratios carefully to obtain whole numbers.**
6. **In combustion analysis, one mole of CO₂ contains one mole of carbon atoms.**
7. **One mole of H₂O contains two moles of hydrogen atoms.**
8. **Water of crystallization contributes to the formula mass of a hydrated salt.**

| Concept | Important point |
