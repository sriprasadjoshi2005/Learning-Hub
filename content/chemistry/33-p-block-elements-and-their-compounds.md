# Lesson 33: p-Block Elements and Their Compounds

The p-block contains elements from Groups 13 to 18. Their valence electrons occupy p-orbitals, and the block includes metals, metalloids and non-metals.

The p-block is chemically diverse. Its elements form covalent compounds, ionic compounds, oxides, oxyacids, hydrides and numerous industrially important substances.

## 1. General Electronic Configuration

The general valence-shell electronic configuration is:

\[
ns^2np^{1-6}
\]

The main groups are:

| Group | Family | General valence configuration |
|---|---|---|
| 13 | Boron family | \(ns^2np^1\) |
| 14 | Carbon family | \(ns^2np^2\) |
| 15 | Nitrogen family | \(ns^2np^3\) |
| 16 | Oxygen family | \(ns^2np^4\) |
| 17 | Halogens | \(ns^2np^5\) |
| 18 | Noble gases | \(ns^2np^6\) |

Helium is an exception with configuration \(1s^2\).

## 2. General Periodic Trends

### 2.1 Atomic Size

Across a period, atomic size generally decreases because effective nuclear charge increases.

Down a group, atomic size generally increases because new electron shells are added.

### 2.2 Ionisation Enthalpy

Ionisation enthalpy generally increases across a period and decreases down a group.

Exceptions occur because of differences in subshell stability and electron pairing.

### 2.3 Electronegativity

Electronegativity generally increases across a period and decreases down a group.

Fluorine is the most electronegative element.

### 2.4 Metallic Character

Metallic character generally decreases across a period and increases down a group.

The p-block includes metallic elements such as aluminium, metalloids such as silicon, and non-metals such as oxygen and chlorine.

## 3. Group 13: Boron Family

Group 13 includes boron, aluminium, gallium, indium and thallium.

Their valence configuration is:

\[
ns^2np^1
\]

The common oxidation state is \(+3\), while the stability of the \(+1\) state increases down the group due to the inert pair effect.

### 3.1 Boron

Boron is a metalloid and generally forms covalent compounds.

It has a high melting point and forms electron-deficient compounds.

Examples include:

\[
BF_3,\quad BCl_3,\quad B_2H_6
\]

### 3.2 Boric Acid

Boric acid has the formula:

\[
H_3BO_3
\]

It behaves as a Lewis acid by accepting an electron pair through reaction with hydroxide ions.

\[
B(OH)_3+OH^-\rightarrow [B(OH)_4]^-
\]

### 3.3 Aluminium

Aluminium is a lightweight metal widely used in transport, construction and electrical applications.

Its oxide and hydroxide are amphoteric.

\[
Al_2O_3+6HCl\rightarrow 2AlCl_3+3H_2O
\]

\[
Al_2O_3+2NaOH+3H_2O
\rightarrow 2Na[Al(OH)_4]
\]

## 4. Group 14: Carbon Family

Group 14 includes carbon, silicon, germanium, tin and lead.

Its valence configuration is:

\[
ns^2np^2
\]

The common oxidation states are \(+4\) and \(+2\). The stability of the \(+2\) state increases down the group due to the inert pair effect.

### 4.1 Carbon

Carbon exhibits extensive catenation, meaning the ability to form bonds with other carbon atoms.

This allows it to form chains, branches and rings.

Carbon occurs in several allotropes.

- Diamond: each carbon is bonded tetrahedrally to four others.
- Graphite: carbon atoms form layered hexagonal sheets.
- Graphene: a single layer of graphite.
- Fullerenes: cage-like carbon structures.

### 4.2 Silicon

Silicon is a metalloid and a major component of Earth's crust.

Silicon dioxide has a giant covalent network structure.

\[
SiO_2
\]

It is used in glass, ceramics and semiconductor technology.

## 5. Group 15: Nitrogen Family

Group 15 includes nitrogen, phosphorus, arsenic, antimony and bismuth.

Its valence configuration is:

\[
ns^2np^3
\]

Common oxidation states include \(-3\), \(+3\) and \(+5\).

### 5.1 Nitrogen

Nitrogen exists as a diatomic molecule.

\[
N_2
\]

Its triple bond gives it high bond strength and relatively low chemical reactivity under ordinary conditions.

Industrial ammonia production uses the Haber process.

\[
N_2+3H_2\rightleftharpoons 2NH_3
\]

### 5.2 Ammonia

Ammonia has a trigonal pyramidal molecular geometry.

It acts as a Lewis base because nitrogen has a lone pair.

It reacts with acids to form ammonium salts.

\[
NH_3+HCl\rightarrow NH_4Cl
\]

### 5.3 Phosphorus

Important allotropes include white phosphorus and red phosphorus.

White phosphorus is highly reactive and toxic, while red phosphorus is comparatively less reactive.

Phosphorus forms several oxides and oxyacids, including:

\[
P_4O_6,\quad P_4O_{10},\quad H_3PO_4
\]

## 6. Group 16: Oxygen Family

Group 16 includes oxygen, sulfur, selenium, tellurium and polonium.

The valence configuration is:

\[
ns^2np^4
\]

Common oxidation states include \(-2\), \(+2\), \(+4\) and \(+6\), depending on the element and compound.

### 6.1 Oxygen

Oxygen commonly exists as \(O_2\), a paramagnetic molecule.

It supports combustion and is essential for aerobic respiration.

### 6.2 Ozone

Ozone has the formula:

\[
O_3
\]

It absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation in the stratosphere.

Ozone is a strong oxidising agent.

### 6.3 Sulfur

Sulfur exists in several allotropes, including rhombic and monoclinic sulfur.

It forms important compounds such as sulfur dioxide and sulfur trioxide.

\[
S+O_2\rightarrow SO_2
\]

\[
2SO_2+O_2\rightleftharpoons 2SO_3
\]

Sulfur trioxide is used in the manufacture of sulfuric acid.

## 7. Group 17: Halogens

Halogens include fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine and astatine.

Their general valence configuration is:

\[
ns^2np^5
\]

They generally form diatomic molecules and tend to gain one electron to form halide ions.

\[
X_2+2e^-\rightarrow 2X^-
\]

### 7.1 Physical Trends

Down the group:

- Atomic size increases.
- Melting and boiling points generally increase.
- Colour becomes darker.
- Oxidising power generally decreases.

Fluorine is the strongest oxidising halogen.

### 7.2 Displacement Reactions

A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halide from solution.

\[
Cl_2+2Br^-\rightarrow 2Cl^-+Br_2
\]

Chlorine can displace bromide and iodide ions, but bromine cannot displace chloride ions.

### 7.3 Hydrogen Halides

Hydrogen halides include:

\[
HF,\quad HCl,\quad HBr,\quad HI
\]

Their acid strength generally increases down the group because the H–X bond becomes weaker.

HF is a weak acid in water despite fluorine's high electronegativity because the H–F bond is exceptionally strong.

## 8. Group 18: Noble Gases

Noble gases include helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon.

They have filled valence shells and are generally unreactive.

Their common uses include:

- Helium in balloons and cryogenic applications.
- Neon in lighting.
- Argon in welding atmospheres.
- Xenon in specialised lamps and chemical compounds.

Some heavier noble gases, particularly xenon, form compounds with highly electronegative elements.

Examples include:

\[
XeF_2,\quad XeF_4,\quad XeF_6
\]

## 9. Oxides and Oxyacids

p-block elements form oxides with different acid-base properties.

- Non-metal oxides are often acidic.
- Metal oxides are often basic.
- Some oxides are amphoteric.

Examples:

| Oxide | Character |
|---|---|
| \(Na_2O\) | Basic |
| \(MgO\) | Basic |
| \(Al_2O_3\) | Amphoteric |
| \(CO_2\) | Acidic |
| \(SO_3\) | Acidic |

Oxyacids contain hydrogen, oxygen and another central element.

Examples include:

\[
H_2SO_4,\quad HNO_3,\quad H_3PO_4,\quad HClO_4
\]

For oxyacids of the same central element, acid strength generally increases with the number of oxygen atoms because additional oxygen atoms withdraw electron density and stabilise the conjugate base.

## 10. Interhalogen Compounds

Interhalogen compounds contain two different halogen elements.

Examples:

\[
ClF,\quad BrF_3,\quad IF_5,\quad IF_7
\]

They are generally more reactive than the constituent halogens, except fluorine.

Their common molecular shapes include linear, T-shaped, square pyramidal and pentagonal bipyramidal structures.

## 11. Common Mistakes and Exam Points

- The p-block includes metals, metalloids and non-metals.
- The inert pair effect becomes more significant down several p-block groups.
- Nitrogen is relatively unreactive because of its strong triple bond.
- Fluorine is the strongest oxidising halogen.
- Acid strength of hydrogen halides increases down Group 17.
- Noble gases are not absolutely incapable of forming compounds.
- Aluminium oxide is amphoteric.
- Ozone is an allotrope of oxygen.

## 12. Lesson Recap

- The p-block contains Groups 13–18.
- Its elements exhibit diverse oxidation states and bonding patterns.
- Carbon and silicon are important Group 14 elements.
- Nitrogen and phosphorus are important Group 15 elements.
- Oxygen and sulfur belong to Group 16.
- Halogens are reactive non-metals, while noble gases have filled valence shells.
- Oxides, oxyacids and interhalogen compounds are important classes of p-block compounds.

   - [ ] \(ns^2np^6\)

2. Which halogen is the strongest oxidising agent?
   - [x] Fluorine
   - [ ] Chlorine
   - [ ] Bromine
   - [ ] Iodine
   > Fluorine has the greatest tendency to gain electrons and is the strongest oxidising halogen.

   - [ ] \(CO_2\)
   - [ ] \(SO_3\)
   - [x] \(Al_2O_3\)
   - [ ] \(Na_2O\)
   > Aluminium oxide reacts with both acids and bases, so it is classified as amphoteric.
