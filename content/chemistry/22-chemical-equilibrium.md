# Lesson 22: Chemical Equilibrium

Chemical equilibrium describes the condition in which the forward and reverse reactions of a reversible process occur at equal rates. At equilibrium, the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant with time, even though both reactions continue at the molecular level. This concept is essential for understanding industrial reactions, biological processes, and the behaviour of chemical systems.

## 1. Introduction to Chemical Equilibrium

A chemical reaction may proceed in both forward and reverse directions.

For a reversible reaction:

\[
A+B\rightleftharpoons C+D
\]

- The forward reaction converts reactants into products.
- The reverse reaction converts products back into reactants.

### 1.1 Dynamic Nature of Equilibrium

Chemical equilibrium is dynamic because both forward and reverse reactions continue.

At equilibrium:

\[
\boxed{r_f=r_r}
\]

Where:
- \(r_f\) = forward reaction rate.
- \(r_r\) = reverse reaction rate.

The concentrations of reactants and products remain constant, but they are not necessarily equal.

### 1.2 Conditions for Equilibrium

- The reaction must be reversible.
- The system must be closed to prevent loss of reacting substances.
- Equilibrium is established when the forward and reverse reaction rates become equal.

## 2. Law of Mass Action

The law of mass action states that, at a given temperature, the rate of a reaction is proportional to the product of the active masses of the reactants, each raised to its stoichiometric coefficient.

For an elementary reaction:

\[
aA+bB\rightleftharpoons cC+dD
\]

The forward rate is:

\[
r_f=k_f[A]^a[B]^b
\]

The reverse rate is:

\[
r_r=k_r[C]^c[D]^d
\]

At equilibrium:

\[
k_f[A]^a[B]^b=k_r[C]^c[D]^d
\]

Rearranging:

\[
\boxed{
K_c=\frac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}
}
\]

For a general reaction, the equilibrium expression follows the balanced chemical equation. The elementary-reaction rate law should not automatically be assumed for a complex overall reaction.

## 3. Equilibrium Constant

The equilibrium constant expresses the relationship between product and reactant activities at equilibrium.

For many dilute solutions, concentrations can be used as approximations to activities.

### 3.1 Concentration Equilibrium Constant

For:

\[
aA+bB\rightleftharpoons cC+dD
\]

\[
\boxed{
K_c=\frac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}
}
\]

The concentrations are measured at equilibrium.

### 3.2 Equilibrium Constant in Terms of Pressure

For gaseous reactions:

\[
\boxed{
K_p=\frac{(P_C)^c(P_D)^d}{(P_A)^a(P_B)^b}
}
\]

Where P represents the equilibrium partial pressure.

### 3.3 Relationship Between \(K_p\) and \(K_c\)

For an ideal-gas reaction:

\[
\boxed{K_p=K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}}
\]

Where:

\[
\Delta n_g=
\text{gaseous product coefficients}
-
\text{gaseous reactant coefficients}
\]

### 3.4 Pure Solids and Liquids

Pure solids and pure liquids are omitted from equilibrium expressions because their activities are treated as constant.

For example:

\[
CaCO_3(s)\rightleftharpoons CaO(s)+CO_2(g)
\]

The equilibrium expression is:

\[
\boxed{K_p=P_{CO_2}}
\]

## 4. Magnitude of the Equilibrium Constant

The magnitude of K indicates the relative position of equilibrium.

| Value of K | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| \(K\gg1\) | Products are favoured |
| \(K\ll1\) | Reactants are favoured |
| \(K\approx1\) | Neither side is overwhelmingly favoured |

A large equilibrium constant does not necessarily mean the reaction is fast.

Equilibrium constants describe thermodynamic composition, not reaction speed.

## 5. Reaction Quotient

The reaction quotient Q has the same mathematical form as the equilibrium constant, but it is calculated using the current concentrations or pressures rather than necessarily equilibrium values.

For:

\[
aA+bB\rightleftharpoons cC+dD
\]

\[
Q_c=\frac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}
\]

The comparison between Q and K predicts the direction in which a system moves to reach equilibrium.

| Comparison | Direction |
|---|---|
| \(Q<K\) | Forward reaction |
| \(Q>K\) | Reverse reaction |
| \(Q=K\) | System is at equilibrium |

## 6. Le Chatelier's Principle

Le Chatelier's principle states that when a system at equilibrium is disturbed, the equilibrium shifts in the direction that partially opposes the disturbance.

### 6.1 Effect of Concentration

For:

\[
N_2+3H_2\rightleftharpoons2NH_3
\]

- Adding nitrogen shifts equilibrium towards ammonia.
- Adding hydrogen shifts equilibrium towards ammonia.
- Removing ammonia shifts equilibrium towards ammonia.
- Adding ammonia shifts equilibrium towards the reactants.

Changing concentration generally changes Q and causes the system to move towards equilibrium again.

### 6.2 Effect of Pressure and Volume

For gaseous equilibria:
- Increasing pressure by decreasing volume favours the side with fewer moles of gas.
- Decreasing pressure by increasing volume favours the side with more moles of gas.
- If both sides have equal gaseous mole counts, a pressure change does not shift equilibrium.

Consider:

\[
N_2(g)+3H_2(g)\rightleftharpoons2NH_3(g)
\]

The reactant side has four gaseous moles, while the product side has two.

Increasing pressure favours ammonia formation.

### 6.3 Effect of Temperature

Temperature changes can alter the equilibrium constant.

For an exothermic forward reaction:

\[
\text{Reactants}\rightleftharpoons\text{Products}+\text{Heat}
\]

Increasing temperature favours the reverse reaction.

For an endothermic forward reaction:

\[
\text{Reactants}+\text{Heat}\rightleftharpoons\text{Products}
\]

Increasing temperature favours the forward reaction.

### 6.4 Effect of a Catalyst

A catalyst:
- Increases the rates of forward and reverse reactions.
- Helps equilibrium be reached faster.
- Does not change the equilibrium constant.
- Does not change the equilibrium composition.

## 7. Industrial Application: Haber Process

The Haber process produces ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen.

\[
N_2(g)+3H_2(g)\rightleftharpoons2NH_3(g)
\]

\[
\Delta H<0
\]

Important conditions:
- Elevated pressure.
- Moderate temperature.
- Iron-based catalyst.

High pressure favours the side with fewer gaseous molecules.

A moderate temperature provides a practical compromise between equilibrium yield and reaction rate.

The catalyst increases the rate at which equilibrium is reached but does not change the equilibrium position.

## 8. Changing the Chemical Equation

The equilibrium constant changes when the balanced chemical equation is modified.

### 8.1 Reversing a Reaction

If:

\[
A\rightleftharpoons B
\]

has equilibrium constant K, then:

\[
B\rightleftharpoons A
\]

has:

\[
\boxed{K'=\frac{1}{K}}
\]

### 8.2 Multiplying the Equation

If every coefficient is multiplied by n:

\[
\boxed{K'=K^n}
\]

### 8.3 Adding Reactions

When two reactions are added, their equilibrium constants are multiplied:

\[
\boxed{K_{\text{overall}}=K_1K_2}
\]

## 9. Common Exam Traps

- Equilibrium does not mean the reaction has stopped.
- Reactant and product concentrations are not necessarily equal.
- A catalyst does not change K.
- Only temperature changes the equilibrium constant for a given reaction.
- Pure solids and liquids are omitted from equilibrium expressions.
- A large K indicates product-favoured equilibrium, not necessarily a fast reaction.
- Pressure changes matter only when gaseous mole counts differ between the two sides.

## 10. Applications of Chemical Equilibrium

- Industrial ammonia production.
- Manufacture of sulphuric acid.
- Gas-phase chemical processes.
- Biological oxygen transport.
- Understanding reversible reactions in nature.
- Designing chemical reactors.
