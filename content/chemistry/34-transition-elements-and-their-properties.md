# Lesson 34: Transition Elements and Their Properties

Transition elements occupy the d-block of the periodic table. They are characterised by partially filled d-orbitals in their atoms or in at least one of their common oxidation states.

They display variable oxidation states, coloured compounds, catalytic activity, complex formation and magnetic behaviour. These properties make them important in chemistry and industrial applications.

## 1. Position and Electronic Configuration

The d-block occupies Groups 3–12.

The general electronic configuration is:

\[
(n-1)d^{1-10}ns^{0-2}
\]

The first transition series runs from scandium to zinc.

Examples:

| Element | Electronic configuration |
|---|---|
| Sc | \([Ar]3d^14s^2\) |
| Ti | \([Ar]3d^24s^2\) |
| Fe | \([Ar]3d^64s^2\) |
| Cu | \([Ar]3d^{10}4s^1\) |
| Zn | \([Ar]3d^{10}4s^2\) |

Chromium and copper show exceptional configurations because half-filled and fully filled d-subshells provide additional stability.

\[
Cr:[Ar]3d^54s^1
\]

\[
Cu:[Ar]3d^{10}4s^1
\]

## 2. What Is a Transition Element?

A transition element is an element whose atom has an incomplete d-subshell or which forms at least one stable ion with an incomplete d-subshell.

Zinc, cadmium and mercury are generally excluded from the strict definition because their common ions have completely filled d-subshells.

For example:

\[
Zn^{2+}:[Ar]3d^{10}
\]

Iron is a transition element because:

\[
Fe^{2+}:[Ar]3d^6
\]

## 3. General Physical Properties

Transition metals generally exhibit:

- High melting and boiling points.
- High densities.
- Good electrical and thermal conductivity.
- Hardness and strength.
- Metallic lustre.

These properties are related to metallic bonding involving valence electrons.

There are exceptions, such as mercury, which is liquid at room temperature.

## 4. Variable Oxidation States

Transition elements can lose electrons from both ns and (n-1)d orbitals.

This allows them to show multiple oxidation states.

For example, iron commonly forms:

\[
Fe^{2+},\quad Fe^{3+}
\]

Manganese exhibits several oxidation states, including:

\[
+2,\quad +4,\quad +6,\quad +7
\]

The availability of different oxidation states makes transition elements useful in redox chemistry.

### 4.1 Why Are Multiple Oxidation States Possible?

The energies of the ns and (n-1)d orbitals are relatively close.

Therefore, different numbers of electrons can participate in bonding or be removed during ion formation.

## 5. Coloured Ions and Compounds

Many transition metal ions are coloured because of electronic transitions between split d-orbitals.

When light is absorbed, an electron can move from one d-orbital energy level to another.

The remaining transmitted or reflected light gives the compound its observed colour.

Examples:

| Ion | Typical appearance |
|---|---|
| \(Cu^{2+}\) | Blue |
| \(Fe^{2+}\) | Pale green |
| \(Fe^{3+}\) | Yellow to brown |
| \(Cr^{3+}\) | Green or violet |
| \(MnO_4^-\) | Purple |

The exact colour depends on oxidation state, ligand environment and geometry.

Ions with \(d^0\) or \(d^{10}\) configurations generally do not show d-d transitions.

## 6. Magnetic Properties

Magnetic behaviour depends on the number of unpaired electrons.

### 6.1 Paramagnetism

Substances with one or more unpaired electrons are attracted by an external magnetic field.

They are paramagnetic.

### 6.2 Diamagnetism

Substances with all electrons paired are weakly repelled by a magnetic field.

They are diamagnetic.

### 6.3 Spin-Only Magnetic Moment

For many transition metal ions, the spin-only magnetic moment is estimated using:

\[
\mu=\sqrt{n(n+2)}\ BM
\]

where:

- \(n\) = number of unpaired electrons.
- \(BM\) = Bohr magneton.

For an ion with four unpaired electrons:

\[
\mu=\sqrt{4(4+2)}
\]

\[
\mu=\sqrt{24}\approx4.90\ BM
\]

## 7. Catalytic Properties

Transition metals and their compounds often act as catalysts.

Their ability to show variable oxidation states and adsorb reactants contributes to catalytic activity.

Examples:

- Iron in the Haber process.
- Vanadium(V) oxide in the Contact process.
- Nickel in hydrogenation reactions.
- Platinum and palladium in catalytic converters.

A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy.

## 8. Formation of Complexes

Transition metal ions readily form coordination complexes with ligands.

A ligand is an ion or molecule that donates an electron pair to a central metal atom or ion.

Examples of ligands include:

\[
H_2O,\quad NH_3,\quad Cl^-,\quad CN^-
\]

Examples of complexes:

\[
[Cu(NH_3)_4]^{2+}
\]

\[
[Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}
\]

Complex formation is favoured by the availability of suitable orbitals and the relatively high charge density of many transition metal ions.

## 9. Alloy Formation

Transition metals often form alloys with one another.

An alloy is a metallic material containing two or more elements, at least one of which is a metal.

Examples:

- Stainless steel: iron, chromium and often nickel.
- Brass: copper and zinc.
- Bronze: copper and tin.

Alloying can improve hardness, strength, corrosion resistance and other properties.

## 10. Important Compounds

### 10.1 Potassium Dichromate

Potassium dichromate:

\[
K_2Cr_2O_7
\]

It is a strong oxidising agent in acidic medium.

The dichromate ion is orange.

### 10.2 Potassium Permanganate

Potassium permanganate:

\[
KMnO_4
\]

It is a strong oxidising agent. The permanganate ion is purple.

In acidic medium, it is reduced to \(Mn^{2+}\).

\[
MnO_4^-+8H^++5e^-\rightarrow Mn^{2+}+4H_2O
\]

### 10.3 Iron Oxides

Iron forms several oxides, including:

\[
FeO,\quad Fe_2O_3,\quad Fe_3O_4
\]

Iron oxides occur in minerals and are important in metallurgy.

## 11. Lanthanoids and Actinoids: Introduction

The f-block elements are placed separately below the main periodic table.

### 11.1 Lanthanoids

Lanthanoids involve filling of the 4f subshell.

They commonly show the \(+3\) oxidation state.

They are used in magnets, phosphors, glass polishing and specialised electronic materials.

### 11.2 Actinoids

Actinoids involve filling of the 5f subshell.

Many are radioactive.

Examples include uranium and plutonium.

They exhibit a wider range of oxidation states than lanthanoids.

## 12. Common Mistakes and Exam Points

- A d-block element is not automatically a transition element.
- Zinc is not a transition element under the strict definition.
- Variable oxidation states arise from the similar energies of ns and (n-1)d orbitals.
- Unpaired electrons produce paramagnetism.
- The spin-only magnetic moment depends on the number of unpaired electrons.
- Coloured d-block ions often involve d-d transitions.
- Transition metals are frequently used as catalysts.
- Lanthanoids and actinoids are f-block elements.

## 13. Lesson Recap

- Transition elements are characterised by partially filled d-orbitals.
- They show variable oxidation states, coloured compounds and magnetic behaviour.
- They form complexes and alloys.
- Their catalytic properties are industrially important.
- Lanthanoids and actinoids are the two major f-block series.
