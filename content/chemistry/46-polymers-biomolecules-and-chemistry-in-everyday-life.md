# Lesson 46: Polymers, Biomolecules and Chemistry in Everyday Life

Chemistry is closely connected to biological systems, medicines, food, clothing, packaging, and household materials. This lesson examines polymers, important biomolecules, pharmaceutical chemistry, and the chemical principles behind everyday products.

## 1. Polymers

A polymer is a large molecule formed by joining many smaller repeating units called monomers.

\[
\text{Monomers}\rightarrow\text{Polymer}
\]

The repeating unit is the structural unit that appears repeatedly along the polymer chain.

### 1.1 Classification by Origin

- Natural polymers: cellulose, starch, proteins, natural rubber.
- Semi-synthetic polymers: chemically modified natural polymers such as cellulose acetate.
- Synthetic polymers: polyethylene, PVC, nylon, and polystyrene.

### 1.2 Classification by Structure

- Linear polymers: long, relatively straight chains.
- Branched polymers: chains with side branches.
- Cross-linked polymers: chains joined through covalent links.

The structure influences density, flexibility, strength, melting behaviour, and resistance to deformation.

## 2. Polymerisation

### 2.1 Addition Polymerisation

Unsaturated monomers join without eliminating small molecules.

Example: polyethylene formation.

\[
nCH_2=CH_2\rightarrow[-CH_2-CH_2-]_n
\]

Other examples include:
- Polypropylene from propene.
- PVC from vinyl chloride.
- Polystyrene from styrene.
- PTFE from tetrafluoroethene.

### 2.2 Condensation Polymerisation

Monomers containing two or more functional groups react with the elimination of small molecules, often water or HCl.

Example: formation of a polyamide from a diamine and a dicarboxylic acid.

\[
nH_2N-R-NH_2+nHOOC-R'-COOH
\]

The reaction forms amide linkages with elimination of water.

Nylon-6,6 is formed from hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid.

### 2.3 Copolymers

A copolymer contains two or more different types of monomer units.

Examples include:
- Buna-S: butadiene and styrene.
- Buna-N: butadiene and acrylonitrile.

## 3. Important Commercial Polymers

| Polymer | Monomer or starting materials | Common application |
|---|---|---|
| Polyethylene | Ethene | Packaging and containers |
| Polypropylene | Propene | Fibres and household products |
| PVC | Vinyl chloride | Pipes and insulation |
| Polystyrene | Styrene | Packaging and insulation |
| PTFE | Tetrafluoroethene | Non-stick coatings |
| Nylon-6,6 | Diamine and dicarboxylic acid | Fibres and engineering parts |
| Bakelite | Phenol and formaldehyde | Electrical components |

### 3.1 Thermoplastics

Thermoplastics soften on heating and can generally be reshaped.

Examples: polyethylene, polypropylene, and PVC.

### 3.2 Thermosetting Polymers

Thermosetting polymers form extensive cross-linked structures and cannot be remoulded easily after curing.

Examples: Bakelite and many epoxy resins.

### 3.3 Elastomers

Elastomers are polymers that can undergo substantial reversible stretching.

Examples: natural rubber and synthetic rubber.

## 4. Biomolecules

Biomolecules are compounds produced by or associated with living organisms.

Major categories include:
- Carbohydrates
- Proteins
- Lipids
- Nucleic acids
- Vitamins

## 5. Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates contain carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen and include sugars and polysaccharides.

### 5.1 Monosaccharides

Monosaccharides are simple sugars that cannot be hydrolysed into smaller carbohydrates.

Examples:
- Glucose
- Fructose
- Galactose

Glucose has the molecular formula:

\[
C_6H_{12}O_6
\]

### 5.2 Disaccharides

Disaccharides form when two monosaccharides join through a glycosidic bond.

| Disaccharide | Components |
|---|---|
| Sucrose | Glucose + fructose |
| Maltose | Glucose + glucose |
| Lactose | Glucose + galactose |

The formation of a glycosidic bond generally involves the elimination of water.

### 5.3 Polysaccharides

Polysaccharides are long carbohydrate chains.

- Starch: energy storage in plants.
- Glycogen: energy storage in animals.
- Cellulose: structural component of plant cell walls.

Starch and glycogen are important energy-storage molecules, while cellulose has a structural role.

## 6. Proteins

Proteins are polymers of amino acids joined through peptide bonds.

An amino acid generally contains:

\[
H_2N-CH(R)-COOH
\]

where \(R\) represents the side chain.

### 6.1 Peptide Bond Formation

The carboxyl group of one amino acid reacts with the amino group of another.

\[
\text{Amino acid}+\text{Amino acid}
\rightarrow\text{Dipeptide}+H_2O
\]

The resulting linkage is:

\[
-CO-NH-
\]

### 6.2 Levels of Protein Structure

- Primary structure: amino acid sequence.
- Secondary structure: local arrangements such as alpha helices and beta sheets.
- Tertiary structure: overall three-dimensional folding.
- Quaternary structure: arrangement of multiple polypeptide subunits.

### 6.3 Denaturation

Denaturation is the disruption of a protein's normal three-dimensional structure.

It may be caused by:
- High temperature
- Extreme pH
- Certain solvents
- Heavy metal ions

Denaturation usually affects biological activity without necessarily breaking the peptide bonds of the primary structure.

## 7. Lipids

Lipids are a broad group of mostly water-insoluble biological molecules.

Important examples include:
- Fats and oils
- Phospholipids
- Steroids
- Waxes

### 7.1 Triglycerides

Triglycerides are esters formed from glycerol and three fatty acids.

They are important energy-storage molecules.

### 7.2 Saturated and Unsaturated Fatty Acids

- Saturated fatty acids contain no carbon–carbon double bonds.
- Unsaturated fatty acids contain one or more carbon–carbon double bonds.

Unsaturation generally introduces bends into fatty acid chains, affecting packing and physical properties.

## 8. Nucleic Acids

Nucleic acids store and transmit genetic information.

### 8.1 DNA

DNA contains:
- Deoxyribose sugar
- Phosphate groups
- Nitrogenous bases A, T, G, and C

Base pairing:

\[
A-T,\qquad G-C
\]

### 8.2 RNA

RNA generally contains ribose and the bases A, U, G, and C.

Important forms include messenger RNA, transfer RNA, and ribosomal RNA.

## 9. Vitamins

Vitamins are organic compounds required in small amounts for normal biological functioning.

### 9.1 Fat-Soluble Vitamins

- Vitamin A
- Vitamin D
- Vitamin E
- Vitamin K

### 9.2 Water-Soluble Vitamins

- Vitamin C
- B-group vitamins

Fat-soluble vitamins can be stored in body tissues, while many water-soluble vitamins require more regular dietary intake.

## 10. Chemistry of Medicines

Medicines interact with biological systems to prevent, diagnose, or treat disease.

### 10.1 Analgesics

Analgesics reduce pain.

Examples include paracetamol and ibuprofen.

### 10.2 Antipyretics

Antipyretics reduce fever. Paracetamol is a commonly used example.

### 10.3 Antibiotics

Antibiotics act against bacterial infections.

Examples include penicillin and amoxicillin.

Antibiotics do not treat viral infections such as the common cold.

### 10.4 Antacids

Antacids neutralise excess stomach acid.

Examples include magnesium hydroxide and calcium carbonate.

\[
Mg(OH)_2+2HCl\rightarrow MgCl_2+2H_2O
\]

### 10.5 Antihistamines

Antihistamines block or reduce the effects of histamine at relevant receptors and are used to manage allergic symptoms.

## 11. Soap and Detergent Chemistry

Soaps and detergents contain molecules with:
- A hydrophilic head that interacts with water.
- A hydrophobic tail that interacts with oils and grease.

They help disperse oily substances in water by forming aggregates such as micelles.

Soap is generally a sodium or potassium salt of a long-chain fatty acid.

Detergents are synthetic surfactants and can be designed to work effectively in harder water.

## 12. Common Exam Traps

- Addition polymerisation generally does not eliminate small molecules.
- Condensation polymerisation often eliminates water or another small molecule.
- Nylon-6,6 is a polyamide.
- Cellulose is structural; starch and glycogen are storage polysaccharides.
- Peptide bonds join amino acids.
- Denaturation does not necessarily break peptide bonds.
- DNA contains thymine; RNA generally contains uracil.
- Antibiotics act against bacteria, not viruses.
- Soap forms insoluble salts with certain calcium and magnesium ions in hard water.

## 13. Summary

- Polymers are large molecules built from repeating monomer units.
- Addition and condensation polymerisation are two major polymer-forming processes.
- Carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids are major biomolecule groups.
- Medicines are designed to interact with biological targets.
- Soaps and detergents use their hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions to remove grease.
