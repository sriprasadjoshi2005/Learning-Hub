# Lesson 15: Hydrogen Bonding and Intermolecular Forces

Molecules interact with one another through attractive and repulsive forces. These interactions are called intermolecular forces when they act between separate molecules. Although they are generally weaker than chemical bonds within molecules, they strongly influence physical properties such as boiling point, melting point, viscosity, surface tension, and solubility.

Hydrogen bonding is a particularly important type of intermolecular attraction. It explains several unusual properties of water, the structure of biological molecules, and the behaviour of many substances containing hydrogen bonded to highly electronegative atoms.

## 1. Intramolecular and Intermolecular Forces

### 1.1 Intramolecular Forces

Intramolecular forces are the chemical bonds that hold atoms together within a molecule or compound.

Examples include:
- Covalent bonds in water.
- Ionic attractions in sodium chloride.
- Metallic bonding in metals.

These forces determine the chemical structure of a substance.

### 1.2 Intermolecular Forces

Intermolecular forces are attractions between separate molecules or particles.

Examples include:
- London dispersion forces.
- Dipole-dipole interactions.
- Hydrogen bonding.
- Ion-dipole interactions.

They influence how molecules behave collectively in liquids, gases, and molecular solids.

### 1.3 Comparison

| Feature | Intramolecular forces | Intermolecular forces |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Within a molecule or structure | Between separate particles |
| Main role | Hold atoms or ions together | Hold molecules near one another |
| Typical strength | Generally stronger | Generally weaker |
| Examples | Covalent and ionic bonding | Dispersion and hydrogen bonding |

## 2. Types of Intermolecular Forces

### 2.1 London Dispersion Forces

London dispersion forces arise from temporary fluctuations in electron distribution.

At any instant, electrons may be distributed unevenly around an atom or molecule. This creates a temporary dipole, which can induce a dipole in a neighbouring particle.

The resulting attraction is called a London dispersion force.

These forces occur in all atoms and molecules, including non-polar substances.

Examples:
- Helium.
- Neon.
- Oxygen.
- Nitrogen.
- Methane.
- Iodine.

### 2.2 Factors Affecting Dispersion Forces

**1. Number of electrons**

Larger electron clouds are generally more polarizable. Their electron distributions can be distorted more easily, producing stronger dispersion forces.

**2. Molecular size**

For similar types of molecules, dispersion forces generally increase with molecular size.

For example, the boiling points of noble gases generally increase down the group:

\[
He<Ne<Ar<Kr<Xe
\]

**3. Molecular shape**

Molecular shape affects how closely molecules can approach and how much surface area can interact.

For molecules with similar molecular masses, elongated molecules can sometimes experience stronger dispersion attractions than compact, spherical molecules.

### 2.3 Dipole-Dipole Interactions

Dipole-dipole interactions occur between polar molecules.

A polar molecule has a permanent dipole, meaning it possesses regions of partial positive and partial negative charge.

The partially positive region of one molecule attracts the partially negative region of another.

Example: Hydrogen chloride.

\[
H^{\delta+}-Cl^{\delta-}
\]

Neighbouring HCl molecules experience attractions between their oppositely charged regions.

Dipole-dipole interactions become more significant when molecular dipole moments are larger and molecular arrangements allow favourable orientations.

### 2.4 Dipole-Induced Dipole Interactions

A polar molecule can distort the electron cloud of a nearby non-polar molecule.

This produces an induced dipole and creates an attractive interaction.

These forces are called dipole-induced dipole interactions.

They help explain why some non-polar substances can dissolve to a limited extent in polar solvents.

### 2.5 Ion-Dipole Interactions

Ion-dipole interactions occur between an ion and a polar molecule.

For example, when sodium chloride dissolves in water:

- The partially negative oxygen end of water attracts Na⁺.
- The partially positive hydrogen ends of water attract Cl⁻.

These interactions help stabilize separated ions in aqueous solution.

Ion-dipole interactions are important in:
- Dissolution of ionic compounds.
- Electrolyte solutions.
- Biological fluids.
- Ion transport.

## 3. Hydrogen Bonding

### 3.1 Definition

Hydrogen bonding is a strong, directional intermolecular attraction involving a hydrogen atom bonded to a highly electronegative atom and a lone pair on another electronegative atom.

The common atoms involved are:

- Nitrogen (N).
- Oxygen (O).
- Fluorine (F).

A typical hydrogen bond is represented as:

\[
X-H\cdots Y
\]

Where:
- X is an electronegative atom bonded to hydrogen.
- H is the hydrogen atom.
- Y is an electronegative atom with an available lone pair.

The dotted line represents the hydrogen bond.

### 3.2 Conditions for Hydrogen Bonding

Hydrogen bonding generally requires:

1. Hydrogen covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom, usually N, O, or F.
2. A nearby electronegative atom with an available lone pair.
3. Suitable molecular geometry for the interaction.

The high electronegativity of the bonded atom makes hydrogen strongly partially positive, allowing it to interact with a lone pair on another atom.

### 3.3 Intermolecular Hydrogen Bonding

Intermolecular hydrogen bonding occurs between different molecules.

For example, water molecules form hydrogen bonds with neighbouring water molecules.

\[
O-H\cdots O
\]

These interactions contribute to water's unusually high boiling point and surface tension.

Other examples include:
- Hydrogen fluoride.
- Ammonia.
- Alcohols.
- Carboxylic acids.

### 3.4 Intramolecular Hydrogen Bonding

Intramolecular hydrogen bonding occurs within the same molecule.

It is possible when suitable hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor groups are positioned close to each other.

For example, some substituted aromatic compounds form intramolecular hydrogen bonds between nearby functional groups.

Intramolecular hydrogen bonding can influence:
- Molecular shape.
- Stability.
- Acidity.
- Solubility.
- Boiling point.

## 4. Properties of Hydrogen Bonding

### 4.1 Directionality

Hydrogen bonds are directional interactions.

Their strength generally depends on the relative positions of the donor, hydrogen, and acceptor atoms.

A nearly linear arrangement:

\[
X-H\cdots Y
\]

often produces a stronger hydrogen bond than a significantly bent arrangement.

### 4.2 Strength

Hydrogen bonds are generally stronger than ordinary dipole-dipole interactions and London dispersion forces of comparable small molecules.

However, their strength varies widely depending on the chemical environment.

They are usually weaker than ordinary covalent bonds.

### 4.3 Influence on Physical Properties

Hydrogen bonding can increase:
- Boiling point.
- Melting point in some structures.
- Viscosity.
- Surface tension.
- Enthalpy of vaporization.

It can also influence solubility and molecular structure.

## 5. Hydrogen Bonding in Water

### 5.1 Structure of Water

A water molecule has two O—H covalent bonds and two lone pairs on oxygen.

Its molecular geometry is bent, with a bond angle of approximately:

\[
104.5^\circ
\]

Oxygen is more electronegative than hydrogen, making the O—H bonds polar.

Water molecules can therefore form hydrogen bonds with one another.

### 5.2 Unusually High Boiling Point

Water has a higher boiling point than expected for a molecule of its small molecular mass.

This is largely due to the extensive hydrogen-bonding network between water molecules.

Additional energy is needed to separate the molecules during vaporization.

### 5.3 High Surface Tension

Water molecules at the surface experience cohesive attractions from neighbouring molecules.

Hydrogen bonding contributes significantly to water's high surface tension.

This helps explain the formation of water droplets and the ability of some small insects to remain on the water surface.

### 5.4 Ice and Its Lower Density

In ordinary ice, hydrogen bonds arrange water molecules into an open crystalline structure.

This structure occupies more volume than the same mass of liquid water under suitable conditions.

Therefore, ice is less dense than liquid water and floats on it.

This property is important for aquatic ecosystems because floating ice can insulate the water below.

## 6. Hydrogen Bonding in Biological Systems

### 6.1 Proteins

Hydrogen bonds help stabilize secondary structures of proteins.

Examples include:
- Alpha helices.
- Beta-pleated sheets.

They form between suitable backbone groups, contributing to the three-dimensional arrangement of proteins.

### 6.2 DNA

Hydrogen bonds help hold complementary DNA bases together.

In the usual Watson-Crick base pairing:
- Adenine pairs with thymine through two hydrogen bonds.
- Guanine pairs with cytosine through three hydrogen bonds.

The sugar-phosphate backbones remain connected through covalent bonds.

### 6.3 Other Biological Molecules

Hydrogen bonding is also important in:
- Carbohydrate structures.
- Enzyme-substrate interactions.
- Molecular recognition.
- Interactions between water and biomolecules.

## 7. Comparison of Intermolecular Forces

| Interaction | Main cause | Typical example |
|---|---|---|
| London dispersion | Temporary and induced dipoles | Ar, I₂ |
| Dipole-dipole | Permanent molecular dipoles | HCl |
| Dipole-induced dipole | Permanent dipole induces another dipole | Polar molecule near a non-polar molecule |
| Hydrogen bonding | Attraction involving X—H and a lone-pair acceptor | H₂O, HF |
| Ion-dipole | Attraction between an ion and a polar molecule | Na⁺ interacting with water |

There is no universal strength order for every possible pair of substances because the strength of an interaction depends on molecular size, charge, geometry, and polarizability.

For many small molecules of comparable size, hydrogen bonding is stronger than ordinary dipole-dipole and dispersion interactions.

## 8. Intermolecular Forces and Physical Properties

### 8.1 Boiling Point

Boiling requires molecules to separate from one another and enter the gas phase.

Stronger intermolecular attractions generally require more energy to overcome.

Therefore, stronger intermolecular forces generally produce higher boiling points among comparable substances.

### 8.2 Melting Point

Melting involves disrupting the ordered arrangement of particles in a solid.

Intermolecular forces influence melting points, but crystal packing and molecular symmetry also play important roles.

### 8.3 Viscosity

Viscosity is the resistance of a fluid to flow.

Stronger molecular attractions can increase resistance to movement and therefore increase viscosity.

### 8.4 Surface Tension

Surface tension is the energy required to increase the surface area of a liquid per unit area, or equivalently the force per unit length acting along its surface.

Strong cohesive interactions generally increase surface tension.

### 8.5 Solubility

A useful principle is that substances with similar types of intermolecular interactions often dissolve well in one another.

Polar solvents generally dissolve many polar and ionic substances.

Non-polar solvents generally dissolve many non-polar substances.

However, actual solubility depends on the overall energetic and entropic changes during dissolution.

## 9. Common Exam Traps

1. **All molecules experience London dispersion forces.** Even polar molecules have dispersion interactions.
2. **Hydrogen bonding is not simply any attraction involving hydrogen.** It requires an appropriate donor and acceptor.
3. **Hydrogen must generally be bonded to N, O, or F for the common strong hydrogen-bonding cases studied at this level.**
4. **Intramolecular hydrogen bonding occurs within one molecule; intermolecular hydrogen bonding occurs between molecules.**
5. **Ice floats because its open crystal structure gives it a lower density than liquid water.**
6. **Boiling does not normally break the covalent O—H bonds in water.** It overcomes intermolecular attractions.
7. **A larger molecule can have stronger dispersion forces even if it is non-polar.**
8. **Molecular polarity and intermolecular force strength are not determined by electronegativity difference alone.**
