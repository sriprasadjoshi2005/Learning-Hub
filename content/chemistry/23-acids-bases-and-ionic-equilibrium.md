# Lesson 23: Acids, Bases, and Ionic Equilibrium

Acids and bases are important classes of chemical substances involved in neutralisation, salt formation, biological regulation, and industrial reactions. Their behaviour in aqueous solutions is explained through ionisation and ionic equilibrium. This lesson introduces major acid-base theories, strong and weak electrolytes, ionisation constants, and the pH scale.

## 1. Introduction to Acids and Bases

Acids and bases can be identified through their chemical behaviour.

Common examples of acids:
- Hydrochloric acid (HCl).
- Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄).
- Nitric acid (HNO₃).
- Acetic acid (CH₃COOH).

Common examples of bases:
- Sodium hydroxide (NaOH).
- Potassium hydroxide (KOH).
- Calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂).
- Ammonia (NH₃).

## 2. Theories of Acids and Bases

### 2.1 Arrhenius Theory

According to the Arrhenius theory:
- An acid increases the concentration of hydrogen ions in aqueous solution.
- A base increases the concentration of hydroxide ions in aqueous solution.

Examples:

\[
HCl(aq)\rightarrow H^+(aq)+Cl^-(aq)
\]

\[
NaOH(aq)\rightarrow Na^+(aq)+OH^-(aq)
\]

In water, hydrogen ions are associated with water molecules, commonly represented as hydronium ions:

\[
H^++H_2O\rightarrow H_3O^+
\]

### 2.2 Brønsted-Lowry Theory

According to this theory:
- An acid is a proton donor.
- A base is a proton acceptor.

Example:

\[
HCl+H_2O\rightarrow H_3O^++Cl^-
\]

HCl donates a proton and acts as an acid.

Water accepts the proton and acts as a base.

### 2.3 Conjugate Acid-Base Pairs

When an acid donates a proton, it forms its conjugate base.

When a base accepts a proton, it forms its conjugate acid.

For:

\[
NH_3+H_2O\rightleftharpoons NH_4^++OH^-
\]

The conjugate pairs are:
- NH₄⁺ and NH₃.
- H₂O and OH⁻.

### 2.4 Lewis Theory

According to Lewis theory:
- A Lewis acid accepts an electron pair.
- A Lewis base donates an electron pair.

Examples:
- BF₃ is a Lewis acid.
- NH₃ is a Lewis base.

\[
BF_3+NH_3\rightarrow F_3B\leftarrow NH_3
\]

Lewis theory includes many reactions that are not limited to proton transfer.

## 3. Strong and Weak Acids and Bases

### 3.1 Strong Acids

Strong acids ionise almost completely in dilute aqueous solution.

Examples:
- HCl.
- HNO₃.
- HBr.
- HI.
- HClO₄.

Sulphuric acid has a strongly ionising first dissociation step; its second dissociation is not complete under ordinary conditions.

### 3.2 Weak Acids

Weak acids ionise only partially in water.

Example:

\[
CH_3COOH\rightleftharpoons H^++CH_3COO^-
\]

Other examples include:
- Carbonic acid.
- Hydrofluoric acid.
- Phosphoric acid.

### 3.3 Strong and Weak Bases

Strong bases dissociate extensively in water.

Examples:
- NaOH.
- KOH.
- Ba(OH)₂.

Weak bases react partially with water.

Example:

\[
NH_3+H_2O\rightleftharpoons NH_4^++OH^-
\]

Strength and concentration are different concepts. A dilute strong acid can have a lower concentration than a concentrated weak acid.

## 4. Acid Ionisation Constant

For a weak acid:

\[
HA\rightleftharpoons H^++A^-
\]

The acid ionisation constant is:

\[
\boxed{K_a=\frac{[H^+][A^-]}{[HA]}}
\]

A larger \(K_a\) generally indicates a stronger acid.

The negative logarithmic form is:

\[
\boxed{pK_a=-\log K_a}
\]

A smaller \(pK_a\) indicates a stronger acid.

## 5. Base Ionisation Constant

For a weak base:

\[
B+H_2O\rightleftharpoons BH^++OH^-
\]

The base ionisation constant is:

\[
\boxed{K_b=\frac{[BH^+][OH^-]}{[B]}}
\]

A larger \(K_b\) generally indicates a stronger base.

\[
\boxed{pK_b=-\log K_b}
\]

A smaller \(pK_b\) indicates a stronger base.

## 6. Ionic Product of Water

Water undergoes slight self-ionisation:

\[
2H_2O\rightleftharpoons H_3O^++OH^-
\]

The ionic product of water is:

\[
\boxed{K_w=[H^+][OH^-]}
\]

At 25°C:

\[
\boxed{K_w=1.0\times10^{-14}}
\]

For pure water at 25°C:

\[
[H^+]=[OH^-]=10^{-7}\text{ M}
\]

The value of \(K_w\) changes with temperature.

## 7. The pH Scale

pH is a logarithmic measure of hydrogen-ion activity. For dilute solutions, concentration is commonly used as an approximation.

\[
\boxed{pH=-\log[H^+]}
\]

Similarly:

\[
\boxed{pOH=-\log[OH^-]}
\]

At 25°C:

\[
\boxed{pH+pOH=14}
\]

### 7.1 Classification at 25°C

| pH | Nature |
|---|---|
| Below 7 | Acidic |
| Equal to 7 | Neutral |
| Above 7 | Basic |

Neutrality means equal hydrogen-ion and hydroxide-ion activities. The neutral pH is 7 at 25°C but changes with temperature.

### 7.2 Worked Example

Calculate the pH of a solution with hydrogen-ion concentration:

\[
[H^+]=10^{-3}\text{ M}
\]

Using:

\[
pH=-\log[H^+]
\]

\[
pH=-\log(10^{-3})
\]

\[
\boxed{pH=3}
\]

The solution is acidic.

## 8. pH of Strong Acids and Bases

For a strong monoprotic acid that dissociates completely:

\[
[H^+]\approx C
\]

Therefore:

\[
pH=-\log C
\]

For a strong monobasic base:

\[
[OH^-]\approx C
\]

Therefore:

\[
pOH=-\log C
\]

At 25°C:

\[
pH=14-pOH
\]

For acids and bases that release multiple ions per formula unit, the stoichiometry must be considered, subject to the extent of dissociation and solution conditions.

## 9. pH of Weak Acids and Bases

For a weak acid HA with initial concentration C:

\[
HA\rightleftharpoons H^++A^-
\]

If its degree of ionisation is small:

\[
[H^+]\approx\sqrt{K_aC}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{pH\approx-\log\sqrt{K_aC}}
\]

For a weak base:

\[
[OH^-]\approx\sqrt{K_bC}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{pOH\approx-\log\sqrt{K_bC}}
\]

These approximations are valid only when ionisation is sufficiently small. Otherwise, the full equilibrium expression should be solved.

## 10. Relationship Between \(K_a\), \(K_b\), and \(K_w\)

For a conjugate acid-base pair:

\[
\boxed{K_aK_b=K_w}
\]

Taking negative logarithms:

\[
\boxed{pK_a+pK_b=pK_w}
\]

At 25°C:

\[
pK_a+pK_b=14
\]

A strong acid has a weak conjugate base, and a strong base has a weak conjugate acid.

## 11. Common Exam Traps

- Acid strength is not the same as acid concentration.
- Strong acids ionise extensively; weak acids establish partial ionisation equilibrium.
- pH is logarithmic, not linear.
- A change of one pH unit represents a tenfold change in hydrogen-ion activity.
- The relation \(pH+pOH=14\) applies at 25°C.
- Lewis acids accept electron pairs, whereas Lewis bases donate electron pairs.
- Water acts as both an acid and a base.

## 12. Applications of Acids and Bases

- Maintaining blood pH.
- Chemical manufacturing.
- Food preservation and processing.
- Pharmaceutical formulation.
- Water treatment.
- Soil acidity management.
- Analytical chemistry.

   - [ ] 2
   - [ ] 3
   - [x] 4
