# Lesson 39: Aliphatic Hydrocarbons and Their Reactions

Hydrocarbons are organic compounds containing only carbon and hydrogen.

Aliphatic hydrocarbons include open-chain alkanes, alkenes and alkynes. They differ in the types of carbon-carbon bonds present and therefore show different chemical properties.

This lesson covers their structures, nomenclature, preparation, physical properties and characteristic reactions.

## 1. Classification of Hydrocarbons

Hydrocarbons are broadly divided into saturated and unsaturated compounds.

### 1.1 Saturated Hydrocarbons

Alkanes contain only carbon-carbon single bonds.

General formula for open-chain alkanes:

\[
C_nH_{2n+2}
\]

Examples:

\[
CH_4,\quad C_2H_6,\quad C_3H_8
\]

### 1.2 Unsaturated Hydrocarbons

Alkenes contain at least one carbon-carbon double bond.

For open-chain compounds containing one double bond:

\[
C_nH_{2n}
\]

Alkynes contain at least one carbon-carbon triple bond.

For open-chain compounds containing one triple bond:

\[
C_nH_{2n-2}
\]

These general formulas apply to acyclic compounds with one multiple bond and no additional rings or multiple bonds.

## 2. Alkanes

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons containing only single covalent bonds.

The carbon atoms are approximately tetrahedral.

The carbon-carbon single bond is a sigma bond formed through head-on orbital overlap.

### 2.1 Nomenclature

The suffix for alkanes is -ane.

Examples:

| Formula | Name |
|---|---|
| \(CH_4\) | Methane |
| \(C_2H_6\) | Ethane |
| \(C_3H_8\) | Propane |
| \(C_4H_{10}\) | Butane |
| \(C_5H_{12}\) | Pentane |

Branched alkanes are named by selecting the longest suitable carbon chain and identifying substituents.

Example:

\[
CH_3CH(CH_3)CH_2CH_3
\]

Name: 2-methylbutane.

## 3. Preparation of Alkanes

### 3.1 Hydrogenation of Alkenes

Alkenes react with hydrogen in the presence of catalysts such as nickel, platinum or palladium.

\[
CH_2=CH_2+H_2\xrightarrow{Ni}CH_3CH_3
\]

### 3.2 Reduction of Haloalkanes

Haloalkanes can be reduced to alkanes using suitable reducing conditions.

\[
R-X+2[H]\rightarrow R-H+HX
\]

### 3.3 Wurtz Reaction

Alkyl halides react with sodium in dry ether to form a higher alkane.

\[
2R-X+2Na\rightarrow R-R+2NaX
\]

This method is most useful for preparing symmetrical alkanes.

### 3.4 Decarboxylation

Sodium salts of carboxylic acids can produce alkanes with one fewer carbon atom through heating with soda lime.

\[
RCOONa+NaOH\xrightarrow{\Delta}RH+Na_2CO_3
\]

## 4. Chemical Reactions of Alkanes

Alkanes are comparatively unreactive because their C-C and C-H sigma bonds are relatively strong and non-polar.

### 4.1 Combustion

Complete combustion produces carbon dioxide and water.

\[
CH_4+2O_2\rightarrow CO_2+2H_2O
\]

Incomplete combustion can produce carbon monoxide or carbon.

### 4.2 Halogenation

Alkanes react with halogens under suitable light or heat conditions through free-radical substitution.

\[
CH_4+Cl_2\xrightarrow{h\nu}CH_3Cl+HCl
\]

Further substitution can produce compounds such as:

\[
CH_2Cl_2,\quad CHCl_3,\quad CCl_4
\]

### 4.3 Cracking

Cracking breaks larger hydrocarbons into smaller molecules, often producing alkanes and alkenes.

It is important in petroleum refining.

## 5. Conformations of Alkanes

Rotation around a carbon-carbon single bond produces different spatial arrangements called conformations.

Ethane is a common example.

### 5.1 Staggered Conformation

The hydrogen atoms on one carbon are positioned between those on the adjacent carbon.

This arrangement has lower torsional strain and is more stable.

### 5.2 Eclipsed Conformation

The hydrogen atoms on adjacent carbon atoms align with one another.

This arrangement has greater torsional strain and is less stable.

The energy difference arises mainly from torsional effects and electron interactions.

## 6. Alkenes

Alkenes contain at least one carbon-carbon double bond.

A carbon-carbon double bond consists of:

- One sigma bond.
- One pi bond.

The carbon atoms involved in a typical alkene double bond are approximately trigonal planar.

Their bond angles are close to \(120^\circ\).

### 6.1 Nomenclature

The suffix for alkenes is -ene.

Examples:

\[
CH_2=CH_2:\text{Ethene}
\]

\[
CH_3CH=CH_2:\text{Prop-1-ene}
\]

\[
CH_3CH=CHCH_3:\text{But-2-ene}
\]

### 6.2 Preparation by Dehydration of Alcohols

Alcohols can lose water under acidic and heated conditions to produce alkenes.

\[
CH_3CH_2OH\xrightarrow[\Delta]{conc.\ H_2SO_4}CH_2=CH_2+H_2O
\]

### 6.3 Preparation by Dehydrohalogenation

Haloalkanes can eliminate hydrogen halide in the presence of a strong base.

\[
CH_3CH_2Br+KOH_{(alc)}
\rightarrow CH_2=CH_2+KBr+H_2O
\]

## 7. Chemical Reactions of Alkenes

The pi bond is relatively electron-rich and makes alkenes susceptible to addition reactions.

### 7.1 Hydrogenation

\[
CH_2=CH_2+H_2\xrightarrow{Ni}CH_3CH_3
\]

### 7.2 Halogen Addition

\[
CH_2=CH_2+Br_2\rightarrow BrCH_2CH_2Br
\]

Bromine solution is decolourised by many alkenes.

### 7.3 Addition of Hydrogen Halides

\[
CH_2=CH_2+HBr\rightarrow CH_3CH_2Br
\]

For unsymmetrical alkenes, the orientation can follow Markovnikov's rule.

### 7.4 Hydration

Alkenes react with water in the presence of suitable acid catalysts to produce alcohols.

\[
CH_2=CH_2+H_2O\rightarrow CH_3CH_2OH
\]

### 7.5 Oxidation

Alkenes react with oxidising agents.

Cold dilute alkaline potassium permanganate can convert many alkenes into vicinal diols.

Strong oxidative cleavage can break the carbon-carbon double bond.

### 7.6 Polymerisation

Many alkenes undergo addition polymerisation.

\[
nCH_2=CH_2\rightarrow (-CH_2-CH_2-)_n
\]

Ethene produces polyethene.

## 8. Markovnikov's Rule

When an unsymmetrical alkene reacts with a hydrogen halide under ordinary ionic conditions, the hydrogen generally adds to the carbon already carrying more hydrogen atoms.

The halide attaches to the other carbon.

Example:

\[
CH_3CH=CH_2+HBr\rightarrow CH_3CHBrCH_3
\]

The major product is 2-bromopropane.

This orientation is commonly explained by formation of the more stable carbocation intermediate.

## 9. Peroxide Effect

In the presence of peroxides, hydrogen bromide can add to certain unsymmetrical alkenes through a free-radical mechanism.

The orientation is anti-Markovnikov.

For propene:

\[
CH_3CH=CH_2+HBr
\xrightarrow{peroxide}
CH_3CH_2CH_2Br
\]

This peroxide effect is characteristic of HBr and is not generally observed with HCl or HI under the same conditions.

## 10. Alkynes

Alkynes contain at least one carbon-carbon triple bond.

A triple bond consists of:

- One sigma bond.
- Two pi bonds.

The carbon atoms involved in a typical triple bond are linear.

The bond angle is approximately:

\[
180^\circ
\]

### 10.1 Nomenclature

The suffix for alkynes is -yne.

Examples:

\[
HC\equiv CH:\text{Ethyne}
\]

\[
CH_3C\equiv CH:\text{Prop-1-yne}
\]

\[
CH_3C\equiv CCH_3:\text{But-2-yne}
\]

### 10.2 Preparation

Ethyne can be prepared by reacting calcium carbide with water.

\[
CaC_2+2H_2O\rightarrow C_2H_2+Ca(OH)_2
\]

Alkynes can also be prepared by double dehydrohalogenation of suitable dihaloalkanes.

## 11. Chemical Reactions of Alkynes

### 11.1 Hydrogenation

Complete hydrogenation produces alkanes.

\[
HC\equiv CH+2H_2\rightarrow CH_3CH_3
\]

Selective catalysts can stop hydrogenation at the alkene stage.

### 11.2 Halogen Addition

Halogens add across the triple bond.

Further addition can occur because two pi bonds are present.

### 11.3 Hydrogen Halide Addition

Hydrogen halides add to alkynes, and the final products depend on reaction conditions and the amount of reagent.

### 11.4 Hydration

Hydration of ethyne in the presence of suitable catalysts produces an enol that rearranges to ethanal.

\[
HC\equiv CH+H_2O\rightarrow CH_2=CHOH
\]

\[
CH_2=CHOH\rightleftharpoons CH_3CHO
\]

### 11.5 Acidity of Terminal Alkynes

Terminal alkynes contain a hydrogen attached directly to an sp-hybridised carbon.

This hydrogen is relatively acidic compared with hydrogen atoms attached to sp² or sp³ carbon.

The conjugate base is stabilised by the high s-character of the sp orbital.

## 12. Comparison of Alkanes, Alkenes and Alkynes

| Property | Alkanes | Alkenes | Alkynes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main bond | Single | Double | Triple |
| General formula | \(C_nH_{2n+2}\) | \(C_nH_{2n}\) | \(C_nH_{2n-2}\) |
| Carbon geometry at bond | Tetrahedral | Trigonal planar | Linear |
| Main reaction type | Substitution | Addition | Addition |
| Typical suffix | -ane | -ene | -yne |

## 13. Common Mistakes and Exam Points

- Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons.
- Alkenes contain at least one double bond.
- Alkynes contain at least one triple bond.
- A double bond consists of one sigma and one pi bond.
- A triple bond consists of one sigma and two pi bonds.
- The peroxide effect is characteristic of HBr.
- Markovnikov's rule predicts orientation in ordinary ionic addition.
- Terminal alkynes are more acidic than ordinary alkenes and alkanes.
- Staggered ethane is more stable than eclipsed ethane.

## 14. Lesson Recap

- Aliphatic hydrocarbons include alkanes, alkenes and alkynes.
- Alkanes mainly undergo substitution and combustion.
- Alkenes and alkynes commonly undergo addition reactions.
- Conformational analysis explains the energy differences between spatial arrangements.
- Markovnikov's rule and the peroxide effect explain different orientations of hydrogen bromide addition.
- Hydrocarbons are important fuels and raw materials for industrial chemical production.
