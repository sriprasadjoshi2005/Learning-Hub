# Lesson 50: Spectroscopy, Integrated Problems and Comprehensive Exam Practice

Spectroscopy uses the interaction of electromagnetic radiation with matter to obtain information about chemical structure and composition. This final lesson connects spectroscopy with analytical chemistry, reaction mechanisms, equilibrium, thermodynamics, electrochemistry, and quantitative problem-solving.

## 1. Electromagnetic Radiation

Electromagnetic radiation travels as waves and can also be described in terms of photons.

The relationship between wavelength, frequency, and speed is:

\[
c=\lambda\nu
\]

where:
- \(c\) is the speed of light.
- \(\lambda\) is wavelength.
- \(\nu\) is frequency.

The energy of a photon is:

\[
E=h\nu
\]

Combining the equations:

\[
E=\frac{hc}{\lambda}
\]

Therefore, shorter wavelengths correspond to higher photon energies.

## 2. Types of Spectroscopy

Different regions of the electromagnetic spectrum interact with different molecular or atomic processes.

| Technique | Main information obtained |
|---|---|
| UV-Visible spectroscopy | Electronic transitions |
| Infrared spectroscopy | Molecular vibrations |
| Nuclear magnetic resonance | Chemical environments of nuclei |
| Atomic absorption spectroscopy | Elemental concentration |
| Mass spectrometry | Mass-to-charge ratios and structural information |
| Raman spectroscopy | Molecular vibrational information |

## 3. UV-Visible Spectroscopy

UV-Visible spectroscopy measures absorption of ultraviolet or visible radiation by a sample.

Absorption can promote electrons from lower-energy to higher-energy electronic states.

### 3.1 Chromophores

A chromophore is the part of a molecule responsible for characteristic light absorption.

Examples include:
- C=C
- C=O
- Aromatic conjugated systems
- Azo groups

Greater conjugation often reduces the energy difference between electronic states and shifts absorption towards longer wavelengths.

### 3.2 Beer–Lambert Law

For a suitable absorbing solution:

\[
A=\varepsilon lc
\]

where:
- \(A\) is absorbance.
- \(\varepsilon\) is molar absorption coefficient.
- \(l\) is path length.
- \(c\) is concentration.

Absorbance is related to transmittance:

\[
A=-\log_{10}T
\]

where \(T\) is the fraction of incident light transmitted.

If transmittance is expressed as a percentage:

\[
A=-\log_{10}\left(\frac{\%T}{100}\right)
\]

### 3.3 Applications

- Measuring solution concentration.
- Studying reaction progress.
- Analysing coloured compounds.
- Investigating conjugation.
- Determining metal-ion concentrations through suitable complexes.

## 4. Infrared Spectroscopy

Infrared spectroscopy measures absorption associated with molecular vibrations.

A molecule absorbs IR radiation when the radiation frequency corresponds to an allowed vibrational transition that involves a change in dipole moment.

### 4.1 Important Absorption Regions

Approximate characteristic regions include:

| Functional group | Approximate absorption region (cm⁻¹) |
|---|---:|
| O–H in alcohols | 3200–3600 |
| O–H in carboxylic acids | 2500–3300, broad |
| N–H | 3300–3500 |
| C–H | 2850–3100 |
| C≡N | 2210–2260 |
| C≡C | 2100–2260 |
| C=O | 1650–1800 |
| C=C | 1600–1680 |

Exact positions vary with molecular structure, conjugation, hydrogen bonding, and measurement conditions.

### 4.2 Functional-Group Identification

Examples:
- A strong absorption near 1700 cm⁻¹ often indicates a carbonyl group.
- A broad absorption in the 3200–3600 cm⁻¹ region may indicate an alcohol O–H group.
- A very broad absorption around 2500–3300 cm⁻¹ can indicate a carboxylic acid O–H group.
- A nitrile often gives a sharp absorption near 2250 cm⁻¹.

### 4.3 Fingerprint Region

The region below approximately 1500 cm⁻¹ contains many complex absorptions.

This fingerprint region is particularly useful for comparing a sample spectrum with a known reference spectrum.

## 5. Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy

NMR spectroscopy studies the behaviour of certain atomic nuclei in a magnetic field.

Commonly used nuclei include:
- Hydrogen-1
- Carbon-13

### 5.1 Chemical Shift

Chemical shift describes the position of an NMR signal relative to a reference compound.

It is expressed in parts per million:

\[
\delta=
\frac{\text{frequency difference}}
{\text{spectrometer frequency}}\times10^6
\]

The common reference compound for proton and carbon NMR is tetramethylsilane (TMS).

### 5.2 Shielding and Deshielding

Electrons around a nucleus partially shield it from the applied magnetic field.

- Greater electron shielding generally produces a lower chemical shift.
- Electron-withdrawing groups generally deshield nearby nuclei and produce higher chemical shifts.

### 5.3 Proton NMR: Number of Signals

The number of distinct proton environments helps determine molecular structure.

Chemically equivalent protons generally produce the same signal.

For example, ethane has one proton environment because the hydrogens are equivalent in its usual rapidly rotating structure.

### 5.4 Integration

The area under an NMR signal is proportional to the number of contributing nuclei.

Integration provides relative numbers of protons in different environments.

### 5.5 Spin–Spin Splitting

In simple first-order spectra, neighbouring nonequivalent protons can split signals according to the \(n+1\) rule.

Here, \(n\) is the number of equivalent neighbouring protons.

Examples:
- No equivalent neighbouring protons: singlet.
- One equivalent neighbouring proton: doublet.
- Two equivalent neighbouring protons: triplet.
- Three equivalent neighbouring protons: quartet.

The rule has limitations in more complex spectra.

## 6. Mass Spectrometry

Mass spectrometry measures ions according to their mass-to-charge ratio.

\[
\frac{m}{z}
\]

The process commonly involves:
1. Sample introduction.
2. Ionisation.
3. Ion acceleration or separation.
4. Detection.
5. Generation of a mass spectrum.

### 6.1 Molecular Ion

The molecular ion may provide information about the molecular mass of the compound.

Its presence and intensity depend on the ionisation method and molecular stability.

### 6.2 Fragmentation

Molecular ions can break into smaller fragments.

The fragmentation pattern provides structural information.

### 6.3 Isotope Patterns

Elements with characteristic isotopes produce recognisable mass-spectral patterns.

For example:
- Chlorine commonly gives molecular-ion peaks in an approximately 3:1 ratio for M and M+2.
- Bromine commonly gives approximately equal M and M+2 peaks.

These patterns are useful for identifying halogen-containing compounds.

## 7. Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy

Atomic absorption spectroscopy measures the absorption of characteristic radiation by free atoms.

It is used to determine the concentration of particular elements, especially metals.

Applications include:
- Analysis of drinking water.
- Measurement of trace metals in soil.
- Food composition analysis.
- Industrial quality control.

A calibration curve made using standards is commonly used to determine the concentration of an unknown sample.

## 8. Chromatography

Chromatography separates mixture components based on their different interactions with a stationary phase and a mobile phase.

### 8.1 Paper Chromatography

Components travel at different rates through paper as solvent moves along it.

The retention factor is:

\[
R_f=
\frac{\text{Distance travelled by solute}}
{\text{Distance travelled by solvent front}}
\]

For a given experimental setup, \(R_f\) values can help compare substances.

### 8.2 Thin-Layer Chromatography

Thin-layer chromatography uses a thin adsorbent layer, commonly silica gel or alumina.

It is useful for:
- Monitoring reaction progress.
- Checking sample purity.
- Comparing compounds.
- Selecting suitable solvents for column chromatography.

### 8.3 Column Chromatography

A mixture passes through a column containing a stationary phase.

Different components travel at different rates and can be collected separately.

### 8.4 High-Performance Liquid Chromatography

HPLC uses a liquid mobile phase pumped through a column under high pressure.

It is widely used in pharmaceutical, food, environmental, and biochemical analysis.

## 9. Combining Spectroscopic Evidence

No single spectral feature should normally be used in isolation to identify an unknown compound.

A reliable structure determination combines:
- Molecular mass.
- Molecular formula.
- Degree of unsaturation.
- IR functional-group evidence.
- NMR chemical shifts and integration.
- Fragmentation patterns.
- Chemical tests and reaction behaviour.

### 9.1 Degree of Unsaturation

For a molecular formula containing carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, and halogens:

\[
DBE=\frac{2C+2+N-H-X}{2}
\]

where:
- \(C\) is the number of carbon atoms.
- \(H\) is the number of hydrogen atoms.
- \(N\) is the number of nitrogen atoms.
- \(X\) is the total number of halogen atoms.

Oxygen and sulfur are not included in this basic expression.

The degree of unsaturation represents the total number of rings and pi-bond equivalents.

Examples:
- One ring contributes 1.
- One double bond contributes 1.
- One triple bond contributes 2.

## 10. Integrated Chemical Problem-Solving

Chemistry examination questions often combine multiple topics.

A reliable method is:

1. Identify the information given.
2. Identify the unknown quantity or structure.
3. Write the relevant equation or principle.
4. Balance chemical equations before using mole ratios.
5. Convert quantities into consistent units.
6. Substitute values carefully.
7. Check units and significant figures.
8. Interpret whether the result is chemically reasonable.

## 11. Integrated Numerical Examples

### 11.1 Stoichiometry and Limiting Reagents

Consider:

\[
2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O
\]

Given 6 mol H₂ and 2 mol O₂:

For hydrogen:

\[
\frac{6}{2}=3
\]

For oxygen:

\[
\frac{2}{1}=2
\]

Oxygen has the smaller ratio and is the limiting reactant.

Water produced:

\[
n(H_2O)=2\times2=4\ mol
\]

Hydrogen consumed:

\[
n(H_2)=2\times2=4\ mol
\]

Hydrogen remaining:

\[
6-4=2\ mol
\]

### 11.2 Equilibrium and Equilibrium Constant

For:

\[
N_2+3H_2\rightleftharpoons2NH_3
\]

The equilibrium constant in terms of concentration is:

\[
K_c=\frac{[NH_3]^2}{[N_2][H_2]^3}
\]

The powers in the expression come from the balanced equation's coefficients.

### 11.3 Electrochemistry

For a galvanic cell:

\[
E^\circ_{\text{cell}}=
E^\circ_{\text{cathode}}-
E^\circ_{\text{anode}}
\]

A positive standard cell potential indicates a spontaneous cell reaction under standard conditions.

The Gibbs energy relation is:

\[
\Delta G^\circ=-nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}}
\]

### 11.4 Thermochemistry

At constant pressure, the heat transferred is related to enthalpy change.

For a reaction:

\[
\Delta H=
\sum \Delta H_f^\circ(\text{products})
-
\sum \Delta H_f^\circ(\text{reactants})
\]

Each term must be multiplied by its stoichiometric coefficient.

## 12. Common Exam Traps

- Shorter wavelength means higher photon energy.
- IR spectroscopy identifies vibrational transitions, not directly molecular mass.
- A broad O–H absorption is different from a sharp nitrile absorption.
- NMR integration gives relative proton numbers, not automatically absolute numbers.
- The \(n+1\) rule applies to simple first-order splitting patterns.
- Chlorine and bromine have characteristic isotope patterns.
- The \(R_f\) value is not an absolute universal constant; it depends on experimental conditions.
- The degree of unsaturation counts rings and pi-bond equivalents.
- Always balance a reaction before applying stoichiometric ratios.
- A positive standard cell potential corresponds to negative standard Gibbs energy for the cell reaction.

## 14. Final Examination Strategy

### 14.1 Numerical Questions

- Write the governing equation first.
- Convert all volumes into litres when using molarity in mol L⁻¹.
- Use kelvin for gas-law temperature calculations.
- Check stoichiometric coefficients.
- Retain unrounded values until the final step.

### 14.2 Organic Chemistry Questions

- Identify the functional group.
- Determine whether the reaction involves substitution, addition, elimination, oxidation, or reduction.
- Check the reaction conditions.
- Consider steric and electronic effects.
- Verify the final product's structure and nomenclature.

### 14.3 Conceptual Questions

- Define the principle precisely.
- Explain the molecular reason behind the observation.
- Include a relevant equation when appropriate.
- Distinguish similar concepts rather than memorising isolated statements.

## 15. Summary

- Spectroscopy provides information about molecular structure and composition.
- UV-Visible spectroscopy studies electronic transitions.
- IR spectroscopy studies molecular vibrations.
- NMR studies nuclear environments in a magnetic field.
- Mass spectrometry provides mass-to-charge and fragmentation information.
- Chromatography separates mixture components.
- Integrated problem-solving combines equations, reaction mechanisms, stoichiometry, and chemical reasoning.
