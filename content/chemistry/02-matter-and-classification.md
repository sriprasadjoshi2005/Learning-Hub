# Lesson 2: Matter and Its Classification

Everything around us, including air, water, rocks, metals, plants, and living organisms, consists of matter. Chemistry studies matter by examining its composition, properties, structure, and transformations. Understanding how matter is classified is essential because different substances behave differently depending on their composition and structure.

This lesson develops the concept of matter, its particulate nature, the classification of substances into elements, compounds, and mixtures, and the differences between homogeneous and heterogeneous systems. It also introduces physical and chemical methods used to separate the components of mixtures.

## 1. Nature of Matter

### 1.1 Definition of Matter

Matter is anything that possesses mass and occupies space.

Examples:
- Air inside a balloon.
- Water in a glass.
- Iron in a metal rod.
- Wood in furniture.
- Soil and rocks.
- Living organisms.

Light, heat, sound, and electromagnetic radiation are forms of energy and are not classified as ordinary matter.

### 1.2 Characteristics of Matter

Matter has several fundamental characteristics:

1. Matter has mass.
2. Matter occupies space.
3. Matter is composed of tiny particles.
4. Particles of matter are in continuous motion.
5. Particles of matter attract one another.
6. There are spaces between particles of matter.

### 1.3 Particulate Nature of Matter

Matter is not continuous at the microscopic level. It consists of extremely small particles such as atoms, molecules, and ions.

For example, a drop of water contains an enormous number of water molecules.

The particulate nature of matter explains:
- Diffusion of gases.
- Dissolution of substances.
- Expansion during heating.
- Compression of gases.
- Chemical reactions.

### 1.4 Interparticle Spaces

Particles of matter are separated by spaces.

The size of these spaces depends on the physical state:

- Solids generally have very small interparticle spaces.
- Liquids have larger interparticle spaces than solids.
- Gases have very large interparticle spaces.

This difference helps explain why gases are much more compressible than solids and liquids.

## 2. Classification of Matter

Matter can be classified according to its physical state and chemical composition.

### 2.1 Classification Based on Composition

Based on composition, matter is divided into two main categories:

1. Pure substances.
2. Mixtures.

### 2.2 Pure Substances

A pure substance consists of only one chemical substance and has a definite composition and characteristic properties.

Pure substances are classified into elements and compounds.

**Elements**

An element is a pure substance consisting of only one type of atom.

Examples:
- Hydrogen (H).
- Oxygen (O).
- Carbon (C).
- Iron (Fe).
- Copper (Cu).
- Gold (Au).

Elements cannot be decomposed into simpler substances by ordinary chemical methods.

**Compounds**

A compound is a pure substance formed when two or more different elements combine chemically in a fixed proportion by mass.

Examples:
- Water (H₂O).
- Carbon dioxide (CO₂).
- Sodium chloride (NaCl).
- Ammonia (NH₃).

Compounds have properties that are generally different from those of their constituent elements.

For example:
- Hydrogen is a combustible gas.
- Oxygen supports combustion.
- Water, formed from hydrogen and oxygen, does not behave like either element.

### 2.3 Mixtures

A mixture is formed when two or more substances combine physically in any proportion without necessarily undergoing a chemical reaction.

Examples:
- Air.
- Salt water.
- Soil.
- Brass.
- Sand and water.

Characteristics of mixtures:

1. Their composition can vary.
2. Their components generally retain their chemical identities.
3. Their components can often be separated by physical methods.
4. They do not have a fixed chemical formula.
5. Their properties depend on the proportions of their components.

### 2.4 Homogeneous Mixtures

A homogeneous mixture has a uniform composition and properties throughout its bulk.

Examples:
- Salt dissolved in water.
- Sugar solution.
- Air.
- Brass.

A homogeneous mixture generally appears as a single phase.

### 2.5 Heterogeneous Mixtures

A heterogeneous mixture has a non-uniform composition, and its components may exist in different phases.

Examples:
- Oil and water.
- Sand and water.
- Soil.
- Granite.

Different portions of a heterogeneous mixture may have different compositions and properties.

## 3. Elements and Their Classification

### 3.1 Metals

Metals are generally characterized by metallic lustre, electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity, and the ability to be shaped.

Common properties:
- Usually good conductors of heat and electricity.
- Generally malleable.
- Generally ductile.
- Often possess metallic lustre.
- Usually form positive ions.

Examples:
- Iron.
- Copper.
- Aluminium.
- Sodium.
- Gold.

### 3.2 Non-Metals

Non-metals generally lack the characteristic physical properties of metals.

Common properties:
- Usually poor conductors of heat and electricity.
- Solid non-metals are often brittle.
- May exist as solids, liquids, or gases.
- Often form covalent compounds.
- May gain electrons during ionic compound formation.

Examples:
- Oxygen.
- Nitrogen.
- Sulfur.
- Carbon.
- Chlorine.

### 3.3 Metalloids

Metalloids possess properties intermediate between metals and non-metals.

Examples:
- Boron.
- Silicon.
- Germanium.
- Arsenic.
- Antimony.

Silicon and germanium are especially important because of their semiconductor properties.

## 4. Compounds and Their Classification

### 4.1 Ionic Compounds

Ionic compounds generally consist of positively and negatively charged ions held together by electrostatic attraction.

Examples:
- Sodium chloride (NaCl).
- Magnesium oxide (MgO).
- Calcium chloride (CaCl₂).

### 4.2 Covalent Compounds

Covalent compounds contain atoms connected through shared electron pairs.

Examples:
- Water (H₂O).
- Methane (CH₄).
- Carbon dioxide (CO₂).
- Ammonia (NH₃).

### 4.3 Molecular and Network Covalent Substances

Molecular covalent substances consist of discrete molecules.

Examples:
- Water.
- Carbon dioxide.
- Methane.

Network covalent substances contain extended structures of covalently bonded atoms.

Examples:
- Diamond.
- Silicon dioxide.
- Silicon carbide.

## 5. Classification of Mixtures

### 5.1 Solutions

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances.

It consists of:

- **Solvent:** The component that dissolves another substance and generally determines the physical state of the solution.
- **Solute:** The substance dissolved in the solvent.

Example:

In salt water:
- Salt is the solute.
- Water is the solvent.

### 5.2 Suspensions

A suspension is a heterogeneous mixture containing relatively large particles dispersed in another substance.

Characteristics:
- Particles may be visible.
- Particles can settle on standing.
- They can generally be separated by filtration.
- They scatter light.

Example: Muddy water.

### 5.3 Colloids

A colloid is a mixture in which particles of one substance are dispersed throughout another substance.

Characteristics:
- Particles are larger than those in ordinary solutions but generally smaller than suspension particles.
- They do not settle easily under ordinary conditions.
- They scatter light through the Tyndall effect.

Examples:
- Milk.
- Fog.
- Smoke.
- Gelatin.
- Mayonnaise.

### 5.4 Solutions, Colloids, and Suspensions: Comparison

| Property | Solution | Colloid | Suspension |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Homogeneous at molecular level | Heterogeneous at microscopic level | Heterogeneous |
| Particle size | Generally below 1 nm | Approximately 1–1000 nm | Generally above 1000 nm |
| Settling | Does not settle | Generally does not settle | Often settles |
| Ordinary filtration | Not separated | Not separated by ordinary filtration | Generally separated |
| Light scattering | Usually no visible Tyndall effect | Shows Tyndall effect | Scatters light |
| Example | Salt water | Milk | Muddy water |

## 6. Physical and Chemical Classification

### 6.1 Physical Properties

Physical properties can be observed or measured without changing the chemical identity of a substance.

Examples:
- Density.
- Melting point.
- Boiling point.
- Colour.
- Hardness.
- Electrical conductivity.

### 6.2 Chemical Properties

Chemical properties describe how a substance behaves during chemical reactions.

Examples:
- Flammability.
- Oxidation.
- Acidity.
- Reactivity with water.
- Corrosion behaviour.

### 6.3 Physical and Chemical Changes

A physical change does not alter the chemical composition of a substance.

Example:

\[
H_2O(s)\rightarrow H_2O(l)
\]

A chemical change produces one or more new substances.

Example:

\[
2Mg+O_2\rightarrow 2MgO
\]

## 7. Important Comparisons and Observations

### 7.1 Pure Substances and Mixtures

| Pure substances | Mixtures |
|---|---|
| Have definite composition. | Have variable composition. |
| Have characteristic properties. | Properties depend on component proportions. |
| Cannot be separated into components by ordinary physical methods. | Components can often be separated physically. |
| May be elements or compounds. | May be homogeneous or heterogeneous. |
| Examples: oxygen, water, sodium chloride. | Examples: air, seawater, soil. |

### 7.2 Important Observations

- Air is a homogeneous mixture of gases under ordinary conditions.
- Distilled water is a pure compound.
- Brass is a homogeneous mixture of metals.
- Milk is a colloidal system.
- Soil is generally heterogeneous.
- A compound has a fixed composition, whereas a mixture does not.
- Elements cannot be decomposed into simpler substances through ordinary chemical reactions.

## 8. Common Exam Traps

1. A pure substance does not necessarily mean an element. Compounds are also pure substances.
2. Air is a mixture, not a compound.
3. Brass is an alloy and is generally classified as a homogeneous mixture.
4. Milk is a colloid, not a true solution.
5. A compound has a fixed composition, but a mixture can have variable composition.
6. Homogeneous mixtures may contain more than one substance.
7. A chemical compound has properties different from its constituent elements.
8. Colloids scatter light due to the Tyndall effect.
9. Mixture components generally retain their chemical identities.
10. An element contains only one type of atom, even when it exists as a molecule such as O₂.

   - [ ] Air.
   - [ ] Brass.
   - [x] Sodium chloride.
   - [ ] Soil.

   > Sodium chloride has a definite chemical composition and consists of sodium and chlorine chemically combined.

   - [ ] Pure distilled water.
   - [ ] Salt solution.
   - [x] Milk.
   - [ ] Pure ethanol.

   > Milk is a colloid, and colloidal particles scatter light, producing the Tyndall effect.

3. Which statement about mixtures is correct?

   - [ ] They always have fixed chemical formulae.
   - [x] Their components can generally be separated by physical methods.
   - [ ] Their components must be chemically bonded.
   - [ ] They always have uniform composition.

   > Mixtures are physical combinations of substances, and their components can often be separated using physical techniques.
