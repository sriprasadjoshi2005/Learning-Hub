# Lesson 8: Quantum Theory and Wave-Particle Duality

Classical physics successfully explains many large-scale physical phenomena, but it cannot adequately describe the behaviour of matter and radiation at atomic and subatomic scales. Experiments involving blackbody radiation, the photoelectric effect, atomic spectra, and electron diffraction revealed that energy and matter exhibit behaviour that requires a new theoretical framework.

Quantum theory explains the quantized nature of energy and the microscopic behaviour of particles. Wave-particle duality establishes that both radiation and matter can display wave-like and particle-like properties.

This lesson introduces the development of quantum theory, blackbody radiation, Planck's hypothesis, the photoelectric effect, de Broglie's matter waves, the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, wavefunctions, and the Schrödinger equation.

## 1. Introduction to Quantum Theory

### 1.1 Classical Physics

Classical physics includes theories such as:
- Newtonian mechanics.
- Classical electromagnetism.
- Classical thermodynamics.

These theories describe many everyday phenomena accurately.

However, classical physics has limitations when applied to:
- Atomic structure.
- Electron behaviour.
- Atomic spectra.
- Blackbody radiation.
- Photoelectric emission.
- Microscopic energy transitions.

### 1.2 Meaning of Quantum Theory

Quantum theory describes the behaviour of matter and energy at microscopic scales.

Its central ideas include:

1. Certain physical quantities can have discrete allowed values.
2. Matter and radiation can exhibit wave-particle duality.
3. Physical predictions are often probabilistic.
4. Measurement outcomes are described using quantum states.
5. The behaviour of microscopic systems is governed by quantum principles.

## 2. Blackbody Radiation

### 2.1 Definition

A blackbody is an ideal object that absorbs all electromagnetic radiation incident upon it, regardless of wavelength or direction.

It also emits radiation whose spectrum depends on its temperature.

### 2.2 Blackbody Radiation

Blackbody radiation is the electromagnetic radiation emitted by an ideal blackbody due to its temperature.

Examples of approximately blackbody-like emitters:
- Stars.
- Hot furnace openings.
- Heated cavities.

### 2.3 Classical Physics Problem

Classical theories predicted an unrealistic distribution of energy at short wavelengths.

This disagreement became known as the ultraviolet catastrophe.

Classical physics could not correctly explain the observed blackbody spectrum.

### 2.4 Planck's Quantum Hypothesis

Max Planck proposed that energy is exchanged in discrete amounts rather than arbitrary continuous quantities.

The energy of an oscillator can be represented as:

\[
\boxed{E_n=nh\nu}
\]

Where:
- \(n=0,1,2,\ldots\)
- \(h\) = Planck's constant.
- \(\nu\) = Frequency.

The energy difference between adjacent allowed levels is:

\[
\boxed{\Delta E=h\nu}
\]

This hypothesis successfully explained the blackbody radiation spectrum.

## 3. The Photoelectric Effect and Quantum Theory

### 3.1 Definition

The photoelectric effect is the emission of electrons from a material surface when radiation of sufficiently high frequency strikes it.

### 3.2 Experimental Results

The important observations are:

- A minimum threshold frequency is required.
- Emission depends on the frequency of incident radiation.
- Increasing intensity increases the number of emitted electrons under suitable conditions.
- The maximum kinetic energy depends on frequency rather than intensity alone.

### 3.3 Einstein's Explanation

Einstein proposed that electromagnetic radiation consists of photons.

Each photon carries energy:

\[
E=h\nu
\]

The photoelectric equation is:

\[
\boxed{h\nu=\phi+K_{\max}}
\]

Where:
- \(\phi\) = Work function.
- \(K_{\max}\) = Maximum kinetic energy.

This provided strong evidence for the particle nature of electromagnetic radiation.

## 4. Wave-Particle Duality of Radiation

### 4.1 Wave Nature of Light

Light exhibits wave-like behaviour through phenomena such as:
- Interference.
- Diffraction.
- Polarization.

### 4.2 Particle Nature of Light

Light exhibits particle-like behaviour through:
- Photoelectric effect.
- Photon interactions.
- Compton scattering.

### 4.3 Complementarity

Wave and particle descriptions are complementary ways of describing quantum phenomena.

An experiment may reveal wave-like or particle-like behaviour depending on the measurement arrangement.

Neither description alone provides a complete classical picture.

## 5. de Broglie's Matter-Wave Hypothesis

### 5.1 Introduction

Louis de Broglie proposed that particles of matter also possess wave-like properties.

This idea extended wave-particle duality from radiation to matter.

### 5.2 de Broglie Wavelength

The wavelength associated with a moving particle is:

\[
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{h}{p}}
\]

For a non-relativistic particle:

\[
p=mv
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{h}{mv}}
\]

Where:
- \(\lambda\) = de Broglie wavelength.
- \(h\) = Planck's constant.
- \(p\) = Momentum.
- \(m\) = Mass.
- \(v\) = Velocity.

### 5.3 Important Observations

- Wavelength decreases as momentum increases.
- A particle with greater mass generally has a shorter wavelength at the same velocity.
- Wave effects are more noticeable for microscopic particles.
- Macroscopic objects have extremely small de Broglie wavelengths under ordinary conditions.

### 5.4 Numerical Example

Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of an electron moving at \(2\times10^6\) m/s.

Given:

\[
h=6.626\times10^{-34}\text{ J s}
\]

\[
m_e=9.109\times10^{-31}\text{ kg}
\]

\[
v=2\times10^6\text{ m/s}
\]

Using:

\[
\lambda=\frac{h}{mv}
\]

\[
\lambda=
\frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}
{9.109\times10^{-31}\times2\times10^6}
\]

\[
\boxed{\lambda\approx3.64\times10^{-10}\text{ m}}
\]

### 5.5 Experimental Verification

The wave nature of electrons was experimentally demonstrated through electron diffraction.

The Davisson-Germer experiment showed that electrons can produce diffraction patterns when scattered by a crystal.

This provided important experimental support for de Broglie's hypothesis.

## 6. Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

### 6.1 Statement

The Heisenberg uncertainty principle states that the position and momentum of a particle cannot both be determined with arbitrary precision simultaneously.

The more precisely one is known, the greater the uncertainty in the other.

### 6.2 Mathematical Expression

\[
\boxed{\Delta x\Delta p\geq\frac{h}{4\pi}}
\]

Where:
- \(\Delta x\) = Uncertainty in position.
- \(\Delta p\) = Uncertainty in momentum.
- \(h\) = Planck's constant.

Since:

\[
p=mv
\]

For a particle of constant mass:

\[
\boxed{\Delta x\Delta v\geq\frac{h}{4\pi m}}
\]

### 6.3 Energy-Time Uncertainty Relation

Another commonly used uncertainty relation is:

\[
\boxed{\Delta E\Delta t\geq\frac{\hbar}{2}}
\]

Where:

\[
\hbar=\frac{h}{2\pi}
\]

### 6.4 Importance

The uncertainty principle explains why electrons cannot be described as particles moving in perfectly defined classical orbits.

It is a fundamental feature of quantum mechanics, not simply a limitation of measuring instruments.

## 7. Wavefunctions and Probability

### 7.1 Wavefunction

A wavefunction is a mathematical function used to describe the quantum state of a particle.

It is commonly represented by:

\[
\psi
\]

The wavefunction contains information about the possible outcomes of measurements.

### 7.2 Probability Density

The probability density associated with a wavefunction is:

\[
\boxed{|\psi|^2}
\]

For a position-dependent wavefunction, this describes the probability density of finding the particle at a particular position.

### 7.3 Normalization

A physically acceptable wavefunction must be normalized so that the total probability of finding the particle somewhere in the allowed region equals one.

In one dimension:

\[
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|\psi(x)|^2dx=1}
\]

For three-dimensional space:

\[
\boxed{\int |\psi|^2d\tau=1}
\]

### 7.4 Interpretation

- The wavefunction itself is generally not directly observable.
- Its squared magnitude gives probability density.
- A normalized wavefunction describes a total probability of one.
- Atomic orbitals are derived from solutions of quantum mechanical equations.

## 8. Schrödinger Wave Equation

### 8.1 Introduction

Erwin Schrödinger developed a mathematical equation describing the quantum behaviour of particles.

It is one of the fundamental equations of non-relativistic quantum mechanics.

### 8.2 Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation

For a particle in a potential energy field:

\[
\boxed{
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi+V\psi=E\psi
}
\]

Where:
- \(\hbar\) = Reduced Planck's constant.
- \(m\) = Particle mass.
- \(\nabla^2\) = Laplacian operator.
- \(V\) = Potential energy.
- \(\psi\) = Wavefunction.
- \(E\) = Total energy.

### 8.3 Meaning of the Equation

The Schrödinger equation determines the allowed quantum states and corresponding energy values for a specified physical system.

For atoms, solving the equation provides:
- Allowed energy levels.
- Atomic orbitals.
- Electron probability distributions.
- Wavefunctions.

### 8.4 Importance in Chemistry

The Schrödinger equation forms the theoretical foundation for:
- Atomic orbitals.
- Electron configurations.
- Chemical bonding theories.
- Molecular structure.
- Quantum chemistry.

## 9. Quantization of Energy

### 9.1 Meaning

Quantization means that a physical quantity can take only certain permitted discrete values in a particular quantum system.

For example, electrons in atoms occupy allowed energy states rather than arbitrary energies.

### 9.2 Energy Transitions

When an electron moves between two allowed energy levels:

\[
\boxed{\Delta E=E_f-E_i}
\]

For absorption:

\[
E_f>E_i
\]

For emission:

\[
E_f<E_i
\]

The photon energy is equal to the magnitude of the energy difference:

\[
\boxed{E_{\text{photon}}=|\Delta E|=h\nu}
\]

## 10. Practical Applications of Quantum Theory

### 10.1 Semiconductor Technology

Quantum theory explains electronic behaviour in semiconductors used in:
- Transistors.
- Integrated circuits.
- Solar cells.
- Light-emitting diodes.

### 10.2 Lasers

Lasers operate using stimulated emission and quantized energy transitions.

### 10.3 Electron Microscopy

The wave properties of electrons allow electron microscopes to examine structures at very small scales.

### 10.4 Spectroscopy

Quantum theory explains the discrete energy transitions responsible for atomic and molecular spectra.

### 10.5 Modern Materials Science

Quantum mechanical principles help explain:
- Electrical conductivity.
- Superconductivity.
- Magnetic properties.
- Optical properties.
- Nanomaterial behaviour.

## 11. Important Comparisons and Observations

### 11.1 Classical and Quantum Physics

| Classical physics | Quantum physics |
|---|---|
| Mainly describes macroscopic systems. | Describes microscopic systems and quantum phenomena. |
| Often uses continuous physical quantities. | Certain quantities have discrete allowed values. |
| Uses classical trajectories. | Uses quantum states and probability amplitudes. |
| Generally deterministic for specified initial conditions. | Measurement outcomes are generally probabilistic. |
| Cannot explain all atomic phenomena. | Explains atomic structure and microscopic behaviour. |
