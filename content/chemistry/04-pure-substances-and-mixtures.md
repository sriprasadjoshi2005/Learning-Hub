# Lesson 4: Pure Substances and Mixtures

Most materials encountered in everyday life contain one or more chemical substances. Some materials consist of a single pure substance, while others contain multiple components physically combined in different proportions. Understanding the distinction between pure substances and mixtures is essential for chemical analysis, separation techniques, and determining the composition of materials.

This lesson examines elements, compounds, solutions, colloids, and suspensions in greater detail. It also introduces important separation techniques such as filtration, distillation, crystallization, chromatography, centrifugation, and sublimation.

## 1. Pure Substances

### 1.1 Definition

A pure substance is a material consisting of only one chemical substance with a definite composition and characteristic properties.

A pure substance may be:
- An element.
- A compound.

Examples:
- Pure copper.
- Pure oxygen.
- Distilled water.
- Pure sodium chloride.

### 1.2 Characteristics of Pure Substances

1. They have a definite chemical composition.
2. They possess characteristic physical and chemical properties.
3. Their composition does not vary from sample to sample.
4. They cannot be separated into different chemical substances by ordinary physical methods.
5. Pure compounds can be decomposed chemically into simpler substances.

### 1.3 Elements

An element consists of only one type of atom and cannot be chemically decomposed into simpler substances.

Examples:

| Element | Symbol |
|---|---|
| Hydrogen | H |
| Carbon | C |
| Nitrogen | N |
| Oxygen | O |
| Sodium | Na |
| Iron | Fe |
| Copper | Cu |
| Silver | Ag |

Elements may exist as individual atoms or molecules.

Examples:
- Helium: He.
- Neon: Ne.
- Hydrogen: H₂.
- Oxygen: O₂.
- Ozone: O₃.

### 1.4 Compounds

A compound is a pure substance containing two or more different elements chemically combined in a fixed proportion.

Examples:

| Compound | Chemical formula | Constituent elements |
|---|---|---|
| Water | H₂O | Hydrogen and oxygen |
| Carbon dioxide | CO₂ | Carbon and oxygen |
| Ammonia | NH₃ | Nitrogen and hydrogen |
| Sodium chloride | NaCl | Sodium and chlorine |
| Calcium carbonate | CaCO₃ | Calcium, carbon, and oxygen |

### 1.5 Characteristics of Compounds

- They have fixed compositions.
- Their constituent elements are chemically bonded.
- Their properties differ from those of their constituent elements.
- They can generally be decomposed through suitable chemical reactions.
- They are represented using chemical formulae.

## 2. Mixtures

### 2.1 Definition

A mixture is a physical combination of two or more substances in which the components retain their chemical identities.

Examples:
- Air.
- Seawater.
- Soil.
- Milk.
- Brass.
- Sand and salt.

### 2.2 Characteristics of Mixtures

1. Their composition can vary.
2. Their components are not necessarily chemically bonded.
3. Their components generally retain their individual properties.
4. They can often be separated using physical techniques.
5. They do not possess a fixed chemical formula.
6. Their melting and boiling behaviour may vary with composition.

### 2.3 Types of Mixtures

Mixtures are broadly classified into:

**Homogeneous mixtures**

These have uniform composition throughout.

Examples:
- Salt solution.
- Sugar solution.
- Air.
- Brass.

**Heterogeneous mixtures**

These have non-uniform composition.

Examples:
- Oil and water.
- Sand and water.
- Granite.
- Soil.

## 3. Solutions

### 3.1 Definition

A solution is a homogeneous mixture consisting of a solute dissolved in a solvent.

A solution may contain:
- A solid dissolved in a liquid.
- A liquid dissolved in another liquid.
- A gas dissolved in a liquid.
- Gases mixed with other gases.

### 3.2 Components of a Solution

**Solute**

The solute is the substance dissolved in the solvent.

**Solvent**

The solvent is the substance that dissolves the solute and generally determines the physical state of the solution.

Example:

In a sodium chloride solution:
- Sodium chloride is the solute.
- Water is the solvent.

### 3.3 Types of Solutions

| Solute | Solvent | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Gas | Gas | Air |
| Gas | Liquid | Carbon dioxide in water |
| Liquid | Liquid | Ethanol in water |
| Solid | Liquid | Salt in water |
| Solid | Solid | Brass |

### 3.4 Solubility

Solubility is the maximum amount of a solute that can dissolve in a specified amount of solvent under particular conditions of temperature and pressure.

Solutions can be classified as:

- **Unsaturated solution:** Contains less solute than the maximum amount that can dissolve under the specified conditions.
- **Saturated solution:** Contains the maximum amount of dissolved solute at equilibrium under the specified conditions.
- **Supersaturated solution:** Contains more dissolved solute than a saturated solution under the same conditions and is generally unstable.

### 3.5 Factors Affecting Solubility

**Nature of solute and solvent**

The chemical nature of the solute and solvent influences their ability to mix.

A useful general principle is:

"Like dissolves like."

Polar substances tend to dissolve in polar solvents, while non-polar substances tend to dissolve in non-polar solvents.

**Temperature**

The effect of temperature depends on the substances involved.

- Solubility of many solids in liquids increases with temperature.
- Solubility of many gases in liquids decreases with increasing temperature.

**Pressure**

Pressure has a significant effect on the solubility of gases in liquids.

Increasing gas pressure above a liquid generally increases the amount of gas dissolved.

## 4. Colloids and Suspensions

### 4.1 Colloids

A colloid is a dispersion system containing particles distributed throughout another substance.

Examples:
- Milk.
- Fog.
- Smoke.
- Gelatin.
- Paint.

Colloidal systems contain two components:

- Dispersed phase.
- Dispersion medium.

### 4.2 Types of Colloids

| Colloid type | Dispersed phase | Dispersion medium | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sol | Solid | Liquid | Paint |
| Gel | Liquid | Solid | Jelly |
| Emulsion | Liquid | Liquid | Milk |
| Aerosol | Solid or liquid | Gas | Smoke or fog |
| Foam | Gas | Liquid | Soap foam |
| Solid foam | Gas | Solid | Pumice stone |

### 4.3 Tyndall Effect

The Tyndall effect is the scattering of light by colloidal particles.

When a beam of light passes through a colloid, its path may become visible because colloidal particles scatter the light.

Examples:
- Sunlight entering a dusty room.
- Headlights visible in fog.
- Light passing through certain colloidal solutions.

### 4.4 Brownian Movement in Colloids

Colloidal particles exhibit random movement due to collisions with molecules of the dispersion medium.

This movement is called Brownian motion.

It helps prevent colloidal particles from settling under ordinary conditions.

### 4.5 Suspensions

A suspension is a heterogeneous mixture containing relatively large particles dispersed in a medium.

Examples:
- Muddy water.
- Chalk powder in water.
- Sand in water.

Characteristics:
- Particles may be visible.
- Particles settle on standing.
- They scatter light.
- Their components can generally be separated by filtration.

## 5. Separation of Mixtures

Different components of mixtures can be separated by selecting suitable physical methods based on differences in their properties.

Important properties used include:
- Particle size.
- Density.
- Solubility.
- Boiling point.
- Volatility.
- Magnetic behaviour.
- Adsorption.
- Sublimation tendency.

## 6. Important Separation Techniques

### 6.1 Filtration

Filtration separates an insoluble solid from a liquid using a porous material.

Example: Separating sand from water.

**Principle:** Differences in particle size.

During filtration:
- The liquid passing through the filter is called the filtrate.
- The solid retained on the filter is called the residue.

Applications:
- Water purification.
- Separation of precipitates.
- Laboratory separation of insoluble solids.

### 6.2 Evaporation

Evaporation separates a dissolved solid from a liquid by vaporizing the liquid.

Example: Obtaining salt from seawater.

**Principle:** Difference in volatility.

Applications:
- Salt production.
- Recovery of dissolved solids.
- Concentration of solutions.

### 6.3 Crystallization

Crystallization is used to obtain a pure solid in crystalline form from a solution.

The method generally involves:
1. Preparing a suitable solution.
2. Concentrating the solution.
3. Cooling or allowing the solvent to evaporate.
4. Collecting the crystals.
5. Drying the crystals.

Example: Purification of copper sulfate crystals.

Crystallization is generally preferred over simple evaporation when the aim is to obtain a purer crystalline product.

### 6.4 Simple Distillation

Simple distillation separates a liquid from dissolved non-volatile substances or separates liquids with sufficiently different boiling points.

**Principle:** Difference in boiling points and volatility.

Process:
1. Heat the mixture.
2. The more volatile component vaporizes.
3. The vapour enters a condenser.
4. The vapour cools and condenses.
5. The liquid distillate is collected.

Example: Obtaining pure water from a salt solution.

### 6.5 Fractional Distillation

Fractional distillation separates miscible liquids with relatively close boiling points.

A fractionating column provides repeated vaporization and condensation.

Examples:
- Separation of ethanol and water.
- Refining petroleum into different fractions.
- Separation of liquid air into its components.

### 6.6 Separating Funnel

A separating funnel separates immiscible liquids having different densities.

Example: Separation of oil and water.

**Principle:** Difference in density and immiscibility.

The denser liquid generally forms the lower layer and can be released through the stopcock.

### 6.7 Sublimation

Sublimation separates a sublimable solid from non-sublimable substances.

Examples:
- Separation of ammonium chloride from sand.
- Purification of iodine.
- Purification of naphthalene.

**Principle:** Difference in sublimation behaviour.

### 6.8 Chromatography

Chromatography separates components of a mixture based on their different distribution between a stationary phase and a mobile phase.

**Stationary phase:** The phase that remains fixed.

**Mobile phase:** The phase that moves through or over the stationary phase.

Examples:
- Paper chromatography.
- Thin-layer chromatography.
- Column chromatography.
- Gas chromatography.
- High-performance liquid chromatography.

Applications:
- Separation of pigments.
- Drug analysis.
- Food testing.
- Forensic investigations.
- Identification of chemical compounds.

### 6.9 Centrifugation

Centrifugation separates suspended particles from a fluid using rapid rotation.

The separation depends on differences in sedimentation behaviour.

Examples:
- Separation of blood components.
- Separation of cream from milk.
- Laboratory separation of precipitates.

### 6.10 Magnetic Separation

Magnetic separation uses a magnet to separate magnetic substances from non-magnetic materials.

Example: Separating iron filings from sand.

### 6.11 Summary of Separation Methods

| Technique | Principle | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Filtration | Particle size | Sand and water |
| Evaporation | Volatility | Salt from seawater |
| Crystallization | Solubility and crystal formation | Copper sulfate purification |
| Simple distillation | Boiling point difference | Pure water from salt solution |
| Fractional distillation | Close boiling point differences | Petroleum fractions |
| Separating funnel | Immiscibility and density | Oil and water |
| Sublimation | Sublimation tendency | Ammonium chloride and sand |
| Chromatography | Different phase interactions | Ink pigments |
| Centrifugation | Sedimentation behaviour | Blood components |
| Magnetic separation | Magnetic properties | Iron and sand |

## 7. Practical Applications

### 7.1 Water Purification

Filtration, sedimentation, and other separation methods are used to remove suspended impurities from water.

Distillation can produce water with a high degree of purity.

### 7.2 Petroleum Refining

Fractional distillation separates crude petroleum into fractions with different boiling ranges.

These fractions are used to produce:
- Petrol.
- Diesel.
- Kerosene.
- Liquefied petroleum gas.
- Lubricating oils.

### 7.3 Pharmaceutical Analysis

Chromatography helps separate, identify, and quantify components in medicines.

### 7.4 Food Processing

Centrifugation, filtration, evaporation, and crystallization are used in dairy processing, sugar production, and food purification.

## 8. Common Exam Traps

1. Pure substances include both elements and compounds.
2. A solution is a homogeneous mixture.
3. Milk is an emulsion and a colloidal system.
4. The Tyndall effect is caused by scattering of light.
5. Filtration cannot ordinarily separate dissolved salt from water.
6. Crystallization is useful for obtaining pure crystalline solids.
7. Fractional distillation is suitable for separating miscible liquids with close boiling points.
8. A separating funnel is used for immiscible liquids.
9. Chromatography depends on differences in interactions with stationary and mobile phases.
10. Centrifugation uses rapid rotation to separate components.
