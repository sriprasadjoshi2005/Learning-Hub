# Lesson 41: Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

Haloalkanes and haloarenes are organic compounds in which one or more hydrogen atoms are replaced by halogen atoms such as fluorine, chlorine, bromine, or iodine. Their chemistry is important for understanding nucleophilic substitution, elimination, reaction mechanisms, and the relationship between molecular structure and reactivity.

## 1. Classification and Nomenclature

### 1.1 Haloalkanes

Haloalkanes contain a halogen atom bonded to an sp³-hybridised carbon atom.

General representation:

\[
R-X
\]

where \(R\) is an alkyl group and \(X\) is a halogen.

Examples:
- CH₃Cl: chloromethane
- CH₃CH₂Br: bromoethane
- CH₃CHClCH₃: 2-chloropropane

### 1.2 Classification Based on the Carbon Attached to Halogen

- Primary (1°): the carbon attached to halogen is bonded to one other carbon atom.
- Secondary (2°): it is bonded to two other carbon atoms.
- Tertiary (3°): it is bonded to three other carbon atoms.

Examples:

\[
CH_3CH_2Cl \quad (1^\circ)
\]

\[
(CH_3)_2CHCl \quad (2^\circ)
\]

\[
(CH_3)_3CCl \quad (3^\circ)
\]

### 1.3 Haloarenes

Haloarenes contain a halogen directly attached to an aromatic ring.

Examples:
- Chlorobenzene: C₆H₅Cl
- Bromobenzene: C₆H₅Br

A benzyl halide such as C₆H₅CH₂Cl is not a haloarene because the chlorine is attached to the side-chain carbon rather than directly to the aromatic ring.

## 2. Nature of the Carbon–Halogen Bond

The carbon–halogen bond is polar because halogens are more electronegative than carbon.

\[
C^{\delta+}-X^{\delta-}
\]

The carbon atom becomes electron-deficient and can be attacked by nucleophiles.

Bond strength generally decreases down the halogen group:

\[
C-F>C-Cl>C-Br>C-I
\]

Consequently, the ease of bond breaking generally follows:

\[
RI>RBr>RCl>RF
\]

Iodoalkanes are generally more reactive in nucleophilic substitution because the carbon–iodine bond is weaker and iodide is a good leaving group.

## 3. Preparation of Haloalkanes

### 3.1 From Alcohols

Alcohols react with hydrogen halides to form haloalkanes.

\[
ROH+HX\rightarrow RX+H_2O
\]

Example:

\[
CH_3CH_2OH+HBr\rightarrow CH_3CH_2Br+H_2O
\]

Other useful reagents include thionyl chloride, phosphorus trichloride, and phosphorus pentachloride.

\[
ROH+SOCl_2\rightarrow RCl+SO_2+HCl
\]

Thionyl chloride is useful because its gaseous by-products escape, helping isolate the alkyl chloride.

### 3.2 From Alkanes

Alkanes undergo free-radical halogenation in the presence of ultraviolet light or heat.

\[
CH_4+Cl_2\xrightarrow{h\nu}CH_3Cl+HCl
\]

Further substitution may produce dichloromethane, trichloromethane, and tetrachloromethane.

### 3.3 From Alkenes

Hydrogen halides add across carbon–carbon double bonds.

\[
CH_2=CH_2+HBr\rightarrow CH_3CH_2Br
\]

For an unsymmetrical alkene, addition usually follows Markovnikov's rule unless a suitable radical mechanism changes the orientation.

## 4. Nucleophilic Substitution Reactions

A nucleophile is an electron-rich species that donates an electron pair to an electron-deficient atom.

General reaction:

\[
R-X+Nu^-\rightarrow R-Nu+X^-
\]

Examples of nucleophiles include OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃, and RO⁻.

### 4.1 SN2 Mechanism

SN2 means bimolecular nucleophilic substitution.

It occurs in a single concerted step:

1. The nucleophile approaches the carbon from the side opposite the leaving group.
2. A new bond forms as the carbon–halogen bond breaks.
3. The product forms with inversion of configuration at a stereogenic carbon.

Rate law:

\[
Rate=k[RX][Nu^-]
\]

The reaction rate depends on both substrate and nucleophile concentrations.

Typical reactivity:

\[
CH_3X>1^\circ>2^\circ\gg3^\circ
\]

Steric hindrance makes SN2 attack difficult at highly substituted carbon atoms.

### 4.2 SN1 Mechanism

SN1 means unimolecular nucleophilic substitution.

It generally occurs in two stages:

1. The carbon–halogen bond breaks, producing a carbocation.
2. The nucleophile attacks the carbocation.

Rate law:

\[
Rate=k[RX]
\]

The rate-determining step is carbocation formation.

Typical substrate reactivity:

\[
3^\circ>2^\circ\gg1^\circ
\]

Tertiary carbocations are more stable because of electron-releasing inductive effects and hyperconjugation.

A planar carbocation may be attacked from either side, often producing racemisation when the reacting carbon is chiral.

### 4.3 Comparison of SN1 and SN2

| Feature | SN1 | SN2 |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Two main stages | One concerted stage |
| Rate law | \(k[RX]\) | \(k[RX][Nu^-]\) |
| Favoured substrate | Tertiary | Methyl and primary |
| Intermediate | Carbocation | None |
| Stereochemical result | Often racemisation | Inversion |
| Favoured solvent | Polar protic | Polar aprotic |

These are general trends. Actual outcomes depend on substrate, nucleophile, solvent, and reaction conditions.

## 5. Elimination Reactions

Haloalkanes can undergo elimination to form alkenes.

### 5.1 Dehydrohalogenation

When a haloalkane is heated with alcoholic potassium hydroxide, hydrogen and halogen are removed from adjacent carbon atoms.

\[
CH_3CH_2Br+KOH
\xrightarrow{\text{alcoholic, heat}}
CH_2=CH_2+KBr+H_2O
\]

### 5.2 Saytzeff's Rule

When more than one alkene can form, the more substituted alkene is generally the major product.

Example: dehydrohalogenation of 2-bromobutane can produce but-1-ene and but-2-ene. But-2-ene is generally the major product.

### 5.3 Substitution Versus Elimination

- Aqueous KOH commonly favours substitution.
- Alcoholic KOH and heat commonly favour elimination.

The distinction between aqueous and alcoholic conditions is frequently tested.

## 6. Reactions of Haloalkanes

| Reagent or condition | Major product type |
|---|---|
| Aqueous KOH | Alcohol |
| Alcoholic KOH, heat | Alkene |
| KCN | Nitrile |
| AgCN | Isocyanide, generally |
| Excess NH₃ | Primary amine, among possible products |
| Sodium alkoxide | Ether |

Example:

\[
CH_3CH_2Br+KCN\rightarrow CH_3CH_2CN+KBr
\]

The nitrile contains one additional carbon atom compared with the original alkyl group.

## 7. Haloarenes: Structure and Reactivity

### 7.1 Why Haloarenes Are Less Reactive

Haloarenes are less reactive towards ordinary nucleophilic substitution than haloalkanes because:

1. The halogen lone pair participates in resonance with the aromatic ring.
2. The carbon–halogen bond acquires partial double-bond character.
3. The carbon attached to halogen is sp²-hybridised.
4. The bond is shorter and stronger than the corresponding bond in many haloalkanes.

### 7.2 Electrophilic Substitution

Halogens deactivate the benzene ring through their electron-withdrawing inductive effect, but direct incoming electrophiles mainly to the ortho and para positions through resonance donation.

Thus, halogens are deactivating but ortho-para directing.

### 7.3 Nucleophilic Substitution of Chlorobenzene

Chlorobenzene can react with strong nucleophiles under sufficiently vigorous conditions.

For example, under suitable high-temperature and high-pressure conditions:

\[
C_6H_5Cl+NaOH\rightarrow C_6H_5OH+NaCl
\]

The reaction produces phenol after appropriate work-up.

Electron-withdrawing groups at suitable positions, especially ortho and para, can facilitate nucleophilic aromatic substitution.

## 8. Organometallic Compounds

Haloalkanes react with magnesium in dry ether to form Grignard reagents.

\[
RX+Mg\xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}}RMgX
\]

Grignard reagents are powerful carbon-based nucleophiles.

They react with water:

\[
RMgX+H_2O\rightarrow RH+Mg(OH)X
\]

Therefore, Grignard reagents must be prepared and handled under anhydrous conditions.

They are also important for forming carbon–carbon bonds through reactions with carbonyl compounds.

## 9. Environmental and Practical Importance

Some halogen-containing compounds have been used as:
- Refrigerants
- Solvents
- Fire-extinguishing agents
- Pesticides
- Industrial chemical intermediates

Certain chlorofluorocarbons contribute to stratospheric ozone depletion. Their use has therefore been restricted or phased out under international environmental agreements.

Not all halogen-containing compounds have the same environmental effects; their persistence, toxicity, and atmospheric behaviour vary considerably.

## 10. Common Exam Traps

- Do not classify benzyl chloride as a haloarene.
- SN1 rate depends on substrate concentration, not nucleophile concentration.
- SN2 is a one-step mechanism and produces inversion at a stereogenic carbon.
- Tertiary haloalkanes are generally poor SN2 substrates.
- Aqueous KOH and alcoholic KOH usually produce different major reaction types.
- Halogens deactivate benzene but direct electrophilic substitution to ortho and para positions.
- Grignard reagents are destroyed by water.
- KCN and AgCN generally produce different products.

## 11. Summary

- Haloalkanes contain a halogen attached to an sp³ carbon.
- Haloarenes contain a halogen directly attached to an aromatic ring.
- SN1 proceeds through a carbocation, whereas SN2 occurs in one concerted step.
- Elimination produces alkenes.
- Haloarenes are relatively resistant to ordinary nucleophilic substitution.
- Grignard reagents are important for carbon–carbon bond formation.

## Quiz

1. Which substrate generally reacts fastest through an SN2 mechanism?
    - [ ] Tertiary butyl chloride
    - [x] Methyl bromide
    - [ ] Tertiary butyl bromide
    - [ ] 2-bromo-2-methylbutane
    > SN2 reactions are favoured by low steric hindrance, making methyl halides especially reactive.

2. What is the rate law for an SN1 reaction?
    - [x] \(Rate=k[RX]\)
    - [ ] \(Rate=k[Nu^-]\)
    - [ ] \(Rate=k[RX][Nu^-]\)
    - [ ] \(Rate=k[RX]^2\)
    > Carbocation formation is the rate-determining step, so the rate depends on the haloalkane concentration.

3. Why is chlorobenzene less reactive than chloroethane towards nucleophilic substitution?
    - [ ] Chlorine is absent from the aromatic ring.
    - [ ] Chlorobenzene has a weaker carbon–chlorine bond.
    - [x] The carbon–chlorine bond has partial double-bond character and is stronger.
    - [ ] Chlorobenzene contains an ionic carbon–chlorine bond.
    > Resonance gives the carbon–chlorine bond in chlorobenzene partial double-bond character, making bond cleavage more difficult.
