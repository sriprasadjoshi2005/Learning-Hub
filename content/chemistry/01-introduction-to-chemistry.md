# Lesson 1: Introduction to Chemistry

Chemistry is the branch of science that studies the composition, structure, properties, and transformations of matter. It explains how substances are formed, how they interact, why chemical reactions occur, and how energy is involved in these processes. From the air we breathe and the food we consume to medicines, fuels, metals, and advanced materials, chemistry plays an essential role in understanding the natural world and developing modern technology.

This lesson introduces the fundamental ideas of chemistry, its major branches, the nature of matter, physical and chemical properties, and the basic laws that govern chemical reactions. These concepts provide the foundation for understanding more advanced topics such as atomic structure, chemical bonding, thermodynamics, and organic chemistry.

## 1. Introduction to Chemistry

### 1.1 Meaning and Definition of Chemistry

Chemistry is the scientific study of matter and the changes it undergoes.

It primarily deals with five interconnected aspects:

- **Composition:** The substances and elements that make up a material.
- **Structure:** The arrangement of atoms, ions, and molecules within a substance.
- **Properties:** The physical and chemical characteristics of matter.
- **Transformations:** The changes that substances undergo during physical and chemical processes.
- **Energy:** The energy absorbed or released during physical and chemical changes.

For example, when hydrogen reacts with oxygen to form water, chemistry explains the composition of the reactants, the rearrangement of their atoms, the formation of new chemical bonds, and the energy released during the reaction.

### 1.2 Importance and Scope of Chemistry

Chemistry is closely connected with other scientific disciplines and has applications in almost every aspect of modern life.

**Major applications include:**

- **Medicine:** Development of pharmaceutical drugs, vaccines, antibiotics, and diagnostic substances.
- **Agriculture:** Production of fertilizers, pesticides, herbicides, and soil-treatment materials.
- **Food science:** Food preservation, nutritional analysis, flavour development, and quality control.
- **Environmental science:** Analysis of air and water pollution, waste treatment, and environmental protection.
- **Energy:** Development of batteries, solar cells, hydrogen fuel, and alternative energy sources.
- **Materials science:** Production of polymers, ceramics, alloys, semiconductors, and nanomaterials.
- **Industry:** Manufacturing of cement, glass, soaps, detergents, paints, and industrial chemicals.

### 1.3 Major Branches of Chemistry

Chemistry is divided into several specialized branches, each focusing on a particular area of study.

**1. Inorganic Chemistry**

The study of most elements and their compounds, generally excluding the majority of carbon-based organic compounds.

Examples:
- Metals and their compounds.
- Minerals and ores.
- Acids, bases, and salts.
- Coordination compounds.

**2. Organic Chemistry**

The study of carbon-containing compounds, particularly hydrocarbons and their derivatives.

Examples:
- Methane and other hydrocarbons.
- Alcohols and ethers.
- Aldehydes and ketones.
- Polymers and pharmaceuticals.

**3. Physical Chemistry**

The study of the physical principles underlying chemical systems and reactions.

Examples:
- Chemical thermodynamics.
- Chemical kinetics.
- Chemical equilibrium.
- Electrochemistry.

**4. Analytical Chemistry**

The study of methods used to identify substances and determine their composition or concentration.

Examples:
- Titration.
- Chromatography.
- Spectroscopy.
- Qualitative and quantitative analysis.

**5. Biochemistry**

The study of chemical substances and reactions occurring in living organisms.

Examples:
- Proteins and enzymes.
- Carbohydrates and lipids.
- DNA and RNA.
- Cellular metabolism.

**6. Environmental Chemistry**

The study of chemical processes occurring in the environment and the effects of chemical substances on air, water, and soil.

Examples:
- Air pollution.
- Water contamination.
- Acid rain.
- Greenhouse gases.

### 1.4 Chemistry and Other Sciences

Chemistry connects several scientific disciplines.

| Related science | Connection with chemistry |
|---|---|
| Physics | Explains atomic structure, energy, and molecular interactions. |
| Biology | Explains the chemical processes occurring in living organisms. |
| Geology | Studies the chemical composition of rocks, minerals, and Earth's materials. |
| Medicine | Uses chemical knowledge to develop drugs and understand biological processes. |
| Environmental science | Applies chemical principles to understand pollution and environmental changes. |
| Engineering | Uses chemical principles to develop materials, fuels, industrial processes, and technologies. |

## 2. Fundamental Concepts of Matter

### 2.1 Definition of Matter

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space.

Examples include:
- Air
- Water
- Wood
- Metals
- Soil
- Human bodies

Light, heat, and sound are not classified as matter because they are forms of energy rather than substances that possess mass and occupy space.

### 2.2 Composition of Matter

Matter is made up of extremely small particles, primarily atoms, molecules, and ions.

**1. Atom**

An atom is the smallest unit of an element that retains its chemical identity.

Examples:
- Hydrogen atom (H)
- Carbon atom (C)
- Oxygen atom (O)
- Sodium atom (Na)

**2. Molecule**

A molecule is a group of two or more atoms chemically bonded together. A molecule may contain atoms of the same element or different elements.

Examples:

- Hydrogen: H₂
- Oxygen: O₂
- Water: H₂O
- Carbon dioxide: CO₂

**3. Ion**

An ion is an electrically charged atom or group of atoms formed by gaining or losing electrons.

Types of ions:

- **Cation:** A positively charged ion formed by losing electrons.
- **Anion:** A negatively charged ion formed by gaining electrons.

Examples:

\[
Na \rightarrow Na^+ + e^-
\]

\[
Cl + e^- \rightarrow Cl^-
\]

### 2.3 Classification of Matter

Matter can be broadly classified into two major categories:

1. Pure substances
2. Mixtures

**1. Pure Substances**

A pure substance consists of only one type of chemical substance and has a fixed composition and characteristic properties.

Pure substances are classified into elements and compounds.

**Elements**

An element is a pure substance consisting of only one type of atom. It cannot be decomposed into simpler substances by ordinary chemical methods.

Examples:

- Hydrogen (H)
- Oxygen (O)
- Iron (Fe)
- Copper (Cu)
- Gold (Au)

**Compounds**

A compound is a pure substance formed when two or more different elements combine chemically in a fixed proportion by mass.

Examples:

- Water (H₂O)
- Carbon dioxide (CO₂)
- Sodium chloride (NaCl)
- Ammonia (NH₃)

**2. Mixtures**

A mixture is formed when two or more substances combine physically without necessarily undergoing a chemical reaction.

The components of a mixture retain their individual chemical identities and can generally be separated using physical methods.

Examples:

- Air
- Salt water
- Soil
- Brass

Mixtures are classified into two types.

**Homogeneous Mixtures**

A homogeneous mixture has a uniform composition throughout its volume.

Examples:
- Salt dissolved in water.
- Sugar dissolved in water.
- Air.
- Brass.

**Heterogeneous Mixtures**

A heterogeneous mixture has a non-uniform composition, and its different components may be physically distinguishable.

Examples:
- Oil and water.
- Sand and water.
- Soil.
- Granite.

### 2.4 Elements, Compounds, and Mixtures: Comparison

| Property | Element | Compound | Mixture |
|---|---|---|---|
| Composition | One type of atom | Two or more elements chemically combined | Two or more substances physically combined |
| Proportion | Fixed | Fixed | Variable |
| Separation | Cannot be chemically decomposed into simpler substances | Can be chemically decomposed | Generally separated physically |
| Properties | Characteristic properties of the element | Different from constituent elements | Components generally retain their properties |
| Representation | Chemical symbol | Chemical formula | No fixed chemical formula |
| Example | Iron (Fe) | Water (H₂O) | Salt water |

## 3. Physical and Chemical Properties of Matter

Matter exhibits different properties that help identify, classify, and study substances.

### 3.1 Physical Properties

Physical properties are characteristics that can be observed or measured without changing the chemical composition of a substance.

Examples:

- Colour
- Density
- Melting point
- Boiling point
- Hardness
- Electrical conductivity
- Solubility
- Physical state

**Density**

Density is the mass of a substance per unit volume.

\[
\boxed{\rho=\frac{m}{V}}
\]

Where:

- \(\rho\) = Density
- \(m\) = Mass
- \(V\) = Volume

The SI unit of density is kilogram per cubic metre (kg/m³).

Another commonly used unit is gram per cubic centimetre (g/cm³).

**Example:**

A metal block has a mass of 200 g and a volume of 50 cm³. Calculate its density.

Given:

\[
m=200\text{ g}
\]

\[
V=50\text{ cm}^3
\]

Using the density formula:

\[
\rho=\frac{m}{V}
\]

\[
\rho=\frac{200}{50}
\]

\[
\boxed{\rho=4\text{ g/cm}^3}
\]

### 3.2 Chemical Properties

Chemical properties describe the ability of a substance to undergo chemical changes and form new substances.

Examples:

- Flammability
- Reactivity with acids
- Reactivity with oxygen
- Corrosiveness
- Chemical stability
- Oxidation behaviour

For example, iron reacts with oxygen and moisture to form rust.

\[
4Fe+3O_2\rightarrow 2Fe_2O_3
\]

The formation of iron oxide demonstrates a chemical property of iron.

### 3.3 Physical Changes

A physical change alters the physical form, appearance, or state of a substance without changing its chemical composition.

Examples:

- Melting of ice.
- Boiling of water.
- Cutting paper.
- Dissolving sugar in water.
- Breaking glass.

For example:

\[
H_2O(s)\rightarrow H_2O(l)
\]

Ice changes into liquid water, but its chemical composition remains H₂O.

### 3.4 Chemical Changes

A chemical change occurs when one or more substances are converted into new substances with different chemical compositions and properties.

Examples:

- Burning of wood.
- Rusting of iron.
- Digestion of food.
- Cooking an egg.
- Reaction between an acid and a base.

For example, hydrogen reacts with oxygen to form water.

\[
2H_2+O_2\rightarrow 2H_2O
\]

The original substances are converted into a new substance.

### 3.5 Physical and Chemical Changes: Comparison

| Physical change | Chemical change |
|---|---|
| Chemical composition remains unchanged. | Chemical composition changes. |
| No new substance is formed. | One or more new substances are formed. |
| Often involves changes in shape, size, or state. | Involves chemical reactions. |
| Usually easier to reverse, although not always. | Often difficult to reverse, although some chemical reactions are reversible. |
| Example: Melting ice. | Example: Rusting iron. |

**Important observation:** Reversibility alone cannot determine whether a change is physical or chemical. The formation of new substances is the more reliable distinction.

## 4. Important Principles and Laws of Chemistry

Several fundamental laws explain the quantitative relationships involved in chemical reactions.

### 4.1 Law of Conservation of Mass

The law of conservation of mass states that mass can neither be created nor destroyed during an ordinary chemical reaction.

Therefore, the total mass of the reactants is equal to the total mass of the products in a closed system.

\[
\boxed{\text{Total mass of reactants}=\text{Total mass of products}}
\]

**Example:**

Consider the formation of water.

\[
2H_2+O_2\rightarrow 2H_2O
\]

Suppose:

- Mass of hydrogen = 4 g
- Mass of oxygen = 32 g

Total mass of reactants:

\[
4+32=36\text{ g}
\]

Therefore, the total mass of water formed is:

\[
\boxed{36\text{ g}}
\]

**Applications:**

- Balancing chemical equations.
- Performing stoichiometric calculations.
- Understanding mass relationships in chemical reactions.
- Determining the quantities of reactants and products.

### 4.2 Law of Definite Proportions

The law of definite proportions, also known as the law of constant composition, states that a pure chemical compound always contains the same elements combined in a fixed proportion by mass, regardless of its source or method of preparation.

**Example:**

Water always contains hydrogen and oxygen in a mass ratio of 1:8.

For every 1 g of hydrogen, 8 g of oxygen combine to form 9 g of water.

\[
\text{Mass ratio of H:O}=1:8
\]

This ratio remains constant whether the water is obtained from rain, rivers, oceans, or chemical reactions.

**Applications:**

- Determining the composition of chemical compounds.
- Calculating the mass of constituent elements.
- Distinguishing pure compounds from mixtures.

### 4.3 Law of Multiple Proportions

The law of multiple proportions states that when two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other element are in a simple whole-number ratio.

**Example:**

Carbon and oxygen form two common compounds:

1. Carbon monoxide (CO)
2. Carbon dioxide (CO₂)

In carbon monoxide:

- 12 g of carbon combines with 16 g of oxygen.

In carbon dioxide:

- 12 g of carbon combines with 32 g of oxygen.

For a fixed mass of carbon, the ratio of oxygen masses is:

\[
16:32=1:2
\]

Therefore, the ratio is a simple whole number.

\[
\boxed{1:2}
\]

### 4.4 Law of Reciprocal Proportions

The law of reciprocal proportions states that when two different elements separately combine with a fixed mass of a third element, the masses of the two elements are either in the same ratio or in a simple whole-number multiple of the ratio in which they combine with each other.

This law demonstrates the consistency of combining mass relationships in chemical compounds.

### 4.5 Gay-Lussac's Law of Combining Volumes

Gay-Lussac's law states that when gases react, the volumes of reacting gases and gaseous products, measured at the same temperature and pressure, are in simple whole-number ratios.

**Example:**

Hydrogen reacts with oxygen to form water vapour.

\[
2H_2(g)+O_2(g)\rightarrow 2H_2O(g)
\]

The volume ratio is:

\[
\boxed{2:1:2}
\]

This means:

- 2 volumes of hydrogen react with
- 1 volume of oxygen to produce
- 2 volumes of water vapour.

### 4.6 Avogadro's Law

Avogadro's law states that equal volumes of all gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules.

Mathematically:

\[
V\propto n
\]

At constant temperature and pressure:

\[
\boxed{\frac{V}{n}=\text{constant}}
\]

Where:

- \(V\) = Volume of gas.
- \(n\) = Number of moles.

This law forms an important foundation for understanding the mole concept and gaseous behaviour.

## 5. Mathematical Derivations and Numerical Examples

Basic mathematical relationships are essential in chemistry because chemical reactions involve measurable quantities such as mass, volume, density, and temperature.

### 5.1 Density

Density is calculated using:

\[
\boxed{\rho=\frac{m}{V}}
\]

Rearranging:

\[
\boxed{m=\rho V}
\]

\[
\boxed{V=\frac{m}{\rho}}
\]

These three forms are useful depending on which quantity is unknown.

**Example 1: Calculating Density**

A liquid has a mass of 150 g and occupies a volume of 100 cm³. Calculate its density.

Given:

\[
m=150\text{ g}
\]

\[
V=100\text{ cm}^3
\]

\[
\rho=\frac{150}{100}
\]

\[
\boxed{\rho=1.5\text{ g/cm}^3}
\]

**Example 2: Calculating Mass**

A substance has a density of 2.5 g/cm³ and occupies a volume of 20 cm³. Calculate its mass.

Given:

\[
\rho=2.5\text{ g/cm}^3
\]

\[
V=20\text{ cm}^3
\]

Using:

\[
m=\rho V
\]

\[
m=2.5\times20
\]

\[
\boxed{m=50\text{ g}}
\]

### 5.2 Conservation of Mass Calculations

**Example:**

In a chemical reaction, 12 g of carbon reacts completely with 32 g of oxygen. Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide formed.

Given:

\[
m_{\text{carbon}}=12\text{ g}
\]

\[
m_{\text{oxygen}}=32\text{ g}
\]

According to the law of conservation of mass:

\[
m_{\text{reactants}}=m_{\text{products}}
\]

Therefore:

\[
m_{\text{CO}_2}=12+32
\]

\[
\boxed{m_{\text{CO}_2}=44\text{ g}}
\]

### 5.3 Mass Ratio Calculations

**Example:**

Water contains hydrogen and oxygen in a mass ratio of 1:8. Calculate the mass of oxygen present in 45 g of water.

Total ratio:

\[
1+8=9
\]

Mass of oxygen:

\[
m_O=\frac{8}{9}\times45
\]

\[
\boxed{m_O=40\text{ g}}
\]

Mass of hydrogen:

\[
m_H=\frac{1}{9}\times45
\]

\[
\boxed{m_H=5\text{ g}}
\]

Verification:

\[
40+5=45\text{ g}
\]

## 6. Practical Applications of Basic Chemistry

The fundamental concepts introduced in this lesson are used in many practical situations.

### 6.1 Chemical Manufacturing

Chemical industries use knowledge of matter, chemical reactions, and mass relationships to manufacture products such as fertilizers, plastics, medicines, and industrial solvents.

### 6.2 Medicine and Healthcare

Chemistry helps determine the composition, purity, stability, and appropriate concentration of pharmaceutical substances.

### 6.3 Environmental Protection

Chemical analysis is used to detect pollutants, measure water quality, identify harmful gases, and develop methods for waste treatment.

### 6.4 Food and Nutrition

Chemistry helps determine the nutritional composition of food, understand food preservation, and study chemical changes during cooking.

### 6.5 Energy and Technology

Chemical principles are used in batteries, fuel cells, combustion engines, solar-energy materials, and the development of advanced electronic components.

## 7. Important Comparisons and Observations

### 7.1 Physical and Chemical Properties

| Physical properties | Chemical properties |
|---|---|
| Observed without changing chemical composition. | Describe the ability to undergo chemical reactions. |
| Include density, colour, and melting point. | Include flammability, acidity, and reactivity. |
| Do not require the formation of a new substance. | Involve the potential formation of new substances. |

### 7.2 Intensive and Extensive Properties

Physical properties can also be classified according to their dependence on the amount of matter.

**Intensive Properties**

These properties do not depend on the quantity of the substance.

Examples:
- Density.
- Temperature.
- Pressure.
- Melting point.
- Boiling point.

**Extensive Properties**

These properties depend on the quantity of the substance.

Examples:
- Mass.
- Volume.
- Total energy.
- Length.

| Intensive properties | Extensive properties |
|---|---|
| Independent of the amount of matter. | Dependent on the amount of matter. |
| Density, temperature, and pressure. | Mass, volume, and total energy. |
| Generally do not add when two identical samples are combined. | Generally add when two samples are combined. |

### 7.3 Important Scientific Observations

- Matter consists of particles that are constantly in motion.
- Elements are the fundamental chemical building blocks of compounds.
- Compounds have fixed compositions and characteristic properties.
- Mixtures can have variable compositions.
- Physical changes do not alter chemical composition.
- Chemical reactions involve the rearrangement of atoms.
- Mass is conserved during ordinary chemical reactions in a closed system.
- Chemical measurements require appropriate units and instruments.

## 8. Common Exam Traps

1. **Matter versus energy:** Light and heat are forms of energy, not matter.

2. **Elements versus compounds:** An element contains one type of atom, whereas a compound contains two or more different elements chemically combined.

3. **Compounds versus mixtures:** Compounds have fixed chemical compositions, whereas mixtures can have variable compositions.

4. **Homogeneous versus heterogeneous mixtures:** A homogeneous mixture has uniform composition, while a heterogeneous mixture does not.

5. **Physical versus chemical changes:** A change of state is generally physical because the chemical identity of the substance remains unchanged.

6. **Reversibility:** Not every physical change is reversible, and reversibility alone does not determine whether a change is physical or chemical.

7. **Conservation of mass:** The total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products in a closed system.

8. **Definite proportions:** A pure compound always has a fixed mass ratio of its constituent elements.

9. **Multiple proportions:** The masses of one element combining with a fixed mass of another element must have a simple whole-number ratio when multiple compounds are formed.

10. **SI units:** The SI unit of amount of substance is the mole, not the gram.
