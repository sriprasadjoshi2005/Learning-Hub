# Lesson 25: Solubility Product and Precipitation

The solubility of ionic compounds determines how much of a substance can dissolve in a solvent. Some salts dissolve readily, while others dissolve only to a very small extent. Solubility equilibrium, the solubility product constant, and precipitation reactions provide a quantitative way to understand these processes.

## 1. Introduction to Solubility

Solubility is the maximum amount of a solute that can dissolve in a specified amount of solvent at a given temperature to form a saturated solution.

### 1.1 Types of Solutions

- Unsaturated solution: contains less solute than the saturation limit.
- Saturated solution: contains the maximum dissolved amount at equilibrium.
- Supersaturated solution: contains more dissolved solute than normally permitted at equilibrium and is metastable.

### 1.2 Solubility of Ionic Compounds

When an ionic compound dissolves, its ions separate and become surrounded by solvent molecules.

Example:

\[
NaCl(s)\rightleftharpoons Na^+(aq)+Cl^-(aq)
\]

For a sparingly soluble salt, dissolution establishes an equilibrium between the undissolved solid and dissolved ions.

## 2. Solubility Equilibrium

Consider a sparingly soluble salt:

\[
AB(s)\rightleftharpoons A^+(aq)+B^-(aq)
\]

At equilibrium, the rates of dissolution and precipitation are equal.

The equilibrium constant for this process is related to the concentrations of the dissolved ions.

The pure solid is omitted from the equilibrium expression.

## 3. Solubility Product Constant

The solubility product constant, \(K_{sp}\), is the product of the equilibrium ionic concentrations, each raised to its stoichiometric coefficient.

For:

\[
AB(s)\rightleftharpoons A^++B^-
\]

\[
\boxed{K_{sp}=[A^+][B^-]}
\]

For:

\[
A_2B(s)\rightleftharpoons2A^++B^{2-}
\]

\[
\boxed{K_{sp}=[A^+]^2[B^{2-}]}
\]

For:

\[
AB_2(s)\rightleftharpoons A^{2+}+2B^-
\]

\[
\boxed{K_{sp}=[A^{2+}][B^-]^2}
\]

The solubility product is defined for a particular temperature.

## 4. Relationship Between Solubility and \(K_{sp}\)

Molar solubility is the number of moles of solute dissolved per litre of saturated solution.

### 4.1 Salt with 1:1 Ion Ratio

For:

\[
AB(s)\rightleftharpoons A^++B^-
\]

Let the molar solubility be s.

Then:

\[
[A^+]=s
\]

\[
[B^-]=s
\]

Therefore:

\[
K_{sp}=s^2
\]

\[
\boxed{s=\sqrt{K_{sp}}}
\]

### 4.2 Salt with 1:2 Ion Ratio

For:

\[
AB_2(s)\rightleftharpoons A^{2+}+2B^-
\]

If the molar solubility is s:

\[
[A^{2+}]=s
\]

\[
[B^-]=2s
\]

Therefore:

\[
K_{sp}=s(2s)^2
\]

\[
\boxed{K_{sp}=4s^3}
\]

### 4.3 Salt with 2:3 Ion Ratio

For:

\[
A_2B_3(s)\rightleftharpoons2A^{3+}+3B^{2-}
\]

The equilibrium concentrations are:

\[
[A^{3+}]=2s
\]

\[
[B^{2-}]=3s
\]

Therefore:

\[
K_{sp}=(2s)^2(3s)^3
\]

\[
\boxed{K_{sp}=108s^5}
\]

The stoichiometry must always be considered when relating solubility to \(K_{sp}\).

## 5. Ionic Product and Precipitation

The ionic product Q is calculated using the current ion concentrations.

It has the same form as the \(K_{sp}\) expression.

For:

\[
AB(s)\rightleftharpoons A^++B^-
\]

\[
Q=[A^+][B^-]
\]

The comparison between Q and \(K_{sp}\) predicts whether precipitation occurs.

| Condition | Result |
|---|---|
| \(Q<K_{sp}\) | Solution is unsaturated |
| \(Q=K_{sp}\) | Solution is saturated and at equilibrium |
| \(Q>K_{sp}\) | Precipitation is thermodynamically favoured |

## 6. Common-Ion Effect

The common-ion effect is the reduction in the solubility of a sparingly soluble salt when another electrolyte containing a common ion is added.

Consider:

\[
AgCl(s)\rightleftharpoons Ag^++Cl^-
\]

Adding NaCl increases the chloride-ion concentration.

The equilibrium shifts towards solid AgCl, reducing its solubility.

This is an application of Le Chatelier's principle.

## 7. Effect of pH on Solubility

The solubility of salts containing basic anions can increase in acidic solutions.

For example:

\[
CaCO_3(s)\rightleftharpoons Ca^{2+}+CO_3^{2-}
\]

Carbonate ions react with hydrogen ions:

\[
CO_3^{2-}+H^+\rightleftharpoons HCO_3^-
\]

Removing carbonate ions from the dissolution equilibrium can promote further dissolution of calcium carbonate.

Salts containing anions that are conjugate bases of weak acids are particularly likely to show pH-dependent solubility.

## 8. Selective Precipitation

Selective precipitation separates ions by choosing conditions under which one ion forms a precipitate before another.

The method depends on:
- Solubility products.
- Ion concentrations.
- Reagent concentration.
- pH.
- Formation of complexes where relevant.

It is widely used in qualitative inorganic analysis.

## 9. Worked Example

The solubility product of a 1:1 salt AB is:

\[
K_{sp}=4.0\times10^{-10}
\]

Calculate its molar solubility in pure water, assuming ideal behaviour.

For:

\[
AB(s)\rightleftharpoons A^++B^-
\]

Let the solubility be s.

\[
K_{sp}=s^2
\]

\[
s=\sqrt{4.0\times10^{-10}}
\]

\[
\boxed{s=2.0\times10^{-5}\text{ mol L}^{-1}}
\]

## 10. Applications

- Qualitative inorganic analysis.
- Water hardness treatment.
- Removal of unwanted ions from wastewater.
- Pharmaceutical formulation.
- Mineral formation and geological processes.
- Industrial crystallisation.
- Separation of metal ions.

## 11. Common Exam Traps

- \(K_{sp}\) expressions include dissolved ions, not pure solids.
- The exponents in the expression come from balanced stoichiometric coefficients.
- Molar solubility and \(K_{sp}\) are not interchangeable.
- A smaller \(K_{sp}\) does not always mean lower molar solubility when salt stoichiometries differ.
- Precipitation is favoured when Q exceeds \(K_{sp}\).
- Adding a common ion generally decreases the solubility of a sparingly soluble salt.
- Temperature can change \(K_{sp}\).
