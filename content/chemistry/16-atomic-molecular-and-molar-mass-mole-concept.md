# Lesson 16: Atomic, Molecular and Molar Mass and the Mole Concept

Chemistry involves enormous numbers of atoms, molecules, and ions. Counting these particles individually is impossible in ordinary laboratory work. Chemists therefore use the mole as a standard counting unit.

The mole connects the microscopic world of atoms and molecules with measurable laboratory quantities such as mass. Atomic mass, molecular mass, formula mass, molar mass, and the Avogadro constant are essential concepts for chemical calculations.

## 1. Atomic Mass

### 1.1 Relative Atomic Mass

Atoms are extremely small, so their masses are expressed using a relative scale.

The unified atomic mass unit, symbol \(u\), is defined as one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

\[
1u=\frac{1}{12}\times\text{mass of one }^{12}C\text{ atom}
\]

The relative atomic mass of an element is the weighted average mass of its atoms compared with one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

It is commonly denoted by \(A_r\).

### 1.2 Isotopes and Average Atomic Mass

Isotopes are atoms of the same element having the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

The atomic mass shown in the periodic table is generally a weighted average based on naturally occurring isotopic abundances.

\[
\boxed{A_r=\frac{\sum(\text{isotopic mass}\times\text{fractional abundance})}{\sum\text{fractional abundances}}}
\]

When abundances are expressed as percentages:

\[
A_r=\frac{\sum(\text{isotopic mass}\times\%\text{abundance})}{100}
\]

**Worked Example**

An element has two isotopes:

- Isotope X: mass = 10 u, abundance = 20%.
- Isotope Y: mass = 11 u, abundance = 80%.

Average atomic mass:

\[
A_r=\frac{10(20)+11(80)}{100}
\]

\[
A_r=\frac{200+880}{100}
\]

\[
\boxed{A_r=10.8}
\]

## 2. Molecular Mass and Formula Mass

### 2.1 Molecular Mass

Molecular mass is the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms present in one molecule.

It is expressed in unified atomic mass units.

\[
\boxed{\text{Molecular mass}=\sum\text{atomic masses}}
\]

**Example: Water (H₂O)**

Using:

- H = 1 u.
- O = 16 u.

\[
M_r=2(1)+16
\]

\[
\boxed{M_r=18u}
\]

**Example: Carbon dioxide (CO₂)**

\[
M_r=12+2(16)
\]

\[
\boxed{M_r=44u}
\]

### 2.2 Formula Mass

Ionic compounds generally do not exist as separate molecules. They form extended ionic lattices.

Therefore, their mass is represented using formula mass, calculated from the chemical formula.

**Example: Sodium chloride (NaCl)**

\[
\text{Formula mass}=23+35.5
\]

\[
\boxed{58.5u}
\]

**Example: Calcium chloride (CaCl₂)**

\[
\text{Formula mass}=40+2(35.5)
\]

\[
\boxed{111u}
\]

## 3. The Mole Concept

### 3.1 Definition of a Mole

The mole is the SI unit for amount of substance.

One mole contains exactly:

\[
\boxed{6.02214076\times10^{23}}
\]

specified elementary entities.

These entities may be:
- Atoms.
- Molecules.
- Ions.
- Electrons.
- Formula units.
- Other specified particles.

This number is called the Avogadro constant.

### 3.2 Avogadro Constant

The Avogadro constant is represented by \(N_A\).

\[
\boxed{N_A=6.02214076\times10^{23}\ mol^{-1}}
\]

For most introductory calculations, it is approximated as:

\[
N_A\approx6.022\times10^{23}\ mol^{-1}
\]

Therefore:
- One mole of carbon atoms contains \(N_A\) carbon atoms.
- One mole of water contains \(N_A\) water molecules.
- One mole of sodium chloride contains \(N_A\) formula units.

### 3.3 Why the Mole Is Important

The mole connects the number of particles to measurable mass.

It allows chemists to:
- Calculate the number of atoms and molecules.
- Determine the mass of a substance.
- Calculate reactant and product quantities.
- Prepare solutions of known concentration.
- Perform quantitative chemical experiments.

## 4. Molar Mass

### 4.1 Definition

Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance.

It is represented by \(M\) and commonly expressed in:

\[
g\,mol^{-1}
\]

The numerical value of molar mass in grams per mole is equal to the relative atomic, molecular, or formula mass.

For example:

| Substance | Relative mass | Molar mass |
|---|---:|---:|
| Carbon | 12 | 12 g mol⁻¹ |
| Water | 18 | 18 g mol⁻¹ |
| CO₂ | 44 | 44 g mol⁻¹ |
| NaCl | 58.5 | 58.5 g mol⁻¹ |

### 4.2 Relationship Between Mass, Moles, and Molar Mass

The fundamental equation is:

\[
\boxed{n=\frac{m}{M}}
\]

Where:
- \(n\) = Amount of substance in moles.
- \(m\) = Mass in grams.
- \(M\) = Molar mass in g mol⁻¹.

Rearranging:

\[
\boxed{m=nM}
\]

And:

\[
\boxed{M=\frac{m}{n}}
\]

### 4.3 Worked Example: Number of Moles

Calculate the number of moles in 36 g of water.

Molar mass of water:

\[
M=18\,g\,mol^{-1}
\]

Given:

\[
m=36\,g
\]

\[
n=\frac{36}{18}
\]

\[
\boxed{n=2\,mol}
\]

### 4.4 Worked Example: Mass of a Substance

Calculate the mass of 0.5 mol of carbon dioxide.

\[
M(CO_2)=44\,g\,mol^{-1}
\]

\[
m=nM
\]

\[
m=0.5\times44
\]

\[
\boxed{m=22\,g}
\]

## 5. Relationship Between Moles and Number of Particles

### 5.1 Basic Formula

The number of particles is calculated using:

\[
\boxed{N=nN_A}
\]

Where:
- \(N\) = Number of particles.
- \(n\) = Number of moles.
- \(N_A\) = Avogadro constant.

Rearranging:

\[
\boxed{n=\frac{N}{N_A}}
\]

### 5.2 Worked Example

Calculate the number of molecules in 2 mol of water.

\[
N=nN_A
\]

\[
N=2\times6.022\times10^{23}
\]

\[
\boxed{N=1.2044\times10^{24}\text{ molecules}}
\]

### 5.3 Number of Atoms in a Molecule

The number of atoms can be calculated by multiplying the number of molecules by the number of atoms in each molecule.

For water:

\[
H_2O
\]

Each molecule contains three atoms.

Therefore, 1 mol of water contains:

\[
3N_A
\]

total atoms.

The number of hydrogen atoms is:

\[
2N_A
\]

The number of oxygen atoms is:

\[
N_A
\]

## 6. Relationship Between Mass and Number of Particles

Combining the mole equations:

\[
n=\frac{m}{M}
\]

and:

\[
N=nN_A
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{N=\frac{mN_A}{M}}
\]

This equation directly connects mass with the number of particles.

**Worked Example**

Calculate the number of molecules in 9 g of water.

Molar mass:

\[
M=18\,g\,mol^{-1}
\]

Number of moles:

\[
n=\frac{9}{18}=0.5\,mol
\]

Number of molecules:

\[
N=0.5\times6.022\times10^{23}
\]

\[
\boxed{N=3.011\times10^{23}}
\]

## 7. Mole Concept for Gases

### 7.1 Molar Volume

The molar volume of a gas is the volume occupied by one mole of that gas under specified conditions of temperature and pressure.

At approximately 273.15 K and 1 atm, an ideal gas occupies about:

\[
22.414\,L\,mol^{-1}
\]

At 273.15 K and 1 bar, the ideal-gas molar volume is approximately:

\[
22.711\,L\,mol^{-1}
\]

Always check the temperature and pressure conditions before using a molar-volume value.

### 7.2 Volume-Mole Relationship

At the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of ideal gases contain equal numbers of molecules.

For a gas at specified conditions:

\[
n=\frac{V}{V_m}
\]

Where:
- \(V\) = Gas volume.
- \(V_m\) = Molar volume at the specified conditions.

### 7.3 Worked Example

Calculate the number of moles in 11.2 L of an ideal gas at 273.15 K and 1 atm.

Given:

\[
V_m=22.4\,L\,mol^{-1}
\]

\[
n=\frac{11.2}{22.4}
\]

\[
\boxed{n=0.5\,mol}
\]

## 8. Combining the Mole Equations

The most useful mole relationships are:

\[
\boxed{n=\frac{m}{M}}
\]

\[
\boxed{N=nN_A}
\]

\[
\boxed{n=\frac{V}{V_m}}
\]

The correct equation depends on the information provided.

| Given information | Required quantity | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| Mass and molar mass | Moles | \(n=m/M\) |
| Moles | Mass | \(m=nM\) |
| Moles | Number of particles | \(N=nN_A\) |
| Number of particles | Moles | \(n=N/N_A\) |
| Gas volume and molar volume | Moles | \(n=V/V_m\) |

## 9. Common Exam Traps

1. **Do not confuse atomic mass with molar mass.** One is expressed in u, while the other is expressed in g mol⁻¹.
2. **Ionic compounds are described using formula units, not individual molecules.**
3. **One mole does not always mean one mole of atoms.** The specified particles must be identified.
4. **One mole of H₂ contains one mole of H₂ molecules but two moles of hydrogen atoms.**
5. **Avogadro's constant applies to the specified elementary entities.**
6. **Molar volume depends on temperature and pressure.**
7. **Always use consistent units in calculations.**
8. **The mole is an amount of substance, not a unit of mass.**

## Quiz

1. How many particles are present in one mole of a substance?
   - [ ] \(6.022\times10^{20}\)
   - [ ] \(3.011\times10^{23}\)
   - [x] \(6.022\times10^{23}\)
   - [ ] \(1.2044\times10^{24}\)

   > One mole contains exactly \(6.02214076\times10^{23}\) specified elementary entities, known as the Avogadro constant.

2. How many moles are present in 36 g of water? (Molar mass = 18 g mol⁻¹)
   - [ ] 0.5 mol
   - [x] 2 mol
   - [ ] 18 mol
   - [ ] 36 mol

   > The number of moles is calculated using \(n=m/M\). Therefore, \(36/18=2\) mol.

3. How many total atoms are present in one mole of water molecules?
   - [ ] \(N_A\)
   - [ ] \(2N_A\)
   - [x] \(3N_A\)
   - [ ] \(4N_A\)

   > Each water molecule contains two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom, giving three atoms per molecule. Therefore, one mole of water contains \(3N_A\) atoms.
