# Lesson 9: Quantum Numbers, Orbitals, and Electronic Configuration

The modern quantum mechanical model describes electrons using wavefunctions and probability distributions rather than fixed circular orbits. These descriptions provide a systematic way to understand the arrangement of electrons within atoms.

Quantum numbers identify the allowed states of electrons, while atomic orbitals describe regions where electrons are most likely to be found. Electronic configuration explains how electrons are distributed among shells, subshells, and orbitals.

This lesson introduces the four quantum numbers, types of atomic orbitals, orbital shapes, electron configurations, and the fundamental principles governing electron filling.

## 1. Atomic Shells and Subshells

### 1.1 Principal Energy Levels

Electrons in atoms occupy different principal energy levels, commonly called shells.

These levels are represented by the principal quantum number:

\[
n=1,2,3,4,\ldots
\]

The shells are also represented by letters:

| Principal quantum number | Shell name |
|---|---|
| 1 | K |
| 2 | L |
| 3 | M |
| 4 | N |
| 5 | O |
| 6 | P |
| 7 | Q |

The principal quantum number describes the main energy level and is related to the size and energy of an orbital.

### 1.2 Subshells

Each principal shell contains one or more subshells.

The subshells are represented by:

- s
- p
- d
- f

The number of subshells in a shell equals its principal quantum number.

Examples:

- Shell 1 contains 1s.
- Shell 2 contains 2s and 2p.
- Shell 3 contains 3s, 3p, and 3d.
- Shell 4 contains 4s, 4p, 4d, and 4f.

### 1.3 Orbitals

An orbital is a region of space described by an atomic wavefunction in which an electron has a particular probability distribution.

Each orbital can accommodate a maximum of two electrons with opposite spins.

## 2. The Four Quantum Numbers

An electron in an atom is described using four quantum numbers.

1. Principal quantum number.
2. Azimuthal quantum number.
3. Magnetic quantum number.
4. Spin quantum number.

### 2.1 Principal Quantum Number (n)

The principal quantum number identifies the main energy level or shell.

Allowed values:

\[
\boxed{n=1,2,3,4,\ldots}
\]

It provides information about:
- Main energy level.
- Approximate orbital size.
- Average distance of an electron from the nucleus.

The maximum number of electrons in a shell is:

\[
\boxed{2n^2}
\]

Examples:

For \(n=1\):

\[
2(1)^2=2
\]

For \(n=2\):

\[
2(2)^2=8
\]

For \(n=3\):

\[
2(3)^2=18
\]

For \(n=4\):

\[
2(4)^2=32
\]

### 2.2 Azimuthal Quantum Number (l)

The azimuthal quantum number identifies the subshell and is related to orbital shape and orbital angular momentum.

Allowed values:

\[
\boxed{l=0,1,2,\ldots,n-1}
\]

The subshell correspondence is:

| Value of l | Subshell |
|---|---|
| 0 | s |
| 1 | p |
| 2 | d |
| 3 | f |

Examples:

For \(n=1\):

\[
l=0
\]

Only the s subshell is possible.

For \(n=2\):

\[
l=0,1
\]

The possible subshells are 2s and 2p.

For \(n=3\):

\[
l=0,1,2
\]

The possible subshells are 3s, 3p, and 3d.

### 2.3 Magnetic Quantum Number (mₗ)

The magnetic quantum number describes the orientation of an orbital in space.

Allowed values:

\[
\boxed{m_l=-l,\ldots,0,\ldots,+l}
\]

The total number of possible values is:

\[
\boxed{2l+1}
\]

Therefore, the number of orbitals in each subshell is:

| Subshell | l | Possible mₗ values | Number of orbitals |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| s | 0 | 0 | 1 |
| p | 1 | -1, 0, +1 | 3 |
| d | 2 | -2, -1, 0, +1, +2 | 5 |
| f | 3 | -3, -2, -1, 0, +1, +2, +3 | 7 |

### 2.4 Spin Quantum Number (mₛ)

The spin quantum number describes the intrinsic spin state of an electron.

Its permitted values are:

\[
\boxed{m_s=+\frac12\text{ or }-\frac12}
\]

The two possible spin states are often represented by:

- Spin up: ↑
- Spin down: ↓

An orbital can accommodate two electrons only when they have opposite spin quantum numbers.

## 3. Summary of Quantum Numbers

| Quantum number | Symbol | Allowed values | Main significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Principal | n | 1, 2, 3, ... | Main energy level |
| Azimuthal | l | 0 to n - 1 | Subshell and orbital shape |
| Magnetic | mₗ | -l to +l | Orbital orientation |
| Spin | mₛ | +1/2 or -1/2 | Electron spin state |

## 4. Atomic Orbitals and Their Shapes

### 4.1 s-Orbitals

An s-orbital has a spherical probability distribution.

Characteristics:
- Spherical shape.
- One orbital per s subshell.
- Maximum capacity of two electrons.
- Exists in every principal shell.

Examples:
- 1s.
- 2s.
- 3s.

### 4.2 p-Orbitals

A p-orbital generally has a dumbbell-shaped probability distribution.

Characteristics:
- Three orbitals in each p subshell.
- Three mutually perpendicular orientations.
- Maximum capacity of six electrons.

The orbitals are commonly labelled:

- \(p_x\)
- \(p_y\)
- \(p_z\)

### 4.3 d-Orbitals

A d subshell contains five orbitals.

Most d-orbitals have four-lobed shapes, while the \(d_{z^2}\) orbital has a different shape involving two lobes and a central ring-like region.

Maximum electron capacity:

\[
5\times2=10
\]

### 4.4 f-Orbitals

An f subshell contains seven orbitals.

These orbitals generally have more complex angular shapes.

Maximum electron capacity:

\[
7\times2=14
\]

### 4.5 Comparison of Subshells

| Subshell | Number of orbitals | Maximum electrons |
|---|---:|---:|
| s | 1 | 2 |
| p | 3 | 6 |
| d | 5 | 10 |
| f | 7 | 14 |

## 5. Number of Orbitals and Electrons

### 5.1 Number of Orbitals in a Subshell

\[
\boxed{\text{Number of orbitals}=2l+1}
\]

### 5.2 Maximum Number of Electrons in a Subshell

Each orbital accommodates two electrons.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{Maximum electrons}=2(2l+1)}
\]

### 5.3 Maximum Number of Electrons in a Shell

The maximum number of electrons in a shell is:

\[
\boxed{2n^2}
\]

### 5.4 Numerical Example

Calculate the maximum number of electrons in the fourth shell.

Given:

\[
n=4
\]

Using:

\[
N=2n^2
\]

\[
N=2(4)^2
\]

\[
\boxed{N=32}
\]

## 6. Rules Governing Electronic Configuration

Electrons occupy atomic orbitals according to specific quantum mechanical principles.

The three important rules are:

1. Aufbau principle.
2. Pauli exclusion principle.
3. Hund's rule.

### 6.1 Aufbau Principle

The Aufbau principle states that electrons occupy orbitals in order of increasing energy.

The word Aufbau means building up.

The approximate orbital filling order is:

\[
\boxed{
1s<2s<2p<3s<3p<4s<3d<4p<5s<4d<5p<6s<4f<5d<6p<7s
}
\]

This sequence can be remembered using the diagonal rule.

### 6.2 Pauli Exclusion Principle

The Pauli exclusion principle states that no two electrons in an atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers.

Consequently:
- Each orbital can contain a maximum of two electrons.
- The two electrons in one orbital must have opposite spins.

Orbital representation:

Correct:

\[
\uparrow\downarrow
\]

Incorrect:

\[
\uparrow\uparrow
\]

### 6.3 Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity

Hund's rule states that electrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing occurs.

Example: Nitrogen has three electrons in its 2p subshell.

Correct arrangement:

\[
2p:\quad \uparrow\quad\uparrow\quad\uparrow
\]

Incorrect arrangement:

\[
2p:\quad \uparrow\downarrow\quad\uparrow\quad -
\]

The singly occupied arrangement generally has lower energy because it reduces electron-pairing repulsion and provides exchange stabilization.

## 7. Electronic Configuration

### 7.1 Definition

Electronic configuration describes the distribution of electrons among the shells, subshells, and orbitals of an atom.

It is written using:
- Principal quantum number.
- Subshell designation.
- Superscript indicating electron count.

Example:

\[
1s^22s^22p^2
\]

This represents:
- Two electrons in 1s.
- Two electrons in 2s.
- Two electrons in 2p.

Total electrons:

\[
2+2+2=6
\]

Therefore, this is the electronic configuration of carbon.

### 7.2 Electronic Configurations of the First Ten Elements

| Element | Atomic number | Electronic configuration |
|---|---:|---|
| Hydrogen | 1 | 1s¹ |
| Helium | 2 | 1s² |
| Lithium | 3 | 1s² 2s¹ |
| Beryllium | 4 | 1s² 2s² |
| Boron | 5 | 1s² 2s² 2p¹ |
| Carbon | 6 | 1s² 2s² 2p² |
| Nitrogen | 7 | 1s² 2s² 2p³ |
| Oxygen | 8 | 1s² 2s² 2p⁴ |
| Fluorine | 9 | 1s² 2s² 2p⁵ |
| Neon | 10 | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ |

### 7.3 Noble Gas Configuration

Noble gas configuration is a shorthand method of representing electronic configurations using the symbol of the preceding noble gas.

Example:

Sodium:

Full configuration:

\[
1s^22s^22p^63s^1
\]

Shorthand configuration:

\[
\boxed{[Ne]3s^1}
\]

This notation reduces repetition.

### 7.4 Electronic Configuration of Ions

When ions form, electrons are gained or lost.

For main-group elements:
- Cations are formed by losing electrons.
- Anions are formed by gaining electrons.

Example: Sodium ion.

Sodium:

\[
Na:1s^22s^22p^63s^1
\]

Sodium ion:

\[
Na^+:1s^22s^22p^6
\]

Example: Chloride ion.

Chlorine:

\[
Cl:1s^22s^22p^63s^23p^5
\]

Chloride ion:

\[
Cl^-:1s^22s^22p^63s^23p^6
\]

### 7.5 Transition Metal Ions

When transition metal cations form, electrons are generally removed from the outermost shell first.

For example:

Iron:

\[
Fe:[Ar]3d^64s^2
\]

Iron(II):

\[
Fe^{2+}:[Ar]3d^6
\]

Iron(III):

\[
Fe^{3+}:[Ar]3d^5
\]

Although the 4s orbital fills before 3d in the usual Aufbau sequence, 4s electrons are removed first during the formation of these cations.

## 8. Exceptions to the Aufbau Principle

Some elements have electronic configurations that differ from the simplest predicted filling order because of the relative stability of particular electron arrangements.

### 8.1 Chromium

Expected configuration:

\[
[Ar]3d^44s^2
\]

Observed configuration:

\[
\boxed{[Ar]3d^54s^1}
\]

The half-filled d subshell contributes to the stability of the observed arrangement.

### 8.2 Copper

Expected configuration:

\[
[Ar]3d^94s^2
\]

Observed configuration:

\[
\boxed{[Ar]3d^{10}4s^1}
\]

The completely filled d subshell contributes to the stability of the observed arrangement.

### 8.3 Important Note

The stability of half-filled and fully filled subshells is a useful introductory explanation, but actual electron configurations are determined by the total energies of the atom's possible electronic states.

## 9. Valence Electrons

### 9.1 Definition

Valence electrons are electrons involved in chemical bonding and chemical reactions.

For many main-group elements, they are the electrons in the outermost occupied shell.

Examples:

- Sodium: 1 valence electron.
- Magnesium: 2 valence electrons.
- Carbon: 4 valence electrons.
- Oxygen: 6 valence electrons.
- Chlorine: 7 valence electrons.

### 9.2 Importance

Valence electrons help determine:
- Chemical reactivity.
- Common oxidation states.
- Bond formation.
- Ionic charge.
- Position within the periodic table.

## 10. Practical Applications

### 10.1 Understanding Chemical Bonding

Electronic configurations explain how atoms gain, lose, or share electrons during bond formation.

### 10.2 Periodic Classification

The outer electronic configuration helps determine the group and block of an element.

### 10.3 Magnetic Properties

The presence of unpaired electrons influences the magnetic behaviour of atoms and ions.

### 10.4 Spectroscopy

Electronic transitions between allowed states produce characteristic absorption and emission spectra.

## 11. Common Exam Traps

1. The principal quantum number starts from 1.
2. The azimuthal quantum number ranges from 0 to n - 1.
3. The magnetic quantum number ranges from -l to +l.
4. The spin quantum number has only two permitted values: +1/2 and -1/2.
5. An s subshell contains one orbital.
6. A p subshell contains three orbitals.
7. A d subshell contains five orbitals.
8. An f subshell contains seven orbitals.
9. Each orbital can hold a maximum of two electrons.
10. The maximum number of electrons in a shell is 2n².
11. Hund's rule requires single occupation of degenerate orbitals before pairing.
12. The Pauli exclusion principle prohibits identical sets of four quantum numbers.
13. The 4s orbital generally fills before 3d in the introductory Aufbau sequence.
14. For common transition metal cations, 4s electrons are removed before 3d electrons.
15. Chromium and copper are important electronic configuration exceptions.
