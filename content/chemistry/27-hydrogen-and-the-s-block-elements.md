## Hydrogen

Hydrogen has three isotopes: protium ($^1$H), deuterium ($^2$H, D) and radioactive tritium ($^3$H). It forms three types of hydride: **ionic** (with s-block metals, e.g. $\mathrm{NaH}$, containing $\mathrm{H^-}$), **covalent** (with p-block, e.g. $\mathrm{NH_3}$) and **metallic** (interstitial, with many d-block metals). Hydrogen peroxide, $\mathrm{H_2O_2}$, acts as both an oxidising and a reducing agent.

## Alkali metals (Group 1)

All have one electron in the outer $s$ orbital, giving low ionisation energies and soft, reactive metals. Reactivity with water increases down the group. They give characteristic flame colours: Li crimson, Na golden yellow, K lilac.

**Lithium is anomalous** owing to its small size and high charge density. It is the hardest of the group, forms the oxide (not the peroxide or superoxide) in air, and its salts are more covalent and more hydrated. It has a diagonal relationship with magnesium: both form nitrides, and their carbonates decompose on heating.

Although Li has the highest ionisation enthalpy in the group, it has the **most negative $E^\circ$** ($-3.04$ V), because the small $\mathrm{Li^+}$ ion has an extremely large hydration enthalpy.

## Alkaline earth metals (Group 2)

Two outer electrons; harder and denser than alkali metals. Down the group:

- Solubility of **sulfates decreases**: $\mathrm{BeSO_4} > \mathrm{MgSO_4} > \mathrm{CaSO_4} > \mathrm{SrSO_4} > \mathrm{BaSO_4}$.
- Solubility of **hydroxides increases**: $\mathrm{Be(OH)_2} < \mathrm{Mg(OH)_2} < \mathrm{Ca(OH)_2} < \mathrm{Sr(OH)_2} < \mathrm{Ba(OH)_2}$.
- **Thermal stability** of carbonates and nitrates increases, because larger cations polarise the anion less.

The trends reflect two competing quantities, lattice enthalpy and hydration enthalpy. For sulfates the large anion makes lattice energy nearly constant while hydration energy falls, so solubility falls.

## Important compounds

$\mathrm{Na_2CO_3}$ is made by the Solvay process; $\mathrm{NaOH}$ by the Castner-Kellner (chlor-alkali) process. Plaster of Paris is $\mathrm{CaSO_4\cdot\tfrac12H_2O}$, which sets to gypsum $\mathrm{CaSO_4\cdot2H_2O}$ when mixed with water.

## Quiz

1. Which of the following correctly describes solubility trends for Group 2 compounds down the group?
   - [x] Sulfates become less soluble and hydroxides more soluble.
   - [ ] Sulfates and hydroxides both become less soluble.
   - [ ] Sulfates become more soluble and hydroxides less soluble.
   - [ ] Sulfates and hydroxides both become more soluble.
   > The sulfate trend is controlled by hydration enthalpy, which falls faster than lattice enthalpy; the hydroxide trend is controlled by lattice enthalpy falling faster as the cation grows.

2. Lithium has the most negative standard electrode potential among the alkali metals mainly because of:
   - [ ] its lowest ionisation enthalpy
   - [ ] its highest atomic radius
   - [ ] its high electronegativity
   - [x] the very high hydration enthalpy of the small $\mathrm{Li^+}$ ion
   > $E^\circ$ for $\mathrm{M^+/M}$ combines sublimation, ionisation and hydration terms. Li's ionisation enthalpy is actually the highest in the group, but the hydration of $\mathrm{Li^+}$ releases so much energy that it dominates.

3. On strong heating, which of these nitrates gives the metal oxide, $\mathrm{NO_2}$ and $\mathrm{O_2}$?
   - [ ] $\mathrm{NaNO_3}$
   - [ ] $\mathrm{KNO_3}$
   - [x] $\mathrm{LiNO_3}$
   - [ ] $\mathrm{RbNO_3}$
   > Lithium behaves like Group 2 (diagonal relationship with Mg): $\mathrm{4LiNO_3 \rightarrow 2Li_2O + 4NO_2 + O_2}$. The other alkali nitrates lose only oxygen, forming the nitrite, e.g. $\mathrm{2NaNO_3 \rightarrow 2NaNO_2 + O_2}$.
