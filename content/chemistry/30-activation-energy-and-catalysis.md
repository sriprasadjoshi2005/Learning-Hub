# Lesson 30: Activation Energy and Catalysis

Reaction mechanisms explain how chemical reactions occur through individual elementary steps. Activation energy determines the minimum energy barrier that reacting particles must overcome, while collision theory and transition-state theory help explain reaction rates. Catalysts alter reaction pathways and are essential in biological systems and industrial chemical production.

## 1. Introduction to Reaction Mechanisms

A reaction mechanism is the sequence of elementary steps through which an overall chemical reaction occurs.

An overall reaction may involve:
- One elementary step.
- Several consecutive steps.
- Intermediate chemical species.
- Temporary activated arrangements of atoms.

The sum of the elementary steps must reproduce the balanced overall reaction.

## 2. Elementary Reactions

An elementary reaction occurs in a single molecular event.

Examples:

\[
A\rightarrow P
\]

\[
A+B\rightarrow P
\]

The molecularity of an elementary reaction is the number of reacting particles involved in that step.

### 2.1 Unimolecular Reactions

A unimolecular elementary reaction involves one reacting particle.

\[
A\rightarrow P
\]

### 2.2 Bimolecular Reactions

A bimolecular elementary reaction involves two reacting particles.

\[
A+B\rightarrow P
\]

or:

\[
2A\rightarrow P
\]

### 2.3 Termolecular Reactions

A termolecular elementary reaction involves three reacting particles in one elementary event.

Such events are relatively uncommon because simultaneous effective collisions among three particles are less probable.

## 3. Molecularity and Reaction Order

Molecularity:
- Applies only to an elementary step.
- Is determined from the number of reacting particles.
- Is always a positive integer.

Reaction order:
- Is obtained from the experimental rate law.
- Applies to the observed reaction rate.
- Can be zero, fractional, or an integer.

For an elementary reaction, the rate law can be written using its molecular stoichiometry.

For a complex overall reaction, the rate law cannot generally be inferred directly from the overall balanced equation.

## 4. Activation Energy

Activation energy is the minimum energy barrier that reacting particles must overcome for a reaction to proceed through a particular pathway.

It is represented by:

\[
E_a
\]

Even when a reaction is thermodynamically favourable, it may proceed slowly if its activation-energy barrier is large.

### 4.1 Energy Profile Diagram

An energy profile diagram represents the potential energy change along a reaction pathway.

Important features:
- Reactants.
- Products.
- Activation-energy barrier.
- Transition state.
- Enthalpy change.

For an exothermic reaction:

\[
\Delta H<0
\]

For an endothermic reaction:

\[
\Delta H>0
\]

Activation energy is distinct from enthalpy change.

### 4.2 Forward and Reverse Activation Energies

For a simple reaction pathway:

\[
\boxed{E_{a,\text{forward}}-E_{a,\text{reverse}}=\Delta H}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{E_{a,\text{forward}}=E_{a,\text{reverse}}+\Delta H}
\]

This relationship assumes the same energy reference and a single pathway with a well-defined transition state.

## 5. Collision Theory

Collision theory explains reaction rates in terms of molecular collisions.

For a collision to produce a reaction, particles generally need:
- Sufficient energy.
- Suitable relative orientation.

Not every collision is effective.

### 5.1 Effective Collisions

An effective collision has sufficient energy to overcome the activation barrier and an appropriate orientation for bond rearrangement.

The reaction rate depends on the number of effective collisions per unit time.

## 6. Arrhenius Equation

The Arrhenius equation describes how the rate constant depends on temperature.

\[
\boxed{k=Ae^{-E_a/RT}}
\]

Where:
- k = rate constant.
- A = pre-exponential factor.
- \(E_a\) = activation energy.
- R = gas constant.
- T = absolute temperature.

Taking natural logarithms:

\[
\boxed{\ln k=\ln A-\frac{E_a}{RT}}
\]

Using common logarithms:

\[
\boxed{\log k=\log A-\frac{E_a}{2.303RT}}
\]

### 6.1 Two-Temperature Form

For two temperatures:

\[
\boxed{
\ln\frac{k_2}{k_1}
=
\frac{E_a}{R}
\left(\frac{1}{T_1}-\frac{1}{T_2}\right)
}
\]

Temperatures must be expressed in kelvin.

### 6.2 Arrhenius Plot

A graph of \(\ln k\) against \(1/T\) gives a straight line for a system following the Arrhenius equation over the measured range.

Its slope is:

\[
\boxed{\text{Slope}=-\frac{E_a}{R}}
\]

Its intercept is:

\[
\ln A
\]

## 7. Effect of Temperature on Reaction Rate

Increasing temperature generally increases the reaction rate.

The reason is that:
- Molecular kinetic energy increases.
- More particles have energy greater than or equal to the activation energy.
- The rate constant generally increases.

The temperature dependence of the rate constant is described quantitatively by the Arrhenius equation.

## 8. Catalysis

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a reaction by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation-energy barrier.

A catalyst:
- Participates in the reaction mechanism.
- Is regenerated by the end of the catalytic cycle.
- Does not change the overall reaction equation.
- Does not change the equilibrium constant.
- Does not change the equilibrium composition.
- Helps equilibrium be reached faster.

## 9. Types of Catalysis

### 9.1 Homogeneous Catalysis

The catalyst and reactants are in the same physical phase.

Example: acid-catalysed ester hydrolysis in aqueous solution.

### 9.2 Heterogeneous Catalysis

The catalyst and reactants are in different physical phases.

Example: hydrogenation of vegetable oils using a solid metal catalyst.

Heterogeneous catalysis commonly involves:
1. Adsorption of reactants onto the catalyst surface.
2. Activation of adsorbed molecules.
3. Surface reaction.
4. Desorption of products.

### 9.3 Enzyme Catalysis

Enzymes are biological catalysts that accelerate biochemical reactions.

They often show:
- High substrate specificity.
- High catalytic efficiency.
- Activity dependent on temperature and pH.
- Regulation by inhibitors and activators.

Example: amylase catalyses the breakdown of starch.

### 9.4 Autocatalysis

In autocatalysis, one of the products of a reaction acts as a catalyst for the same reaction.

## 10. Catalysts and Equilibrium

A catalyst lowers the activation-energy barrier for both the forward and reverse reactions through an alternative pathway.

It increases both reaction rates and allows the system to reach equilibrium faster.

It does not change:

\[
K
\]

or:

\[
\Delta G^\circ
\]

Therefore, a catalyst does not increase the equilibrium yield of a reaction at a fixed temperature.

## 11. Catalytic Inhibitors and Poisons

A catalyst inhibitor decreases catalytic activity.

A catalyst poison strongly reduces or destroys catalytic activity, often by occupying active sites or chemically altering the catalyst.

Examples:
- Certain sulphur compounds can poison metal catalysts.
- Competitive inhibitors can reduce enzyme activity.
- Some substances temporarily block catalyst active sites.

## 12. Industrial Applications of Catalysis

### 12.1 Haber Process

Iron-based catalysts are used in ammonia production.

\[
N_2+3H_2\rightleftharpoons2NH_3
\]

### 12.2 Contact Process

Vanadium(V) oxide is used as a catalyst in the oxidation of sulphur dioxide.

\[
2SO_2+O_2\rightleftharpoons2SO_3
\]

### 12.3 Catalytic Converters

Automobile catalytic converters help convert harmful exhaust gases into less harmful substances.

They can promote:
- Oxidation of carbon monoxide.
- Oxidation of unburnt hydrocarbons.
- Reduction of nitrogen oxides.

### 12.4 Biological Catalysis

Enzymes accelerate reactions involved in:
- Digestion.
- Cellular respiration.
- DNA replication.
- Metabolism.
- Biosynthesis.

## 13. Common Exam Traps

- Activation energy is not the same as reaction enthalpy.
- Molecularity applies to elementary reactions, not complex overall reactions.
- Reaction order is determined experimentally.
- Increasing temperature generally increases the rate constant.
- A catalyst lowers the activation barrier through an alternative pathway.
- A catalyst does not change the equilibrium constant.
- A catalyst is regenerated in the overall catalytic process.
- The Arrhenius equation requires absolute temperature in kelvin.
