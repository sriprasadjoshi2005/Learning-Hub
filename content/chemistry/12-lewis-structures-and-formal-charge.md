# Lesson 12: Lewis Structures and Formal Charge

Lewis structures are diagrams used to represent the arrangement of valence electrons in atoms, molecules, and polyatomic ions. They show how atoms are connected through covalent bonds and how their remaining valence electrons are distributed as lone pairs. Lewis structures help us understand molecular bonding, electron-pair arrangements, chemical stability, and the formation of ions.

Formal charge is a method of assigning electrons to individual atoms within a Lewis structure. It helps us compare alternative structures and identify the most reasonable electron distribution. These concepts form the foundation for understanding resonance, molecular geometry, bond polarity, and advanced chemical bonding theories.

## 1. Introduction to Lewis Structures

### 1.1 What Is a Lewis Structure?

A Lewis structure is a representation of a molecule or polyatomic ion that shows:

- Chemical symbols representing atoms.
- Lines representing covalent bonds.
- Dots representing non-bonding valence electrons.
- Lone pairs of electrons.
- Formal charges, when present.

A single covalent bond represents one shared pair of electrons and is drawn as one line between two atoms.

For example, the Lewis structure of hydrogen molecule is:

```text
H—H
```

Each hydrogen atom contributes one electron to the shared pair. Both hydrogen atoms achieve a stable duet configuration.

### 1.2 Valence Electrons

Valence electrons are the electrons present in the outermost occupied shell of an atom. They are primarily responsible for chemical bonding.

For representative elements, the group number can help determine the number of valence electrons.

| Element | Group | Valence electrons |
|---|---:|---:|
| Hydrogen | 1 | 1 |
| Carbon | 14 | 4 |
| Nitrogen | 15 | 5 |
| Oxygen | 16 | 6 |
| Fluorine | 17 | 7 |
| Neon | 18 | 8 |

For example, oxygen has the electronic configuration:

\[
1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^4
\]

Its outermost shell contains six electrons, so oxygen has six valence electrons.

### 1.3 Bonding Pairs and Lone Pairs

Valence electrons in a Lewis structure are classified into two categories.

**Bonding pair:** A pair of electrons shared between two atoms.

**Lone pair:** A pair of valence electrons that belongs to one atom and is not shared in a covalent bond.

For example, the Lewis structure of water contains two bonding pairs and two lone pairs on oxygen.

```text
  ..
H—O—H
  ..
```

Oxygen shares one electron pair with each hydrogen and retains two lone pairs.

## 2. The Octet Rule and Its Application

### 2.1 Statement of the Octet Rule

The octet rule states that atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons to achieve eight electrons in their valence shell, resembling the electronic configuration of a noble gas.

For example:

- Carbon generally forms four covalent bonds.
- Nitrogen generally forms three covalent bonds and retains one lone pair.
- Oxygen generally forms two covalent bonds and retains two lone pairs.
- Halogens generally form one covalent bond and retain three lone pairs.

### 2.2 Duet Rule

Hydrogen and helium follow the duet rule because their first electron shell can accommodate a maximum of two electrons.

Hydrogen achieves a stable configuration by sharing one electron with another atom.

For example, in hydrogen chloride:

\[
H-Cl
\]

Hydrogen shares one electron pair and achieves two electrons in its first shell. Chlorine completes its octet.

### 2.3 Importance and Limitations

The octet rule is useful for predicting many common covalent structures, but it is not universal.

Important exceptions include:

1. Incomplete octets, such as in BeCl₂ and BF₃.
2. Odd-electron molecules, such as NO and NO₂.
3. Expanded valence-shell structures, such as PCl₅ and SF₆.

Therefore, the octet rule is a useful guideline rather than an absolute law.

## 3. Steps for Drawing Lewis Structures

A systematic procedure helps avoid mistakes when constructing Lewis structures.

### 3.1 Step 1: Count the Total Number of Valence Electrons

Add the valence electrons contributed by every atom.

For a negative ion, add electrons equal to the magnitude of its charge.

For a positive ion, subtract electrons equal to its charge.

**Example: Carbon dioxide (CO₂)**

Carbon contributes four electrons.

Each oxygen contributes six electrons.

\[
4+2(6)=16
\]

Therefore, CO₂ contains 16 valence electrons.

### 3.2 Step 2: Select the Central Atom

The central atom is generally the least electronegative atom, excluding hydrogen.

Hydrogen is never selected as the central atom because it can form only one covalent bond.

For CO₂, carbon is the central atom.

The initial arrangement is:

```text
O—C—O
```

### 3.3 Step 3: Connect the Atoms Using Single Bonds

Draw one single bond between the central atom and each surrounding atom.

Each single bond represents two electrons.

For CO₂:

```text
O—C—O
```

Two single bonds use:

\[
2\times2=4\text{ electrons}
\]

Remaining electrons:

\[
16-4=12
\]

### 3.4 Step 4: Complete the Octets of the Outer Atoms

Distribute the remaining electrons as lone pairs around the surrounding atoms until their octets are complete.

Oxygen requires six additional electrons when it has one single bond.

The initial electron distribution becomes:

```text
 ..       ..
:O—C—O:
 ..       ..
```

Each oxygen has three lone pairs and one bonding pair.

Carbon, however, has only four electrons around it and does not satisfy the octet rule.

### 3.5 Step 5: Form Multiple Bonds if Necessary

If the central atom does not have an octet, convert suitable lone pairs from surrounding atoms into additional bonding pairs.

For CO₂, one lone pair from each oxygen is converted into a shared bonding pair.

The final Lewis structure is:

```text
..     ..
O=C=O
..     ..
```

Carbon now has eight electrons around it, and each oxygen also satisfies the octet rule.

### 3.6 Step 6: Check the Total Electron Count

Always verify that the total number of electrons represented in the structure equals the number initially calculated.

For CO₂:

- Two double bonds contain eight bonding electrons.
- Four lone pairs contain eight non-bonding electrons.

\[
8+8=16
\]

The electron count is correct.

### 3.7 Step 7: Calculate Formal Charges

Calculate the formal charge on each atom to evaluate the electron distribution.

A reasonable Lewis structure generally has:

- Small formal charges.
- Minimal separation of positive and negative charges.
- Negative formal charges preferentially located on more electronegative atoms.
- Appropriate valence-shell configurations whenever possible.

## 4. Drawing Lewis Structures of Common Molecules

### 4.1 Hydrogen Molecule (H₂)

Each hydrogen atom contributes one valence electron.

\[
1+1=2
\]

The two atoms share one electron pair.

```text
H—H
```

Each hydrogen achieves a stable duet configuration.

### 4.2 Water Molecule (H₂O)

Total valence electrons:

\[
2(1)+6=8
\]

Oxygen is the central atom.

Two single bonds use four electrons. The remaining four electrons form two lone pairs on oxygen.

```text
  ..
H—O—H
  ..
```

Oxygen satisfies the octet rule, while each hydrogen satisfies the duet rule.

### 4.3 Ammonia Molecule (NH₃)

Total valence electrons:

\[
5+3(1)=8
\]

Nitrogen is the central atom.

Three single bonds use six electrons. The remaining two electrons form one lone pair on nitrogen.

```text
   ..
H—N—H
   |
   H
```

Nitrogen has three bonding pairs and one lone pair.

### 4.4 Methane Molecule (CH₄)

Total valence electrons:

\[
4+4(1)=8
\]

Carbon is the central atom and forms four single bonds with hydrogen atoms.

```text
    H
    |
H—C—H
    |
    H
```

Carbon completes its octet, and all four hydrogen atoms complete their duets.

### 4.5 Oxygen Molecule (O₂)

Each oxygen atom contributes six valence electrons.

\[
6+6=12
\]

The two oxygen atoms share two electron pairs, forming a double bond.

```text
..   ..
O = O
..   ..
```

Each oxygen has two lone pairs and participates in a double bond.

### 4.6 Nitrogen Molecule (N₂)

Each nitrogen atom contributes five valence electrons.

\[
5+5=10
\]

The nitrogen atoms share three electron pairs, forming a triple bond.

```text
:N≡N:
```

Each nitrogen retains one lone pair and completes its octet.

### 4.7 Ammonium Ion (NH₄⁺)

Total valence electrons:

\[
5+4(1)-1=8
\]

Nitrogen is surrounded by four hydrogen atoms through four single bonds.

```text
      H
      |
[ H—N—H ]⁺
      |
      H
```

Nitrogen has four bonding pairs and no lone pairs.

The overall charge is +1, and the formal charge on nitrogen is +1.

### 4.8 Nitrate Ion (NO₃⁻)

Total valence electrons:

\[
5+3(6)+1=24
\]

Nitrogen is the central atom.

A valid Lewis structure contains one N=O double bond and two N—O single bonds.

One representative structure is:

```text
       O
       ||
[ O⁻—N⁺—O⁻ ]⁻
```

The two singly bonded oxygen atoms each carry a formal charge of −1, while nitrogen carries +1. The double-bonded oxygen has a formal charge of zero.

The total formal charge is:

\[
(+1)+(-1)+(-1)+0=-1
\]

The double bond can be placed between nitrogen and any one of the three oxygen atoms. This produces three equivalent resonance contributors, which are discussed in a later lesson.

## 5. Formal Charge

### 5.1 Meaning of Formal Charge

Formal charge is the hypothetical charge assigned to an atom in a molecule or ion when bonding electrons are divided equally between the bonded atoms.

It helps determine whether a proposed Lewis structure represents a reasonable distribution of electrons.

Formal charge is not necessarily the actual electrical charge on an atom. It is a bookkeeping quantity used in Lewis structures.

### 5.2 Formal Charge Formula

The formal charge on an atom is calculated using:

\[
\boxed{FC=V-N-\frac{B}{2}}
\]

Where:

- \(FC\) = Formal charge.
- \(V\) = Number of valence electrons in the isolated neutral atom.
- \(N\) = Number of non-bonding electrons assigned to the atom.
- \(B\) = Number of bonding electrons surrounding the atom.

Alternatively:

\[
\boxed{FC=V-L-\text{Number of bonds}}
\]

Here, \(L\) represents the number of lone-pair electrons.

Both formulas give the same result when applied correctly.

### 5.3 Formal Charge of Carbon in Methane (CH₄)

Carbon has four valence electrons.

In methane:

- Carbon has no lone-pair electrons.
- Carbon forms four single bonds.
- Carbon is surrounded by eight bonding electrons.

Using the formula:

\[
FC=4-0-\frac{8}{2}
\]

\[
FC=4-4
\]

\[
\boxed{FC=0}
\]

Therefore, carbon has zero formal charge in methane.

### 5.4 Formal Charge of Oxygen in Water (H₂O)

Oxygen has six valence electrons.

In water:

- Oxygen has four non-bonding electrons.
- Oxygen participates in two single bonds.
- There are four bonding electrons around oxygen.

\[
FC=6-4-\frac{4}{2}
\]

\[
FC=6-4-2
\]

\[
\boxed{FC=0}
\]

Therefore, oxygen has zero formal charge in water.

### 5.5 Formal Charge of Nitrogen in Ammonium (NH₄⁺)

Nitrogen has five valence electrons.

In ammonium:

- Nitrogen has no lone-pair electrons.
- Nitrogen forms four single bonds.
- There are eight bonding electrons around nitrogen.

\[
FC=5-0-\frac{8}{2}
\]

\[
FC=5-4
\]

\[
\boxed{FC=+1}
\]

Each hydrogen has zero formal charge.

Therefore, the overall charge of NH₄⁺ is +1.

### 5.6 Formal Charge in Nitrate (NO₃⁻)

Consider one resonance contributor of nitrate:

- Nitrogen forms one double bond and two single bonds.
- Nitrogen has no lone pairs.
- The double-bonded oxygen has two lone pairs.
- Each singly bonded oxygen has three lone pairs.

For nitrogen:

\[
FC=5-0-\frac{8}{2}
\]

\[
\boxed{FC=+1}
\]

For the double-bonded oxygen:

\[
FC=6-4-\frac{4}{2}
\]

\[
\boxed{FC=0}
\]

For each singly bonded oxygen:

\[
FC=6-6-\frac{2}{2}
\]

\[
\boxed{FC=-1}
\]

The total formal charge is:

\[
(+1)+0+(-1)+(-1)=-1
\]

This agrees with the overall charge of the nitrate ion.

## 6. Exceptions to the Octet Rule

Although many molecules follow the octet rule, some stable compounds do not.

### 6.1 Incomplete Octet

Some atoms are stable in structures where the central atom has fewer than eight electrons.

**Beryllium chloride (BeCl₂)**

Beryllium has two valence electrons and forms two single bonds.

```text
Cl—Be—Cl
```

The central beryllium atom has only four electrons around it.

**Boron trifluoride (BF₃)**

Boron has three valence electrons and forms three single bonds.

```text
    F
    |
F—B—F
```

Boron has only six electrons around it.

These are examples of incomplete-octet structures.

### 6.2 Odd-Electron Molecules

Some molecules contain an odd total number of valence electrons. Consequently, not all electrons can be arranged into pairs.

Examples include:

- Nitric oxide (NO).
- Nitrogen dioxide (NO₂).

For nitric oxide:

\[
5+6=11
\]

Since there are eleven valence electrons, one electron remains unpaired.

Such molecules are often called free radicals.

### 6.3 Expanded Octet

Some central atoms from period 3 and beyond are conventionally represented with more than eight electrons in Lewis structures.

Examples include:

**Phosphorus pentachloride (PCl₅)**

```text
       Cl
       |
Cl—P—Cl
   / \
 Cl   Cl
```

Phosphorus is surrounded by five bonding pairs, giving ten electrons in the Lewis representation.

**Sulfur hexafluoride (SF₆)**

```text
        F
        |
    F—S—F
     / | \
    F  F  F
```

Sulfur is surrounded by six bonding pairs, giving twelve electrons in the conventional Lewis representation.

Expanded-octet Lewis structures are useful electron-counting representations. Their bonding should not be interpreted as requiring the participation of d orbitals; modern bonding descriptions use more advanced molecular orbital approaches.

## 7. Evaluating and Comparing Lewis Structures

### 7.1 Formal Charge and Structural Stability

When several Lewis structures are possible, formal charge helps identify the more reasonable representation.

Generally, a preferred Lewis structure has:

1. The smallest possible formal charges.
2. The least separation of positive and negative charges.
3. Negative formal charges on more electronegative atoms.
4. Complete octets for atoms whenever chemically reasonable.

However, these are guidelines. Structures with formal charges may still be necessary to represent certain molecules and ions correctly.

### 7.2 Formal Charge and Overall Charge

The sum of the formal charges on all atoms must equal the overall charge of the molecule or ion.

For a neutral molecule:

\[
\sum FC=0
\]

For a positively charged ion:

\[
\sum FC=+\text{ionic charge}
\]

For a negatively charged ion:

\[
\sum FC=-\text{magnitude of ionic charge}
\]

This is an important verification step when drawing Lewis structures.

### 7.3 Formal Charge Versus Oxidation State

Formal charge and oxidation state are different concepts.

| Feature | Formal charge | Oxidation state |
|---|---|---|
| Electron assignment | Bonding electrons divided equally | Bonding electrons assigned to the more electronegative atom |
| Main purpose | Evaluating Lewis structures | Tracking electron transfer and oxidation-reduction |
| Bond treatment | Covalent bonds divided equally | Bonds treated as ionic for assignment |
| Dependence | Lewis structure | Electronegativity and bonding arrangement |

The two values can be different for the same atom.

For example, carbon in methane has a formal charge of zero, but its oxidation state is −4.

## 8. Common Errors When Drawing Lewis Structures

### 8.1 Incorrect Electron Counting

A frequent mistake is forgetting to add electrons for negative charges or subtract electrons for positive charges.

Always calculate the total valence electrons before drawing the structure.

### 8.2 Choosing the Wrong Central Atom

Hydrogen cannot be the central atom because it forms only one bond.

The central atom is generally the least electronegative atom, excluding hydrogen.

### 8.3 Forgetting Lone Pairs

A structure may have the correct number of bonds but still be incorrect because lone pairs are missing.

Always distribute the remaining electrons and verify the octets.

### 8.4 Giving Hydrogen More Than Two Electrons

Hydrogen follows the duet rule, not the octet rule.

It can form only one ordinary covalent bond.

### 8.5 Confusing Formal Charge with Actual Charge

Formal charge is a bookkeeping value. It does not necessarily represent the actual distribution of electron density in a molecule.

### 8.6 Ignoring the Overall Charge

The sum of all formal charges must equal the charge of the entire species.

A structure with an incorrect total formal charge is not valid.

## 9. Applications of Lewis Structures and Formal Charge

Lewis structures and formal charges are useful in several areas of chemistry.

### 9.1 Predicting Bonding

They help identify:

- The number of bonds formed by an atom.
- The number of lone pairs.
- Whether single, double, or triple bonds are present.

### 9.2 Understanding Molecular Geometry

The number of bonding pairs and lone pairs around a central atom helps predict molecular geometry using VSEPR theory.

For example, the Lewis structure of NH₃ shows three bonding pairs and one lone pair around nitrogen. This information is used to predict its trigonal pyramidal molecular shape.

### 9.3 Understanding Chemical Reactivity

Lone pairs can participate in coordinate bond formation and chemical reactions.

Formal charges can help identify electron-rich and electron-deficient sites in a Lewis structure.

### 9.4 Understanding Resonance

Some molecules cannot be represented accurately using only one Lewis structure.

Multiple valid Lewis structures can be drawn by changing the arrangement of electrons without changing the positions of the atoms.

These structures are called resonance contributors.

The actual molecule is described by a resonance hybrid rather than rapidly switching between separate structures.

### 9.5 Understanding Chemical Stability

Formal charge calculations help compare alternative electron arrangements and identify structures that are more reasonable according to Lewis theory.
