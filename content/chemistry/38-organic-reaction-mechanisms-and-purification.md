# Lesson 38: Organic Reaction Mechanisms and Purification

Organic reactions involve the breaking and formation of covalent bonds. Understanding their mechanisms helps explain how reactants are converted into products and why different conditions produce different outcomes.

Organic compounds must also be separated and purified before their properties and compositions can be studied accurately.

This lesson covers major reaction types, substitution and addition mechanisms, elimination, oxidation and reduction, and important purification and analytical methods.

## 1. Organic Reaction Mechanisms

A reaction mechanism is the step-by-step description of how a chemical reaction occurs.

It shows:

- Which bonds break.
- Which bonds form.
- How electrons move.
- Which intermediates appear.
- Which step controls the reaction rate.

Curved arrows are used to represent electron-pair movement.

A full-headed curved arrow represents movement of an electron pair. A single-headed or fishhook arrow represents movement of one electron.

## 2. Major Types of Organic Reactions

### 2.1 Substitution Reactions

A substitution reaction occurs when one atom or group is replaced by another.

Example: chlorination of methane.

\[
CH_4+Cl_2\xrightarrow{h\nu}CH_3Cl+HCl
\]

This reaction proceeds through a free-radical chain mechanism.

### 2.2 Addition Reactions

An addition reaction occurs when atoms or groups add across a multiple bond.

Example: hydrogenation of ethene.

\[
CH_2=CH_2+H_2\xrightarrow{Ni}CH_3CH_3
\]

The carbon-carbon double bond becomes a single bond.

### 2.3 Elimination Reactions

An elimination reaction removes atoms or groups from a molecule, often forming a multiple bond.

Example: dehydration of ethanol.

\[
CH_3CH_2OH\xrightarrow[\Delta]{conc.\ H_2SO_4}CH_2=CH_2+H_2O
\]

### 2.4 Rearrangement Reactions

A rearrangement reaction involves reorganisation of atoms or groups within a molecule.

These reactions can produce more stable intermediates or products.

### 2.5 Oxidation Reactions

Organic oxidation may involve:

- Addition of oxygen.
- Removal of hydrogen.
- Increase in oxidation state of carbon.

Example: oxidation of ethanol to ethanal.

\[
CH_3CH_2OH+[O]\rightarrow CH_3CHO+H_2O
\]

Further oxidation can produce ethanoic acid.

### 2.6 Reduction Reactions

Organic reduction may involve:

- Addition of hydrogen.
- Removal of oxygen.
- Decrease in oxidation state of carbon.

Example: hydrogenation of an alkene.

\[
CH_2=CH_2+H_2\rightarrow CH_3CH_3
\]

## 3. Free-Radical Substitution

The chlorination of methane is a standard example of a radical chain reaction.

### 3.1 Initiation

Light causes homolytic cleavage of chlorine.

\[
Cl_2\xrightarrow{h\nu}2Cl^\bullet
\]

### 3.2 Propagation

A chlorine radical removes hydrogen from methane.

\[
Cl^\bullet+CH_4\rightarrow HCl+CH_3^\bullet
\]

The methyl radical reacts with chlorine.

\[
CH_3^\bullet+Cl_2\rightarrow CH_3Cl+Cl^\bullet
\]

The newly formed chlorine radical continues the chain.

### 3.3 Termination

Two radicals combine to form a stable molecule.

\[
Cl^\bullet+Cl^\bullet\rightarrow Cl_2
\]

\[
CH_3^\bullet+Cl^\bullet\rightarrow CH_3Cl
\]

Termination removes radicals from the reaction mixture.

## 4. Nucleophilic Substitution

Nucleophilic substitution occurs when a nucleophile replaces a leaving group.

A common example is the hydrolysis of a haloalkane.

\[
CH_3Br+OH^-\rightarrow CH_3OH+Br^-
\]

Two important mechanisms are \(S_N1\) and \(S_N2\).

### 4.1 \(S_N2\) Mechanism

The \(S_N2\) reaction occurs in a single concerted step.

The nucleophile attacks the carbon from the side opposite the leaving group.

The bond to the nucleophile forms as the bond to the leaving group breaks.

The rate law is:

\[
Rate=k[RX][Nu^-]
\]

The reaction rate depends on both the substrate and nucleophile concentrations.

The mechanism commonly favours less sterically hindered substrates.

### 4.2 \(S_N1\) Mechanism

The \(S_N1\) reaction occurs through a carbocation intermediate.

First, the leaving group departs.

\[
R-X\rightarrow R^++X^-
\]

Then the nucleophile attacks the carbocation.

The rate law is:

\[
Rate=k[RX]
\]

The reaction rate depends on the substrate concentration.

Tertiary carbocations are often favoured because of their greater stability.

## 5. Electrophilic Addition

Alkenes contain electron-rich pi bonds and commonly undergo electrophilic addition.

Example: addition of hydrogen bromide to ethene.

\[
CH_2=CH_2+HBr\rightarrow CH_3CH_2Br
\]

The pi bond interacts with an electrophile, followed by nucleophilic attack.

For unsymmetrical alkenes, the orientation of addition may follow Markovnikov's rule under ordinary ionic conditions.

## 6. Elimination Mechanisms

Elimination reactions remove groups from adjacent atoms, often forming an alkene.

Two important mechanisms are E1 and E2.

### 6.1 E1 Mechanism

E1 elimination occurs in two steps.

1. The leaving group departs, forming a carbocation.
2. A base removes a proton from an adjacent carbon, forming a double bond.

The rate depends mainly on the substrate concentration.

### 6.2 E2 Mechanism

E2 elimination occurs in one concerted step.

A base removes a proton while the leaving group departs and the double bond forms.

The rate depends on both substrate and base concentrations.

## 7. Purification of Organic Compounds

Organic samples may contain impurities from preparation or extraction.

Purification separates the desired compound from unwanted substances.

### 7.1 Crystallisation

Crystallisation is used to purify solids.

The impure solid is dissolved in a suitable hot solvent. The solution is cooled so that the pure compound crystallises.

Impurities remain dissolved or are removed by filtration.

### 7.2 Sublimation

Sublimation is used when a solid changes directly into vapour on heating and returns to solid form on cooling.

Examples include iodine and certain organic solids.

### 7.3 Simple Distillation

Simple distillation separates a volatile liquid from non-volatile impurities or from another liquid with a sufficiently large boiling-point difference.

The liquid is vaporised and the vapour is condensed.

### 7.4 Fractional Distillation

Fractional distillation separates miscible liquids with relatively close boiling points.

A fractionating column allows repeated vaporisation and condensation, improving separation.

### 7.5 Steam Distillation

Steam distillation is useful for volatile compounds that are immiscible or only slightly miscible with water and may decompose at their normal boiling points.

### 7.6 Differential Extraction

A compound is transferred between immiscible solvents according to its relative solubility.

A separating funnel is commonly used.

### 7.7 Chromatography

Chromatography separates components based on their different interactions with stationary and mobile phases.

In paper chromatography:

- Paper acts as the stationary phase.
- A solvent acts as the mobile phase.
- Components travel at different rates.

## 8. Chromatography and Retention Factor

The retention factor in paper or thin-layer chromatography is:

\[
R_f=
\frac{\text{Distance travelled by substance}}
{\text{Distance travelled by solvent front}}
\]

For example, if a substance travels 3.0 cm and the solvent front travels 6.0 cm:

\[
R_f=\frac{3.0}{6.0}=0.50
\]

The \(R_f\) value is characteristic of a substance only under specified experimental conditions.

## 9. Qualitative Organic Analysis

Qualitative analysis identifies which elements or functional groups are present.

### 9.1 Detection of Nitrogen, Sulfur and Halogens

The sodium fusion test, also called Lassaigne's test, converts certain covalently bonded elements into water-soluble ionic forms.

The resulting extract can be tested for nitrogen, sulfur and halogens.

### 9.2 Tests for Unsaturation

Bromine water can be decolourised by many alkenes and alkynes through addition reactions.

Cold dilute alkaline potassium permanganate can also react with many unsaturated compounds.

These tests must be interpreted carefully because other substances can also react with the reagents.

### 9.3 Functional Group Tests

Examples include:

- Carbonyl compounds: 2,4-DNP can produce a coloured precipitate.
- Aldehydes: Tollens' reagent can produce a silver mirror.
- Carboxylic acids: sodium bicarbonate can release carbon dioxide.
- Phenols: ferric chloride can produce characteristic colours with suitable phenols.

## 10. Quantitative Organic Analysis

Quantitative analysis determines the amount or percentage of an element in a compound.

For example, percentage composition can be determined from the masses of elements obtained during analysis.

\[
\%\text{ element}=
\frac{\text{Mass of element}}{\text{Mass of sample}}\times100
\]

Combustion analysis is commonly used to determine carbon and hydrogen content.

## 11. Common Mistakes and Exam Points

- A mechanism explains individual steps, not just the overall equation.
- \(S_N1\) involves a carbocation intermediate.
- \(S_N2\) occurs in a single concerted step.
- E1 and E2 are elimination mechanisms.
- Crystallisation is commonly used to purify solids.
- Fractional distillation is useful for miscible liquids with close boiling points.
- Chromatography separates compounds based on different interactions with two phases.
- \(R_f\) is the ratio of substance travel distance to solvent-front distance.

## 12. Lesson Recap

- Organic reactions include substitution, addition, elimination, rearrangement, oxidation and reduction.
- Radical substitution proceeds through initiation, propagation and termination.
- Nucleophilic substitution occurs through \(S_N1\) or \(S_N2\) mechanisms.
- Elimination occurs through E1 or E2 pathways.
- Purification methods include crystallisation, sublimation, distillation, extraction and chromatography.
- Qualitative and quantitative analysis identify and measure components of organic compounds.
