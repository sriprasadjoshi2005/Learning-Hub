# Lesson 11: Introduction to Chemical Bonding

Chemical bonding explains how atoms combine to form molecules, compounds, and extended structures. Most atoms do not exist independently under ordinary conditions because interactions between their electrons and nuclei can produce more stable arrangements.

Chemical bonds arise from electrostatic attractions and the redistribution or sharing of electrons between atoms. Understanding these interactions helps explain molecular structures, physical properties, chemical reactions, and the formation of materials.

This lesson introduces the fundamental principles of chemical bonding, the reasons atoms form bonds, the octet rule, types of chemical bonds, bond formation, and factors affecting bond strength.

## 1. Introduction to Chemical Bonding

### 1.1 Definition

A chemical bond is an attractive interaction that holds atoms or ions together in a stable chemical structure.

Chemical bonds form when the total energy of the interacting particles decreases relative to their separated state.

### 1.2 Why Do Atoms Form Chemical Bonds?

Atoms form chemical bonds because suitable arrangements of atoms can have lower potential energy and greater stability.

During bond formation:
- Attractive forces develop between electrons and nuclei.
- Electron-electron and nucleus-nucleus repulsions also occur.
- A stable arrangement is established when the total energy reaches a minimum.

The resulting arrangement is generally more stable than the separated atoms.

### 1.3 Role of Valence Electrons

Valence electrons are the outermost electrons of an atom that commonly participate in chemical bonding.

Examples:

| Element | Electronic configuration | Valence electrons |
|---|---|---:|
| Hydrogen | 1s¹ | 1 |
| Carbon | 1s² 2s² 2p² | 4 |
| Nitrogen | 1s² 2s² 2p³ | 5 |
| Oxygen | 1s² 2s² 2p⁴ | 6 |
| Sodium | [Ne] 3s¹ | 1 |
| Chlorine | [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵ | 7 |

The number and arrangement of valence electrons strongly influence the bonding behaviour of an element.

## 2. The Octet Rule

### 2.1 Statement

The octet rule states that atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons to achieve eight electrons in their valence shell, resembling the electronic configuration of a noble gas.

### 2.2 Examples

**Sodium chloride (NaCl)**

Sodium has one valence electron:

\[
Na:2,8,1
\]

It loses one electron:

\[
Na\rightarrow Na^++e^-
\]

Chlorine has seven valence electrons:

\[
Cl:2,8,7
\]

It gains one electron:

\[
Cl+e^-\rightarrow Cl^-
\]

The resulting ions have stable noble-gas-like configurations.

**Methane (CH₄)**

Carbon has four valence electrons and shares electrons with four hydrogen atoms.

Carbon achieves an octet, while each hydrogen achieves a duet.

### 2.3 Duet Rule

The first shell can accommodate a maximum of two electrons.

Hydrogen and helium are associated with this stable two-electron arrangement.

Hydrogen generally forms one covalent bond to achieve a duet.

### 2.4 Limitations of the Octet Rule

The octet rule is useful for predicting many simple compounds, but it is not universal.

Important exceptions include:

**Incomplete octet**

Some atoms remain surrounded by fewer than eight electrons.

Examples:
- BeCl₂.
- BF₃.

**Odd-electron molecules**

Some molecules contain an odd total number of electrons.

Examples:
- NO.
- NO₂.

**Expanded valence shell**

Some elements, particularly certain elements from Period 3 onward, form compounds commonly represented with more than eight electrons around the central atom in Lewis structures.

Examples:
- PCl₅.
- SF₆.

The octet rule is an introductory guideline rather than a complete theory of chemical bonding.

## 3. Types of Chemical Bonds

The major types of chemical bonding include:

1. Ionic bonding.
2. Covalent bonding.
3. Coordinate covalent bonding.
4. Metallic bonding.

### 3.1 Ionic Bonding

Ionic bonding results from electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.

It commonly occurs when one atom transfers electron density to another, producing cations and anions.

Example:

\[
Na^++Cl^-\rightarrow NaCl
\]

Ionic compounds generally form extended crystal lattices rather than separate NaCl molecules.

### 3.2 Covalent Bonding

Covalent bonding involves the sharing of electron pairs between atoms.

Example:

\[
H\cdot+\cdot H\rightarrow H:H
\]

The two hydrogen atoms share one electron pair to form H₂.

### 3.3 Coordinate Covalent Bonding

A coordinate covalent bond is formed when both electrons in a shared pair are initially supplied by the same atom.

Example:

\[
NH_3+H^+\rightarrow NH_4^+
\]

Nitrogen donates its lone pair to a proton to form an additional N–H bond.

After formation, the coordinate bond is not fundamentally different from the other N–H covalent bonds in ammonium.

### 3.4 Metallic Bonding

Metallic bonding involves the attraction between positively charged metal ion cores and delocalized electrons throughout a metallic structure.

This model helps explain:
- Electrical conductivity.
- Thermal conductivity.
- Metallic lustre.
- Malleability.
- Ductility.

## 4. Lewis Symbols and Valence Electrons

### 4.1 Lewis Symbols

A Lewis symbol represents an element's chemical symbol surrounded by dots corresponding to its valence electrons.

Examples:

- Hydrogen: H with one dot.
- Carbon: C with four dots.
- Nitrogen: N with five dots.
- Oxygen: O with six dots.
- Chlorine: Cl with seven dots.

### 4.2 Lewis Electron-Dot Representation

The dots are distributed around the four sides of the element symbol.

Each side can initially accommodate one electron before pairing begins.

This representation helps identify:
- Bonding electrons.
- Lone pairs.
- Possible bonding capacity.
- Electron transfer or sharing.

### 4.3 Bonding Pairs and Lone Pairs

A bonding pair is an electron pair shared between atoms.

A lone pair is a pair of valence electrons that is not shared in a conventional covalent bond.

Example: Water.

Oxygen has six valence electrons.

In H₂O:
- Two electron pairs form O–H bonds.
- Two electron pairs remain as lone pairs on oxygen.

## 5. Bond Formation and Potential Energy

### 5.1 Energy Changes During Bond Formation

When two atoms approach each other, their potential energy changes because of attractive and repulsive interactions.

At large separation, the interaction energy approaches zero by convention.

As the atoms approach:
- Attraction between each nucleus and the other atom's electrons lowers the energy.
- Repulsive interactions become increasingly important at short distances.

At an equilibrium separation, the potential energy reaches a minimum.

### 5.2 Equilibrium Bond Distance

The equilibrium bond distance is the internuclear separation at which the potential energy is minimum.

At this distance, attractive and repulsive forces balance.

### 5.3 Bond Dissociation Energy

Bond dissociation enthalpy is the enthalpy required to break a specified covalent bond homolytically in the gaseous phase.

For a diatomic molecule:

\[
AB(g)\rightarrow A(g)+B(g)
\]

A larger bond dissociation enthalpy generally indicates a stronger bond.

## 6. Factors Affecting Bond Strength

### 6.1 Bond Order

Bond order describes the number of bonding interactions between two atoms.

For many simple molecules:

- Single bond: bond order 1.
- Double bond: bond order 2.
- Triple bond: bond order 3.

Higher bond order generally corresponds to:
- Greater bond strength.
- Shorter bond length.

### 6.2 Atomic Size

For comparable bonds, smaller atoms often form shorter bonds.

The overlap of their atomic orbitals can be more effective when the atoms approach sufficiently closely.

### 6.3 Orbital Overlap

Effective orbital overlap contributes to stronger covalent bonding.

The extent and orientation of overlap influence bond strength and directionality.

### 6.4 Electronegativity Difference

Electronegativity difference influences the distribution of electron density in a bond.

A larger difference generally produces greater bond polarity.

However, ionic and covalent bonding exist along a continuum rather than as completely separate categories.

## 7. Bonding and Physical Properties

### 7.1 Ionic Compounds

Ionic compounds commonly:
- Have relatively high melting and boiling points.
- Form crystalline solids.
- Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in suitable solvents.
- Are often soluble in polar solvents.

### 7.2 Covalent Molecular Substances

Many molecular covalent substances:
- Have relatively low melting and boiling points.
- Exist as gases, liquids, or soft solids.
- Do not conduct electricity effectively.
- Have properties influenced by intermolecular forces.

These are general patterns, not universal rules.

### 7.3 Giant Covalent Structures

Some covalent substances form extended networks.

Examples:
- Diamond.
- Silicon dioxide.
- Silicon carbide.

They often have high melting temperatures because breaking the structure requires disrupting many strong covalent bonds.

### 7.4 Metallic Substances

Metals commonly exhibit:
- Electrical conductivity.
- Thermal conductivity.
- Malleability.
- Ductility.
- Metallic lustre.

## 8. Important Comparisons

| Property | Ionic bonding | Covalent bonding | Metallic bonding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main interaction | Attraction between ions | Shared electron pairs | Metal cores and delocalized electrons |
| Common participants | Metals and non-metals | Usually non-metals | Metals |
| Directionality | Generally non-directional | Directional | Generally non-directional |
| Electrical conductivity | Molten or suitable aqueous solutions | Usually poor for molecular substances | Generally good |
| Typical structure | Ionic lattice | Molecules or networks | Metallic lattice |

## 9. Practical Applications

### 9.1 Material Selection

Bonding models help explain why different materials are selected for electrical, structural, and thermal applications.

### 9.2 Biological Molecules

Covalent bonds form the molecular frameworks of proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, and nucleic acids.

### 9.3 Electronics

Covalent bonding and metallic bonding are important in understanding semiconductors, conductors, and insulating materials.

### 9.4 Industrial Materials

Ionic and covalent structures influence the properties of ceramics, glass, salts, and industrial compounds.

## 10. Common Exam Traps

1. Chemical bonding generally lowers the total energy of the interacting particles.
2. Valence electrons play the major role in ordinary chemical bonding.
3. The octet rule is a useful guideline, not a universal law.
4. Hydrogen commonly follows the duet rule.
5. Ionic bonding involves electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
6. Covalent bonding involves shared electron pairs.
7. A coordinate covalent bond initially receives both bonding electrons from one atom.
8. Metallic bonding involves delocalized electrons.
9. Bond formation and bond breaking have opposite energy effects.
10. A shorter bond is often stronger when comparing similar bonds.
11. Higher bond order generally corresponds to greater bond strength.
12. Ionic compounds do not generally conduct electricity in the solid state because their ions cannot move freely.
13. Not all covalent substances have low melting points; giant covalent structures can have very high melting points.
