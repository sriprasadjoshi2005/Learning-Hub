# Lesson 42: Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers

Alcohols, phenols, and ethers are important oxygen-containing organic compounds. Their reactions involve the hydroxyl group, aromatic ring, and carbon–oxygen bonds. Understanding their acidity, preparation, oxidation, dehydration, and substitution reactions is essential for organic reaction prediction.

## 1. Classification and Structure

### 1.1 Alcohols

Alcohols contain a hydroxyl group bonded to an sp³-hybridised carbon atom.

General formula:

\[
R-OH
\]

Examples:
- CH₃OH: methanol
- CH₃CH₂OH: ethanol
- CH₃CH₂CH₂OH: propan-1-ol

Alcohols are classified as primary, secondary, or tertiary according to the number of carbon groups attached to the carbon bearing the hydroxyl group.

### 1.2 Phenols

Phenols contain a hydroxyl group directly attached to an aromatic ring.

\[
C_6H_5OH
\]

The simplest member is phenol.

### 1.3 Ethers

Ethers contain an oxygen atom bonded to two carbon-containing groups.

\[
R-O-R'
\]

Examples:
- CH₃OCH₃: methoxymethane
- CH₃OC₂H₅: methoxyethane
- C₆H₅OCH₃: methoxybenzene

## 2. Nomenclature

For alcohols, choose the longest chain containing the hydroxyl group and use the suffix `-ol`.

Examples:
- CH₃CH₂OH: ethanol
- CH₃CH(OH)CH₃: propan-2-ol
- HOCH₂CH₂OH: ethane-1,2-diol

For phenols, the carbon attached to OH is assigned position 1.

For ethers, IUPAC naming generally treats the smaller group and oxygen as an alkoxy substituent.

Example:

\[
CH_3OCH_2CH_3
\]

Methoxyethane.

## 3. Preparation of Alcohols

### 3.1 Hydration of Alkenes

Water adds across an alkene double bond under suitable acidic conditions.

\[
CH_2=CH_2+H_2O
\xrightarrow{H^+}
CH_3CH_2OH
\]

For unsymmetrical alkenes, acid-catalysed hydration generally follows Markovnikov orientation.

### 3.2 Hydrolysis of Haloalkanes

\[
RX+KOH_{(aq)}\rightarrow ROH+KX
\]

Example:

\[
CH_3CH_2Br+KOH\rightarrow CH_3CH_2OH+KBr
\]

### 3.3 Reduction of Carbonyl Compounds

Aldehydes produce primary alcohols:

\[
RCHO+2[H]\rightarrow RCH_2OH
\]

Ketones produce secondary alcohols:

\[
RCOR'+2[H]\rightarrow RCH(OH)R'
\]

Common reducing agents include sodium borohydride and lithium aluminium hydride.

### 3.4 Grignard Reactions

Grignard reagents react with carbonyl compounds followed by acidic work-up.

- Formaldehyde generally gives primary alcohols.
- Other aldehydes generally give secondary alcohols.
- Ketones generally give tertiary alcohols.

This is an important method of increasing carbon-chain length.

## 4. Physical Properties of Alcohols

Alcohols form intermolecular hydrogen bonds.

Consequences include:
- Higher boiling points than comparable hydrocarbons.
- Lower volatility than similar-sized non-hydrogen-bonding molecules.
- High water solubility for smaller alcohols.

Water solubility generally decreases as the hydrocarbon chain becomes longer because the nonpolar portion becomes increasingly important.

## 5. Chemical Reactions of Alcohols

### 5.1 Reaction with Active Metals

Alcohols react with sodium to produce sodium alkoxides and hydrogen gas.

\[
2ROH+2Na\rightarrow2RONa+H_2
\]

Example:

\[
2C_2H_5OH+2Na\rightarrow2C_2H_5ONa+H_2
\]

### 5.2 Reaction with Hydrogen Halides

\[
ROH+HX\rightarrow RX+H_2O
\]

The reaction mechanism depends on the structure of the alcohol and reaction conditions.

### 5.3 Dehydration

Alcohols lose water under acidic conditions to produce alkenes.

\[
CH_3CH_2OH
\xrightarrow[\text{heat}]{\text{conc. }H_2SO_4}
CH_2=CH_2+H_2O
\]

Dehydration is an elimination reaction.

### 5.4 Oxidation

Primary alcohols can be oxidised to aldehydes and then to carboxylic acids.

\[
RCH_2OH\rightarrow RCHO\rightarrow RCOOH
\]

Secondary alcohols are oxidised to ketones.

\[
R_2CHOH\rightarrow R_2CO
\]

Tertiary alcohols resist ordinary oxidation because the carbon bearing OH has no hydrogen atom attached to it.

## 6. Acidity of Alcohols and Phenols

Alcohols are weak acids.

\[
ROH\rightleftharpoons RO^-+H^+
\]

Phenol is generally more acidic than ordinary alcohols because the phenoxide ion is stabilised by resonance.

\[
C_6H_5OH\rightleftharpoons C_6H_5O^-+H^+
\]

The negative charge in phenoxide can be delocalised into the aromatic ring.

Electron-withdrawing substituents generally increase phenol acidity by stabilising the phenoxide ion. Electron-donating substituents generally decrease it.

Phenol reacts with sodium hydroxide:

\[
C_6H_5OH+NaOH\rightarrow C_6H_5ONa+H_2O
\]

Ordinary alcohols generally do not react appreciably with aqueous NaOH because they are much weaker acids.

## 7. Reactions of Phenol

### 7.1 Bromination

Phenol reacts readily with bromine water to form 2,4,6-tribromophenol.

\[
C_6H_5OH+3Br_2
\rightarrow C_6H_2Br_3OH+3HBr
\]

The product forms a white precipitate.

### 7.2 Nitration

Dilute nitric acid produces a mixture of ortho- and para-nitrophenol.

Concentrated nitric acid under suitable conditions produces 2,4,6-trinitrophenol, also known as picric acid.

### 7.3 Kolbe–Schmitt Reaction

Sodium phenoxide reacts with carbon dioxide under pressure and heat, followed by acidification, to produce salicylic acid.

\[
C_6H_5ONa+CO_2
\xrightarrow{\text{pressure, heat}}
\text{sodium salicylate}
\]

Acidification produces 2-hydroxybenzoic acid.

### 7.4 Reimer–Tiemann Reaction

Phenol reacts with chloroform and aqueous sodium hydroxide under suitable conditions to produce salicylaldehyde as the major product after work-up.

This reaction introduces a formyl group into the aromatic ring.

## 8. Ethers

### 8.1 Preparation: Williamson Ether Synthesis

An alkoxide ion reacts with a primary haloalkane through nucleophilic substitution.

\[
RONa+R'X\rightarrow ROR'+NaX
\]

Example:

\[
C_2H_5ONa+CH_3I\rightarrow C_2H_5OCH_3+NaI
\]

Primary haloalkanes are generally preferred because SN2 substitution is favoured.

### 8.2 Physical Properties

Ethers have polar carbon–oxygen bonds but cannot form hydrogen bonds with themselves as alcohols do.

They therefore generally have lower boiling points than comparable alcohols.

They can accept hydrogen bonds from water, so smaller ethers have some water solubility.

### 8.3 Cleavage by Hydrogen Halides

Ethers react with concentrated HI or HBr under suitable conditions.

\[
ROR'+HI\rightarrow RI+R'OH
\]

With excess HI and suitable heating, further cleavage can occur.

For aryl alkyl ethers such as anisole, cleavage generally occurs at the alkyl–oxygen bond rather than the aryl–oxygen bond.

## 9. Comparison of Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers

| Property | Alcohols | Phenols | Ethers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional group | R–OH | Ar–OH | R–O–R′ |
| OH attached to | sp³ carbon | Aromatic ring | Not present |
| Acidity | Weak | Stronger than alcohols | Very weak |
| Self hydrogen bonding | Yes | Yes | No |
| Typical reaction | Oxidation | Electrophilic substitution | Cleavage |

## 10. Common Exam Traps

- Phenol is not classified as an ordinary alcohol.
- Phenol is more acidic than ethanol because phenoxide is resonance-stabilised.
- Primary alcohols generally oxidise first to aldehydes.
- Secondary alcohols oxidise to ketones.
- Tertiary alcohols resist ordinary oxidation.
- Alcoholic dehydration produces an alkene, not an ether.
- Williamson synthesis is most effective with primary haloalkanes.
- Phenol gives a white precipitate with bromine water.

## 11. Summary

- Alcohols contain an OH group attached to an sp³ carbon.
- Phenols contain OH directly attached to an aromatic ring.
- Ethers contain an oxygen atom between two carbon groups.
- Phenols are more acidic than ordinary alcohols.
- Alcohol oxidation depends on whether the alcohol is primary, secondary, or tertiary.
- Williamson synthesis is an important ether preparation method.
