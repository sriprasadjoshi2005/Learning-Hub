# Lesson 43: Aldehydes and Ketones

Aldehydes and ketones are carbonyl compounds containing the carbon–oxygen double bond. They are central to organic chemistry because the polar carbonyl group undergoes nucleophilic addition, oxidation, reduction, and carbon–carbon bond-forming reactions.

## 1. Structure and Classification

The carbonyl group is:

\[
>C=O
\]

The carbonyl carbon is electrophilic because oxygen attracts electron density.

\[
C^{\delta+}=O^{\delta-}
\]

### 1.1 Aldehydes

Aldehydes contain a terminal carbonyl group bonded to at least one hydrogen atom.

General formula:

\[
RCHO
\]

Examples:
- HCHO: methanal
- CH₃CHO: ethanal
- C₆H₅CHO: benzaldehyde

### 1.2 Ketones

Ketones contain a carbonyl group bonded to two carbon-containing groups.

\[
RCOR'
\]

Examples:
- CH₃COCH₃: propanone
- CH₃COCH₂CH₃: butan-2-one

## 2. Nomenclature

Aldehydes use the suffix `-al`.

Examples:
- HCHO: methanal
- CH₃CHO: ethanal
- CH₃CH₂CHO: propanal

Ketones use the suffix `-one`.

Examples:
- CH₃COCH₃: propanone
- CH₃COCH₂CH₃: butan-2-one

The longest chain containing the carbonyl group is selected, and the ketone carbonyl receives the lowest possible number.

## 3. Preparation

### 3.1 Oxidation of Alcohols

Primary alcohols produce aldehydes under controlled oxidation.

\[
RCH_2OH+[O]\rightarrow RCHO+H_2O
\]

Further oxidation can produce carboxylic acids.

Secondary alcohols produce ketones:

\[
R_2CHOH+[O]\rightarrow R_2CO+H_2O
\]

### 3.2 Dehydrogenation of Alcohols

Passing alcohol vapours over heated copper can produce carbonyl compounds.

\[
RCH_2OH\xrightarrow{Cu,\,573K}RCHO+H_2
\]

Secondary alcohols similarly produce ketones.

### 3.3 Ozonolysis of Alkenes

Ozonolysis breaks a carbon–carbon double bond and can produce aldehydes and ketones after suitable work-up.

The products depend on the structure of the original alkene and the reaction conditions.

### 3.4 Friedel–Crafts Acylation

Aromatic compounds can react with acyl chlorides in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst such as AlCl₃ to form aromatic ketones.

\[
C_6H_6+RCOCl
\xrightarrow{AlCl_3}
C_6H_5COR+HCl
\]

## 4. Physical Properties

- The carbonyl group is polar.
- Aldehydes and ketones have dipole–dipole attractions.
- They cannot form hydrogen bonds with themselves through the carbonyl oxygen alone.
- They can accept hydrogen bonds from water.
- Lower molecular mass members are often water-soluble.

Their boiling points are generally higher than comparable nonpolar hydrocarbons but lower than comparable alcohols.

## 5. Nucleophilic Addition

The electrophilic carbonyl carbon is attacked by a nucleophile.

General representation:

\[
R_2C=O+Nu^-\rightarrow R_2C(O^-)Nu
\]

The oxygen is subsequently protonated when the reaction conditions provide a proton source.

### 5.1 Addition of Hydrogen Cyanide

\[
R_2C=O+HCN\rightarrow R_2C(OH)CN
\]

The product is a cyanohydrin.

This reaction introduces a carbon atom through the cyanide group.

### 5.2 Addition of Sodium Bisulfite

Aldehydes and many ketones form bisulfite addition compounds with sodium hydrogen sulfite.

This reaction can be used in purification and identification.

### 5.3 Addition of Grignard Reagents

Grignard reagents add to carbonyl compounds, followed by acidic work-up.

- Formaldehyde produces primary alcohols.
- Other aldehydes produce secondary alcohols.
- Ketones produce tertiary alcohols.

This reaction is important for carbon–carbon bond formation.

## 6. Reduction Reactions

Aldehydes are reduced to primary alcohols.

\[
RCHO+2[H]\rightarrow RCH_2OH
\]

Ketones are reduced to secondary alcohols.

\[
RCOR'+2[H]\rightarrow RCH(OH)R'
\]

Common reducing agents include:
- NaBH₄
- LiAlH₄
- H₂ with suitable catalysts

### 6.1 Clemmensen Reduction

The carbonyl group is reduced to a methylene group under strongly acidic conditions using zinc amalgam.

\[
RCO R'\xrightarrow{Zn(Hg),\,HCl}RCH_2R'
\]

### 6.2 Wolff–Kishner Reduction

The carbonyl group is reduced to a methylene group under strongly basic conditions using hydrazine and heat.

\[
R_2C=O\xrightarrow{NH_2NH_2,\,KOH,\,heat}R_2CH_2
\]

The acidic and basic conditions distinguish these two named reactions.

## 7. Oxidation Reactions

### 7.1 Aldehydes

Aldehydes are readily oxidised to carboxylic acids.

\[
RCHO+[O]\rightarrow RCOOH
\]

### 7.2 Ketones

Ketones resist mild oxidation. Strong oxidising conditions may break carbon–carbon bonds and produce smaller carboxylic acids.

This difference forms the basis of several qualitative tests.

## 8. Chemical Tests

### 8.1 Tollens' Test

Aldehydes reduce Tollens' reagent, producing metallic silver.

Observation: a silver mirror or dark silver deposit.

Ketones generally do not give this test.

### 8.2 Fehling's Test

Many aliphatic aldehydes reduce Fehling's solution to brick-red copper(I) oxide.

\[
Cu_2O
\]

Most ordinary ketones do not give this result.

### 8.3 Schiff's Test

Aldehydes restore the pink or magenta colour of Schiff's reagent.

### 8.4 Iodoform Test

Compounds containing the \(CH_3CO-\) group, and compounds oxidisable to it, can give a yellow precipitate of iodoform.

\[
CHI_3
\]

Ethanol and ethanal also give the test because they can be converted into the required reactive structure under the test conditions.

## 9. Alpha Hydrogen and Aldol Condensation

An alpha carbon is directly adjacent to the carbonyl carbon. Hydrogen atoms attached to this carbon are called alpha hydrogens.

Aldehydes and ketones with at least one alpha hydrogen can undergo aldol condensation in dilute base.

Example:

\[
2CH_3CHO
\xrightarrow{\text{dilute }NaOH}
CH_3CH(OH)CH_2CHO
\]

The product is 3-hydroxybutanal.

On heating, dehydration can produce an alpha,beta-unsaturated carbonyl compound.

\[
CH_3CH(OH)CH_2CHO
\rightarrow CH_3CH=CHCHO+H_2O
\]

## 10. Cannizzaro Reaction

Aldehydes without alpha hydrogen can undergo disproportionation in concentrated alkali.

One molecule is oxidised to a carboxylate ion, while another is reduced to an alcohol.

Example:

\[
2HCHO+NaOH\rightarrow HCOONa+CH_3OH
\]

This is a redox reaction occurring between two molecules of the same aldehyde.

## 11. Aldehydes Versus Ketones

| Feature | Aldehydes | Ketones |
|---|---|---|
| Carbonyl position | Usually terminal | Within carbon chain |
| Carbonyl groups attached to | At least one H | Two carbon groups |
| Mild oxidation | Readily oxidised | Generally resistant |
| Tollens' test | Usually positive | Usually negative |
| Fehling's test | Many aliphatic aldehydes positive | Usually negative |
| Reduction product | Primary alcohol | Secondary alcohol |

## 12. Common Exam Traps

- Aldehydes generally oxidise more readily than ketones.
- Both aldehydes and ketones undergo nucleophilic addition.
- Tollens' reagent distinguishes most aldehydes from ordinary ketones.
- Fehling's test is not equally reliable for all aromatic aldehydes.
- Aldol condensation requires at least one alpha hydrogen.
- Cannizzaro reaction is characteristic of aldehydes without alpha hydrogen.
- Clemmensen reduction uses acidic conditions; Wolff–Kishner uses basic conditions.
- Iodoform test is associated with the methyl carbonyl group or compounds oxidisable to it.

## 13. Summary

- Aldehydes and ketones contain a polar carbonyl group.
- Nucleophiles attack the electrophilic carbonyl carbon.
- Aldehydes reduce to primary alcohols; ketones reduce to secondary alcohols.
- Aldehydes are generally oxidised to carboxylic acids.
- Tollens' and Fehling's tests help identify aldehydes.
- Aldol and Cannizzaro reactions depend on the presence or absence of alpha hydrogen.
