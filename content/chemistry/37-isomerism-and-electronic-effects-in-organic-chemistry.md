# Lesson 37: Isomerism and Electronic Effects in Organic Chemistry

Organic compounds can have the same molecular formula but different structures or spatial arrangements. This phenomenon is called isomerism.

The reactivity of organic molecules is also strongly influenced by the movement and distribution of electrons. Inductive effects, resonance, hyperconjugation and electromeric effects help explain stability, acidity, basicity and reaction mechanisms.

## 1. Isomerism

Isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different arrangements of atoms.

They are broadly classified into structural isomerism and stereoisomerism.

## 2. Structural Isomerism

Structural isomers have different connectivity of atoms.

### 2.1 Chain Isomerism

Compounds differ in the arrangement of their carbon skeletons.

Example: \(C_4H_{10}\).

- Butane: \(CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_3\)
- 2-Methylpropane: \(CH_3CH(CH_3)CH_3\)

### 2.2 Position Isomerism

Compounds have the same carbon skeleton and functional group but differ in the position of the functional group or multiple bond.

Example:

- Propan-1-ol: \(CH_3CH_2CH_2OH\)
- Propan-2-ol: \(CH_3CH(OH)CH_3\)

### 2.3 Functional Group Isomerism

Compounds have the same molecular formula but different functional groups.

Example: \(C_2H_6O\).

- Ethanol: \(CH_3CH_2OH\)
- Methoxymethane: \(CH_3OCH_3\)

### 2.4 Metamerism

Metamers have the same functional group but different alkyl groups attached to a polyvalent functional atom or group.

It can occur in ethers, secondary amines and related compounds.

### 2.5 Ring-Chain Isomerism

One isomer contains an open chain while another contains a ring.

Example: \(C_3H_6\).

- Propene.
- Cyclopropane.

### 2.6 Tautomerism

Tautomers are rapidly interconverting structural forms that differ in the position of a hydrogen atom and a double bond.

Keto-enol tautomerism is a common example.

\[
CH_3COCH_3\rightleftharpoons CH_2=C(OH)CH_3
\]

## 3. Stereoisomerism

Stereoisomers have the same connectivity but differ in their three-dimensional arrangement.

Important forms include geometrical and optical isomerism.

### 3.1 Geometrical Isomerism

Geometrical isomerism arises when rotation is restricted, commonly around a carbon-carbon double bond or within a ring.

For geometrical isomerism in a simple alkene, each carbon of the double bond must have two different substituents.

In cis isomers, similar groups lie on the same side.

In trans isomers, similar groups lie on opposite sides.

For more general systems, E/Z notation is used.

### 3.2 Optical Isomerism

Optical isomers are non-superimposable mirror images called enantiomers.

A common source of chirality is a tetrahedral carbon attached to four different groups.

Such a carbon is called a chiral centre.

Enantiomers rotate plane-polarised light in opposite directions under the same conditions.

## 4. Bond Fission

A covalent bond can break in two major ways.

### 4.1 Homolytic Fission

Each atom takes one electron from the shared pair.

\[
A-B\rightarrow A^\bullet+B^\bullet
\]

The products are free radicals.

Homolytic fission is commonly promoted by heat, light or radical initiators.

### 4.2 Heterolytic Fission

One atom takes both bonding electrons.

\[
A-B\rightarrow A^++B^-
\]

The products are ions.

The direction of electron movement depends on electronegativity and the stability of the resulting ions.

## 5. Reaction Intermediates

Reaction intermediates are short-lived species formed during a multistep reaction.

### 5.1 Carbocations

A carbocation is a positively charged carbon species.

Example:

\[
CH_3^+
\]

Carbocations are generally electron-deficient and act as electrophiles.

Their stability in simple alkyl systems generally follows:

\[
3^\circ>2^\circ>1^\circ>CH_3^+
\]

This trend is influenced by inductive effects and hyperconjugation.

### 5.2 Carbanions

A carbanion contains a negatively charged carbon atom.

Example:

\[
CH_3^-
\]

It has a lone pair and commonly behaves as a nucleophile or base.

For simple alkyl carbanions, stability generally follows the reverse trend:

\[
CH_3^->1^\circ>2^\circ>3^\circ
\]

Electron-withdrawing groups and resonance can strongly stabilise carbanions.

### 5.3 Free Radicals

Free radicals contain an unpaired electron.

They are often formed through homolytic bond cleavage.

In simple alkyl systems, radical stability commonly follows:

\[
3^\circ>2^\circ>1^\circ>CH_3^\bullet
\]

## 6. Electrophiles and Nucleophiles

### 6.1 Electrophiles

Electrophiles are electron-pair acceptors.

They are attracted to electron-rich regions.

Examples:

\[
H^+,\quad NO_2^+,\quad BF_3,\quad AlCl_3
\]

### 6.2 Nucleophiles

Nucleophiles are electron-pair donors.

They attack electron-deficient centres.

Examples:

\[
OH^-,\quad CN^-,\quad NH_3,\quad H_2O
\]

A nucleophile does not have to carry a negative charge. Neutral molecules with available lone pairs can also act as nucleophiles.

## 7. Inductive Effect

The inductive effect is the permanent displacement of sigma-bond electron density due to electronegativity differences.

It is transmitted through sigma bonds and decreases rapidly with distance.

### 7.1 Negative Inductive Effect

Groups that withdraw electron density show a \(-I\) effect.

Examples include:

\[
-NO_2,\quad -CN,\quad -COOH,\quad -F,\quad -Cl
\]

### 7.2 Positive Inductive Effect

Groups that release electron density through sigma bonds show a \(+I\) effect.

Alkyl groups generally show a \(+I\) effect.

### 7.3 Applications

Inductive effects help explain:

- Relative acid strength.
- Relative base strength.
- Stability of carbocations and carbanions.
- Reactivity of organic compounds.

For example, electron-withdrawing groups stabilise the conjugate base of a carboxylic acid, generally increasing its acidity.

## 8. Resonance Effect

Resonance occurs when electrons are delocalised over a system of adjacent p-orbitals.

A molecule may be represented using multiple contributing structures.

The actual molecule is a resonance hybrid rather than rapidly switching between structures.

### 8.1 Positive Resonance Effect

Groups that donate electron density through resonance show a \(+R\) effect.

Examples include:

\[
-OH,\quad -OR,\quad -NH_2
\]

### 8.2 Negative Resonance Effect

Groups that withdraw electron density through resonance show a \(-R\) effect.

Examples include:

\[
-NO_2,\quad -CHO,\quad -COOH,\quad -CN
\]

### 8.3 Stability Through Resonance

Delocalisation often lowers the energy of a molecule or ion.

For example, the acetate ion is stabilised because its negative charge is delocalised over two oxygen atoms.

## 9. Hyperconjugation

Hyperconjugation is the delocalisation of electrons from a sigma bond, commonly a C-H bond, into an adjacent empty or partially filled orbital or pi system.

It contributes to the stability of carbocations and alkenes.

For simple alkyl carbocations, greater hyperconjugation generally increases stability.

This helps explain the usual order:

\[
3^\circ>2^\circ>1^\circ>CH_3^+
\]

## 10. Electromeric Effect

The electromeric effect is a temporary complete transfer of a pi-electron pair to one atom under the influence of an attacking reagent.

It occurs in compounds containing multiple bonds.

Unlike the inductive effect, it is temporary and operates during reagent attack.

## 11. Acidity and Basicity in Organic Compounds

### 11.1 Acidity

An acid donates a proton.

The strength of an acid depends largely on the stability of its conjugate base.

Factors include:

- Resonance stabilisation.
- Inductive effects.
- Electronegativity.
- Atomic size.
- Hybridisation.

For carbon acids, a greater s-character in the hybrid orbital holding the negative charge generally stabilises the conjugate base.

\[
sp>sp^2>sp^3
\]

### 11.2 Basicity

A base accepts a proton or donates an electron pair.

Basicity depends on the availability of the lone pair and the stability of the protonated form.

Resonance delocalisation can reduce the availability of a lone pair.

For example, the nitrogen lone pair in an amide is delocalised into the carbonyl group, making it much less basic than the lone pair in an ordinary amine.

## 12. Common Mistakes and Exam Points

- Structural isomers have different connectivity.
- Stereoisomers have the same connectivity but different spatial arrangements.
- Homolytic fission produces radicals.
- Heterolytic fission produces ions.
- Electrophiles accept electron pairs.
- Nucleophiles donate electron pairs.
- Inductive effects operate through sigma bonds.
- Resonance involves delocalisation of electrons across conjugated orbitals.
- Hyperconjugation involves sigma-electron delocalisation.
- A stable conjugate base generally corresponds to a stronger acid.

## 13. Lesson Recap

- Organic isomerism includes structural and stereoisomerism.
- Bonds can break homolytically or heterolytically.
- Carbocations, carbanions and radicals are important reaction intermediates.
- Electrophiles accept electron pairs, while nucleophiles donate them.
- Inductive, resonance, hyperconjugative and electromeric effects influence molecular stability and reactivity.
- Acidity and basicity depend on electronic structure and conjugate-species stability.
