# Lesson 35: Coordination Compounds and f-Block Elements

Coordination compounds contain a central metal atom or ion surrounded by molecules or ions called ligands. They are important in biological systems, analytical chemistry, catalysis and industrial processes.

This lesson develops the basic terminology, nomenclature, bonding, geometry, isomerism and magnetic behaviour of coordination compounds. It also introduces lanthanoids and actinoids.

## 1. Introduction to Coordination Compounds

A coordination compound contains a central metal atom or ion bonded to surrounding ligands through coordinate bonds.

A coordinate bond is formed when both electrons in the shared pair are donated by one atom or ion.

Example:

\[
[Cu(NH_3)_4]SO_4
\]

The complex ion is:

\[
[Cu(NH_3)_4]^{2+}
\]

Copper(II) is the central metal ion, and ammonia molecules are ligands.

## 2. Important Terms

### 2.1 Central Metal Ion

The central metal ion accepts electron pairs from ligands.

Examples include:

\[
Fe^{2+},\quad Cu^{2+},\quad Co^{3+}
\]

### 2.2 Ligand

A ligand donates an electron pair to the central metal.

Common ligands include:

| Ligand | Formula | Charge |
|---|---|---:|
| Aqua | \(H_2O\) | 0 |
| Ammine | \(NH_3\) | 0 |
| Carbonyl | \(CO\) | 0 |
| Chloro | \(Cl^-\) | -1 |
| Cyano | \(CN^-\) | -1 |
| Hydroxo | \(OH^-\) | -1 |

### 2.3 Coordination Number

The coordination number is the number of donor atoms directly bonded to the central metal ion.

For:

\[
[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}
\]

The coordination number is 6.

### 2.4 Coordination Sphere

The central metal and ligands enclosed within square brackets form the coordination sphere.

In:

\[
[Co(NH_3)_6]Cl_3
\]

The coordination sphere is \([Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}\), and the chloride ions are counterions.

### 2.5 Denticity

Denticity refers to the number of donor atoms through which a ligand attaches to a central metal ion.

- Monodentate: one donor atom.
- Bidentate: two donor atoms.
- Polydentate: multiple donor atoms.

Ethane-1,2-diamine is a bidentate ligand.

EDTA is a common hexadentate ligand.

## 3. Types of Ligands

### 3.1 Monodentate Ligands

They coordinate through one donor atom.

Examples:

\[
NH_3,\quad H_2O,\quad Cl^-
\]

### 3.2 Bidentate Ligands

They coordinate through two donor atoms.

Example:

\[
en=H_2NCH_2CH_2NH_2
\]

### 3.3 Polydentate Ligands

They coordinate through more than two donor atoms.

EDTA can coordinate through six donor atoms.

### 3.4 Ambidentate Ligands

Ambidentate ligands can coordinate through either of two different donor atoms, but generally through only one at a time.

Examples:

\[
NO_2^-,\quad SCN^-
\]

## 4. Oxidation State and Coordination Number

These two quantities are different.

The oxidation state is the formal charge assigned to the central metal after considering ligand charges.

The coordination number counts the donor atoms directly attached to the metal.

Example:

\[
[Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}
\]

Let the oxidation state of iron be \(x\).

\[
x+6(-1)=-4
\]

\[
x=+2
\]

The oxidation state of iron is \(+2\), while its coordination number is 6.

## 5. Nomenclature of Coordination Compounds

The names of coordination compounds follow systematic rules.

Important principles:

1. Name the cation before the anion.
2. Name ligands before the central metal.
3. Arrange ligand names alphabetically, ignoring multiplicative prefixes.
4. Use prefixes such as di-, tri-, tetra- and hexa- for repeated ligands.
5. Write the oxidation state of the metal in Roman numerals.
6. For anionic complexes, the metal name generally ends in -ate.

### 5.1 Examples

\[
[Co(NH_3)_6]Cl_3
\]

Name: hexaamminecobalt(III) chloride.

\[
K_4[Fe(CN)_6]
\]

Name: potassium hexacyanidoferrate(II).

\[
[Cu(NH_3)_4]SO_4
\]

Name: tetraamminecopper(II) sulfate.

## 6. Isomerism in Coordination Compounds

Isomers have the same molecular formula but differ in the arrangement of atoms or groups.

Coordination compounds exhibit structural and stereoisomerism.

### 6.1 Structural Isomerism

Structural isomers differ in the connectivity of ligands or ions.

Important types include:

- Ionisation isomerism.
- Hydrate or solvate isomerism.
- Linkage isomerism.
- Coordination isomerism.

### 6.2 Linkage Isomerism

Ambidentate ligands can attach through different donor atoms.

For example, nitrite can coordinate through nitrogen or oxygen.

### 6.3 Stereoisomerism

Stereoisomers have the same connectivity but different spatial arrangements.

Important types include:

- Geometrical isomerism.
- Optical isomerism.

### 6.4 Geometrical Isomerism

Square-planar complexes can show cis and trans forms.

For example:

\[
[Pt(NH_3)_2Cl_2]
\]

In the cis form, identical ligands are adjacent.

In the trans form, identical ligands are opposite.

Octahedral complexes can also show geometrical isomerism.

### 6.5 Optical Isomerism

Optical isomers are non-superimposable mirror images.

They are called enantiomers.

Some octahedral complexes containing bidentate ligands show optical isomerism.

## 7. Bonding in Coordination Compounds

Several models are used to explain metal-ligand bonding.

### 7.1 Valence Bond Theory

Valence bond theory explains bonding through overlap between ligand donor orbitals and suitable hybrid orbitals of the central metal ion.

Common hybridisation patterns include:

| Geometry | Hybridisation |
|---|---|
| Linear | \(sp\) |
| Tetrahedral | \(sp^3\) |
| Square planar | \(dsp^2\) |
| Octahedral | \(d^2sp^3\) or \(sp^3d^2\) |

These descriptions are useful for introductory coordination chemistry, although they do not fully explain all magnetic and spectral properties.

### 7.2 Crystal Field Theory

Crystal field theory treats ligands as sources of electrostatic fields that affect the energies of metal d-orbitals.

In an isolated metal ion, the five d-orbitals have equal energy in a spherical environment.

When ligands approach, the d-orbitals split into groups of different energies.

## 8. Crystal Field Splitting

### 8.1 Octahedral Complexes

In an octahedral field, the d-orbitals split into two sets.

- Lower-energy \(t_{2g}\): \(d_{xy}, d_{xz}, d_{yz}\)
- Higher-energy \(e_g\): \(d_{x^2-y^2}, d_{z^2}\)

The energy separation is called:

\[
\Delta_o
\]

### 8.2 Tetrahedral Complexes

In a tetrahedral field, the splitting pattern is reversed.

- Lower-energy e set.
- Higher-energy \(t_2\) set.

The tetrahedral splitting is smaller than the octahedral splitting for comparable metal-ligand systems.

Approximately:

\[
\Delta_t\approx\frac{4}{9}\Delta_o
\]

### 8.3 Strong-Field and Weak-Field Ligands

Weak-field ligands produce relatively small splitting.

Examples include:

\[
I^-,\quad Br^-,\quad Cl^-
\]

Strong-field ligands produce relatively large splitting.

Examples include:

\[
CN^-,\quad CO
\]

Whether a complex is high spin or low spin depends on the relationship between crystal field splitting and electron pairing energy.

## 9. Magnetic Behaviour of Complexes

A complex with unpaired electrons is paramagnetic.

A complex with all electrons paired is diamagnetic.

For example, octahedral \(Fe^{2+}\) is a \(d^6\) ion.

In a weak-field environment, it may form a high-spin arrangement with four unpaired electrons.

In a sufficiently strong-field environment, it may form a low-spin arrangement with no unpaired electrons.

## 10. Importance of Coordination Compounds

Coordination compounds have many applications.

- Haemoglobin contains an iron coordination centre.
- Chlorophyll contains magnesium.
- Vitamin B12 contains cobalt.
- EDTA is used in complexometric titrations.
- Certain platinum complexes are used in cancer treatment.
- Metal complexes are important catalysts in industrial chemistry.

## 11. Lanthanoids

Lanthanoids are the elements associated with filling the 4f subshell.

They generally exhibit the \(+3\) oxidation state.

### 11.1 Lanthanoid Contraction

The gradual decrease in atomic and ionic radii across the lanthanoid series is called lanthanoid contraction.

It occurs because the 4f electrons shield the increasing nuclear charge poorly.

Consequences include:

- Similar sizes of some 4d and 5d transition elements.
- Similar chemical properties among lanthanoids.
- Difficulty in separating individual lanthanoids.

### 11.2 Uses

Lanthanoids are used in:

- Permanent magnets.
- Phosphors and display technologies.
- Glass polishing.
- Specialised alloys.
- Optical and electronic materials.

## 12. Actinoids

Actinoids are associated with filling the 5f subshell.

They include actinium through lawrencium.

Important characteristics:

- Most are radioactive.
- They show several oxidation states.
- Their 5f orbitals can participate in bonding.
- Many are synthetic or occur only in trace quantities.

Uranium and plutonium are important in nuclear technology.

## 13. Common Mistakes and Exam Points

- Coordination number counts donor atoms, not the number of ligands.
- Oxidation state and coordination number are different.
- Ligands donate electron pairs to the central metal.
- Ambidentate ligands have two possible donor atoms.
- Cis and trans forms are geometrical isomers.
- Crystal field splitting depends on geometry and ligand strength.
- Strong-field ligands can favour low-spin complexes.
- Lanthanoid contraction results from poor shielding by 4f electrons.

## 14. Lesson Recap

- Coordination compounds contain a central metal and surrounding ligands.
- Ligands may be monodentate, bidentate, polydentate or ambidentate.
- Coordination compounds exhibit structural and stereoisomerism.
- Crystal field theory explains d-orbital splitting.
- Magnetic behaviour depends on unpaired electrons.
- Lanthanoids show contraction across the series.
- Actinoids are largely radioactive and exhibit variable oxidation states.
