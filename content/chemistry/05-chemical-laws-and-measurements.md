# Lesson 5: Chemical Laws and Measurements

Chemistry is a quantitative science. Chemical reactions involve measurable quantities of substances, and understanding these quantities is essential for predicting how much reactant is required and how much product can be formed.

The development of chemistry was strongly influenced by fundamental laws describing the relationships between the masses and volumes of reacting substances. These laws established the foundations of chemical calculations, atomic theory, and stoichiometry.

This lesson covers the major laws of chemical combination, SI units, scientific notation, significant figures, dimensional analysis, measurement accuracy, and basic numerical applications.

## 1. Laws of Chemical Combination

### 1.1 Introduction

The laws of chemical combination describe quantitative relationships between substances participating in chemical reactions.

The major laws include:

1. Law of conservation of mass.
2. Law of definite proportions.
3. Law of multiple proportions.
4. Law of reciprocal proportions.
5. Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes.
6. Avogadro's law.

## 2. Law of Conservation of Mass

### 2.1 Statement

The law of conservation of mass states that mass can neither be created nor destroyed during an ordinary chemical reaction in a closed system.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{Mass of reactants}=\text{Mass of products}}
\]

### 2.2 Explanation

During a chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged to form new substances.

The atoms are not ordinarily created or destroyed during the reaction. Therefore, the total mass remains constant.

### 2.3 Numerical Example

When 12 g of carbon reacts completely with 32 g of oxygen, calculate the mass of carbon dioxide formed.

Reaction:

\[
C+O_2\rightarrow CO_2
\]

Total mass of reactants:

\[
12+32=44\text{ g}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{m_{CO_2}=44\text{ g}}
\]

### 2.4 Applications

- Balancing chemical equations.
- Stoichiometric calculations.
- Industrial chemical production.
- Quantitative chemical analysis.

## 3. Law of Definite Proportions

### 3.1 Statement

The law of definite proportions states that a pure chemical compound always contains the same elements combined in a fixed proportion by mass, irrespective of its source or method of preparation.

It is also called the law of constant composition.

### 3.2 Example: Water

Water contains hydrogen and oxygen in a fixed mass ratio:

\[
H:O=1:8
\]

For every 1 g of hydrogen, 8 g of oxygen combine to form 9 g of water.

### 3.3 Numerical Example

Calculate the mass of oxygen present in 72 g of water.

The mass ratio is:

\[
H:O=1:8
\]

Total parts:

\[
1+8=9
\]

Mass of oxygen:

\[
m_O=\frac{8}{9}\times72
\]

\[
\boxed{m_O=64\text{ g}}
\]

Mass of hydrogen:

\[
m_H=\frac{1}{9}\times72
\]

\[
\boxed{m_H=8\text{ g}}
\]

## 4. Law of Multiple Proportions

### 4.1 Statement

When two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other element are in a simple whole-number ratio.

### 4.2 Example: Carbon Oxides

Carbon forms two common oxides:

- Carbon monoxide (CO).
- Carbon dioxide (CO₂).

For 12 g of carbon:

| Compound | Mass of carbon | Mass of oxygen |
|---|---:|---:|
| CO | 12 g | 16 g |
| CO₂ | 12 g | 32 g |

The oxygen masses are in the ratio:

\[
16:32=1:2
\]

This is a simple whole-number ratio.

## 5. Law of Reciprocal Proportions

### 5.1 Statement

When two elements separately combine with a fixed mass of a third element, the masses of the two elements are either in the same ratio or in a simple whole-number multiple of the ratio in which they combine with each other.

### 5.2 Explanation

This law compares the combining masses of three elements.

Suppose elements A and B separately combine with a fixed mass of element C.

The mass ratio of A and B in those compounds can be compared with the ratio in which A and B combine directly.

### 5.3 Importance

The law helped establish the quantitative consistency of chemical combination and contributed to the development of atomic theory.

## 6. Gay-Lussac's Law of Combining Volumes

### 6.1 Statement

When gases react, the volumes of reacting gases and gaseous products, measured at the same temperature and pressure, are in simple whole-number ratios.

### 6.2 Example

Hydrogen reacts with oxygen to form water vapour.

\[
2H_2(g)+O_2(g)\rightarrow2H_2O(g)
\]

The volume ratio is:

\[
\boxed{2:1:2}
\]

Thus, two volumes of hydrogen react with one volume of oxygen to produce two volumes of water vapour, provided all gases are measured under the same temperature and pressure conditions.

## 7. Avogadro's Law

### 7.1 Statement

Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.

### 7.2 Mathematical Expression

At constant temperature and pressure:

\[
V\propto n
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\frac{V}{n}=\text{constant}}
\]

Where:

- \(V\) = Volume.
- \(n\) = Number of moles.

### 7.3 Applications

- Understanding gaseous reactions.
- Establishing the relationship between gas volume and amount of substance.
- Developing the mole concept.
- Performing gas-volume calculations.

## 8. Introduction to Scientific Measurements

### 8.1 Physical Quantities

A physical quantity is a measurable property expressed using a numerical value and a unit.

Examples:
- Mass = 5 kg.
- Length = 2 m.
- Temperature = 300 K.
- Volume = 1 L.

Every measurement must include an appropriate unit.

### 8.2 International System of Units

The International System of Units is known as SI.

The seven SI base quantities are:

| Base quantity | SI unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Length | Metre | m |
| Mass | Kilogram | kg |
| Time | Second | s |
| Electric current | Ampere | A |
| Thermodynamic temperature | Kelvin | K |
| Amount of substance | Mole | mol |
| Luminous intensity | Candela | cd |

### 8.3 Important Derived Units

Derived units are formed by combining SI base units.

| Quantity | SI unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Area | Square metre | m² |
| Volume | Cubic metre | m³ |
| Density | Kilogram per cubic metre | kg/m³ |
| Force | Newton | N |
| Pressure | Pascal | Pa |
| Energy | Joule | J |
| Power | Watt | W |
| Electric charge | Coulomb | C |

Examples:

\[
1N=1kg\,m\,s^{-2}
\]

\[
1J=1kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}
\]

\[
1Pa=1kg\,m^{-1}\,s^{-2}
\]

## 9. Scientific Notation

### 9.1 Definition

Scientific notation expresses very large or very small numbers in the form:

\[
\boxed{a\times10^n}
\]

Where:

\[
1\leq |a|<10
\]

and \(n\) is an integer.

### 9.2 Examples

Large number:

\[
602000000000000000000000
\]

\[
=6.02\times10^{23}
\]

Small number:

\[
0.0000015
\]

\[
=1.5\times10^{-6}
\]

### 9.3 Importance in Chemistry

Scientific notation is particularly useful when working with:
- Atomic masses.
- Molecular dimensions.
- Avogadro's constant.
- Very small concentrations.
- Large numbers of particles.

## 10. Significant Figures

### 10.1 Definition

Significant figures are the meaningful digits in a measured value, including all certain digits and the first uncertain digit.

They indicate the precision of a measurement.

### 10.2 Rules for Counting Significant Figures

**Rule 1: All non-zero digits are significant.**

Example:

\[
245
\]

Contains three significant figures.

**Rule 2: Zeros between non-zero digits are significant.**

Example:

\[
2005
\]

Contains four significant figures.

**Rule 3: Leading zeros are not significant.**

Example:

\[
0.0045
\]

Contains two significant figures.

**Rule 4: Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant.**

Example:

\[
2.500
\]

Contains four significant figures.

**Rule 5: Trailing zeros in a whole number without a decimal point may be ambiguous.**

Example:

\[
1500
\]

Its significant figures cannot always be determined without additional notation.

Scientific notation removes this ambiguity:

\[
1.5\times10^3
\]

contains two significant figures.

\[
1.500\times10^3
\]

contains four significant figures.

### 10.3 Significant Figures in Calculations

**Multiplication and Division**

The final answer should generally contain the same number of significant figures as the measured quantity with the fewest significant figures.

Example:

\[
2.5\times3.42=8.55
\]

The result is reported to two significant figures:

\[
\boxed{8.6}
\]

**Addition and Subtraction**

The final answer should generally have the same number of decimal places as the measurement with the fewest decimal places.

Example:

\[
12.35+4.2=16.55
\]

Rounded to one decimal place:

\[
\boxed{16.6}
\]

### 10.4 Rounding Off

When rounding:
- If the first discarded digit is less than 5, retain the preceding digit.
- If it is greater than 5, increase the preceding digit by one.
- If it is exactly 5, the rounding convention used by the question or laboratory should be followed.

## 11. Accuracy, Precision, and Errors

### 11.1 Accuracy

Accuracy describes how close a measured value is to the true or accepted value.

### 11.2 Precision

Precision describes how close repeated measurements are to one another.

A measurement may be precise without being accurate.

### 11.3 Types of Experimental Errors

**Systematic Errors**

These errors consistently shift measurements in one direction.

Causes:
- Incorrect instrument calibration.
- Zero error.
- Faulty measurement method.
- Consistent environmental effects.

**Random Errors**

These errors vary unpredictably between measurements.

Causes:
- Small fluctuations in conditions.
- Observer variations.
- Instrument limitations.

**Gross Errors**

These arise from major mistakes such as incorrect readings, recording errors, or improper experimental procedures.

### 11.4 Percentage Error

Percentage error is calculated using:

\[
\boxed{\%\text{ error}=
\frac{|\text{Measured value}-\text{Accepted value}|}
{\text{Accepted value}}\times100}
\]

Example:

A measured mass is 9.8 g, while the accepted value is 10.0 g.

\[
\%\text{ error}=
\frac{|9.8-10.0|}{10.0}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{2\%}
\]

## 12. Dimensional Analysis and Unit Conversion

### 12.1 Meaning

Dimensional analysis uses the relationships between physical quantities and their units to convert measurements and check equations.

### 12.2 Example: Converting Grams to Kilograms

Convert 750 g into kilograms.

\[
1\text{ kg}=1000\text{ g}
\]

\[
750\text{ g}\times\frac{1\text{ kg}}{1000\text{ g}}
\]

\[
\boxed{0.75\text{ kg}}
\]

### 12.3 Example: Converting Cubic Centimetres to Millilitres

\[
1\text{ cm}^3=1\text{ mL}
\]

Therefore:

\[
250\text{ cm}^3=250\text{ mL}
\]

### 12.4 Temperature Scales

The Kelvin and Celsius scales are related by:

\[
\boxed{T(K)=t(^{\circ}C)+273.15}
\]

For example:

\[
25^{\circ}C=25+273.15
\]

\[
\boxed{298.15K}
\]

## 13. Common Exam Traps

1. Conservation of mass applies to ordinary chemical reactions in a closed system.
2. The law of definite proportions concerns fixed mass ratios in pure compounds.
3. The law of multiple proportions compares different compounds formed by the same two elements.
4. Gay-Lussac's law compares gas volumes at the same temperature and pressure.
5. Avogadro's law relates gas volume to the number of moles at constant temperature and pressure.
6. The SI unit of mass is kilogram, not gram.
7. The SI unit of amount of substance is mole.
8. Leading zeros are not significant figures.
9. Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant.
10. Precision and accuracy describe different aspects of measurement quality.
11. Temperature in thermodynamic equations is generally expressed in kelvin.
12. Unit conversion factors must be arranged so unwanted units cancel.
