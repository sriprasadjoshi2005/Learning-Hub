---
title: Molecular Orbital Theory
level: intermediate
---

Valence bond theory cannot explain why $\mathrm{O_2}$ is attracted to a magnet, or why $\mathrm{B_2}$ exists at all. **Molecular orbital (MO) theory** treats electrons as belonging to the whole molecule. Atomic orbitals combine (the LCAO method), and $n$ atomic orbitals always produce $n$ molecular orbitals:

- **Bonding MO:** waves add in phase. Electron density builds between the nuclei, and the energy is lower than the separate atoms.
- **Antibonding MO** (marked $*$): waves cancel. There is a node between the nuclei and the energy is higher.

Electrons fill MOs by the Aufbau principle, Pauli exclusion and Hund's rule, exactly as in atoms.

## Energy ordering

For $\mathrm{O_2}$, $\mathrm{F_2}$ and heavier diatomics:

$$\sigma_{2s} < \sigma^*_{2s} < \sigma_{2p_z} < \pi_{2p_x} = \pi_{2p_y} < \pi^*_{2p_x} = \pi^*_{2p_y} < \sigma^*_{2p_z}$$

For $\mathrm{Li_2}$ to $\mathrm{N_2}$, $2s$ and $2p$ orbitals are close in energy and mix, which pushes $\sigma_{2p_z}$ **above** the $\pi_{2p}$ pair. Using the wrong order for $\mathrm{B_2}$, $\mathrm{C_2}$ or $\mathrm{N_2}$ is a standard exam trap.

## Bond order and properties

$$\text{bond order} = \tfrac12\,(N_b - N_a)$$

where $N_b$ and $N_a$ are the electrons in bonding and antibonding MOs. A higher bond order means a **shorter, stronger** bond. A bond order of zero means the molecule does not form.

A species with any unpaired electron is **paramagnetic**; one with all electrons paired is **diamagnetic**.

| Species | Valence electrons | Bond order | Magnetism |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathrm{B_2}$ | 6 | 1 | Paramagnetic (two unpaired $\pi$) |
| $\mathrm{C_2}$ | 8 | 2 | Diamagnetic |
| $\mathrm{N_2}$ | 10 | 3 | Diamagnetic |
| $\mathrm{O_2}$ | 12 | 2 | Paramagnetic (two unpaired $\pi^*$) |

## Ions

Removing an electron from a bonding MO lowers the bond order; removing one from an antibonding MO raises it. So $\mathrm{N_2^+}$ is weaker than $\mathrm{N_2}$, but $\mathrm{O_2^+}$ has a **higher** bond order (2.5) than $\mathrm{O_2}$, because the electron removed came from $\pi^*$.

## Quiz

1. Which of the following species has the longest bond length?
   - [x] $\mathrm{O_2^{2-}}$
   - [ ] $\mathrm{O_2^+}$
   - [ ] $\mathrm{O_2}$
   - [ ] $\mathrm{O_2^-}$
   > Bond orders are $\mathrm{O_2^+}$ 2.5, $\mathrm{O_2}$ 2, $\mathrm{O_2^-}$ 1.5 and $\mathrm{O_2^{2-}}$ 1. The extra electrons enter antibonding $\pi^*$ orbitals, so the bond weakens and lengthens.

2. Which of the following is paramagnetic?
   - [ ] $\mathrm{N_2}$
   - [ ] $\mathrm{C_2}$
   - [ ] $\mathrm{O_2^{2-}}$
   - [x] $\mathrm{B_2}$
   > $\mathrm{B_2}$ has its last two electrons in the degenerate $\pi_{2p}$ pair, one in each by Hund's rule, so two are unpaired. $\mathrm{N_2}$, $\mathrm{C_2}$ and $\mathrm{O_2^{2-}}$ have all electrons paired.

3. How does the bond order change on going from $\mathrm{N_2}$ to $\mathrm{N_2^+}$, and from $\mathrm{O_2}$ to $\mathrm{O_2^+}$, respectively?
   - [ ] Increases, increases
   - [ ] Decreases, decreases
   - [x] Decreases, increases
   - [ ] Increases, decreases
   > In $\mathrm{N_2}$ the electron lost comes from a bonding orbital, so the bond order falls from 3 to 2.5. In $\mathrm{O_2}$ the electron lost comes from an antibonding $\pi^*$ orbital, so the bond order rises from 2 to 2.5.
