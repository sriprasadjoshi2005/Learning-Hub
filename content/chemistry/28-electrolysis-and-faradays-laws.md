# Lesson 28: Electrolysis and Faraday's Laws

Electrolysis uses electrical energy to drive non-spontaneous chemical reactions. It is widely used in metal extraction, electroplating, purification, and industrial chemical production. Faraday's laws provide quantitative relationships between the amount of electricity passed through an electrolyte and the amount of substance produced or consumed at the electrodes.

## 1. Introduction to Electrolysis

Electrolysis is the chemical decomposition of an electrolyte by passing an electric current through its molten or aqueous form.

An electrolytic cell contains:
- An electrolyte.
- Two electrodes.
- An external direct-current power source.

The power source drives a non-spontaneous redox reaction.

## 2. Electrodes in an Electrolytic Cell

The anode is the electrode where oxidation occurs.

The cathode is the electrode where reduction occurs.

In an electrolytic cell:
- Anode is positive.
- Cathode is negative.

The electrode definitions do not change between galvanic and electrolytic cells.

Only their signs differ.

## 3. Movement of Ions

During electrolysis:
- Cations move towards the cathode.
- Anions move towards the anode.

At the cathode, cations may gain electrons.

At the anode, anions or other species may lose electrons.

## 4. Electrolysis of Molten Salts

Molten ionic compounds contain mobile ions without water being present.

Example: molten sodium chloride.

At the cathode:

\[
Na^++e^-\rightarrow Na
\]

At the anode:

\[
2Cl^-\rightarrow Cl_2+2e^-
\]

Overall:

\[
2NaCl(l)\rightarrow2Na(l)+Cl_2(g)
\]

## 5. Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions

In aqueous electrolysis, water may compete with dissolved ions for discharge at the electrodes.

The products depend on:
- Electrode potentials.
- Concentration of ions.
- Nature of the electrodes.
- pH and solution composition.
- Kinetic factors and overpotential.

### 5.1 Electrolysis of Aqueous Sodium Chloride

In concentrated aqueous sodium chloride with suitable inert electrodes:

At the cathode:

\[
2H_2O+2e^-\rightarrow H_2+2OH^-
\]

At the anode, chloride oxidation can occur:

\[
2Cl^-\rightarrow Cl_2+2e^-
\]

Overall:

\[
2NaCl+2H_2O\rightarrow2NaOH+H_2+Cl_2
\]

This is the basis of the chlor-alkali process.

### 5.2 Electrolysis of Copper Sulphate

Using inert electrodes, copper ions are reduced at the cathode:

\[
Cu^{2+}+2e^-\rightarrow Cu
\]

At the anode, water is oxidised under typical conditions:

\[
2H_2O\rightarrow O_2+4H^++4e^-
\]

Using copper electrodes instead changes the anode reaction:

\[
Cu\rightarrow Cu^{2+}+2e^-
\]

The choice of electrode therefore affects the products.

## 6. Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis

Faraday's laws relate the amount of chemical change to the electrical charge passed through an electrolyte.

### 6.1 Faraday's First Law

The mass of a substance deposited or liberated during electrolysis is directly proportional to the total electric charge passed.

\[
\boxed{m\propto Q}
\]

Since:

\[
Q=It
\]

\[
\boxed{m=ZIt}
\]

Where:
- m = mass deposited.
- Z = electrochemical equivalent.
- I = current.
- t = time.

### 6.2 Faraday's Second Law

When the same quantity of electricity passes through different electrolytes, the masses of substances deposited are proportional to their equivalent masses.

\[
\boxed{\frac{m_1}{m_2}=\frac{E_1}{E_2}}
\]

Here, E represents equivalent mass.

## 7. Quantitative Electrolysis

One mole of electrons carries one Faraday of charge.

\[
\boxed{F\approx96485\text{ C mol}^{-1}}
\]

The amount of electrons transferred is:

\[
n(e^-)=\frac{Q}{F}
\]

If the electrode reaction requires z electrons per ion:

\[
M^{z+}+ze^-\rightarrow M
\]

The amount of metal deposited is:

\[
n(M)=\frac{Q}{zF}
\]

The mass deposited is:

\[
\boxed{m=\frac{MIt}{zF}}
\]

Where M is the molar mass.

## 8. Worked Example

A current of 2 A is passed through a solution containing Cu²⁺ ions for 30 minutes. Calculate the mass of copper deposited.

Given:

\[
I=2\text{ A}
\]

\[
t=30\times60=1800\text{ s}
\]

\[
M_{Cu}=63.5\text{ g mol}^{-1}
\]

\[
z=2
\]

Using:

\[
m=\frac{MIt}{zF}
\]

\[
m=\frac{63.5\times2\times1800}{2\times96485}
\]

\[
\boxed{m\approx1.18\text{ g}}
\]

## 9. Applications of Electrolysis

### 9.1 Electroplating

Electroplating deposits a thin layer of one metal onto another material.

Applications:
- Corrosion protection.
- Decorative finishes.
- Improved surface properties.
- Electrical contacts.

### 9.2 Electrorefining

Electrorefining purifies metals using electrolysis.

For copper refining:
- Impure copper acts as the anode.
- Pure copper is deposited at the cathode.
- Less reactive impurities may collect as anode sludge.

### 9.3 Metal Extraction

Electrolysis is used to extract highly reactive metals such as aluminium from molten ionic compounds.

### 9.4 Industrial Chemicals

Electrolysis is used in the production of:
- Chlorine.
- Hydrogen.
- Sodium hydroxide.
- Aluminium.
- Other industrial materials.

## 10. Common Exam Traps

- Oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode.
- In an electrolytic cell, the anode is positive and the cathode is negative.
- Cations move towards the cathode.
- Anions move towards the anode.
- Aqueous solutions may produce products involving water.
- Electrode material can change the electrode reactions.
- Time must be converted into seconds when current is expressed in amperes.
- The number of electrons required per ion must be included in Faraday's equation.
