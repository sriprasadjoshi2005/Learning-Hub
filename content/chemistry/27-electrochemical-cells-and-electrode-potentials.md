# Lesson 27: Electrochemical Cells and Electrode Potentials

Electrochemistry studies the relationship between chemical reactions and electrical energy. Electrochemical cells use redox reactions to convert chemical energy into electrical energy or use electrical energy to drive chemical changes. Their operation depends on electron transfer, electrode reactions, ion movement, and electrode potentials.

## 1. Introduction to Electrochemical Cells

An electrochemical cell is a system in which oxidation and reduction reactions are coupled.

There are two major types:

1. Galvanic or voltaic cells.
2. Electrolytic cells.

A galvanic cell converts chemical energy into electrical energy through a spontaneous redox reaction.

An electrolytic cell uses electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous chemical reaction.

## 2. Galvanic Cells

A galvanic cell consists of two half-cells connected through an external conducting wire and an ionic pathway.

Each half-cell contains an electrode in contact with an electrolyte.

### 2.1 Daniell Cell

The Daniell cell uses zinc and copper electrodes.

Overall reaction:

\[
Zn(s)+Cu^{2+}(aq)\rightarrow Zn^{2+}(aq)+Cu(s)
\]

Oxidation at the zinc electrode:

\[
Zn\rightarrow Zn^{2+}+2e^-
\]

Reduction at the copper electrode:

\[
Cu^{2+}+2e^-\rightarrow Cu
\]

### 2.2 Anode and Cathode

The anode is the electrode where oxidation occurs.

The cathode is the electrode where reduction occurs.

In a galvanic cell:
- Anode is negative.
- Cathode is positive.

Remember:

**An Ox, Red Cat** means oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction occurs at the cathode.

## 3. Salt Bridge

A salt bridge connects the two half-cells and permits ion migration.

Its functions include:
- Maintaining electrical neutrality.
- Completing the ionic circuit.
- Preventing rapid charge accumulation.
- Reducing direct mixing of the electrolytes.

In a typical Daniell cell:
- Anions migrate towards the anode compartment.
- Cations migrate towards the cathode compartment.

## 4. Cell Representation

A galvanic cell can be represented using conventional cell notation.

For the Daniell cell:

\[
Zn(s)|Zn^{2+}(aq)||Cu^{2+}(aq)|Cu(s)
\]

Conventions:
- A single vertical line represents a phase boundary.
- A double vertical line represents the salt bridge.
- The anode is written on the left.
- The cathode is written on the right.

## 5. Electrode Potential

Electrode potential measures the tendency of an electrode reaction to occur in a particular direction.

Absolute electrode potentials cannot be measured independently. They are measured relative to a reference electrode.

### 5.1 Standard Hydrogen Electrode

The standard hydrogen electrode is the primary reference electrode.

Its standard electrode potential is defined as:

\[
\boxed{E^\circ=0.00\text{ V}}
\]

It consists of:
- A platinum electrode.
- Hydrogen gas at standard pressure.
- An aqueous solution with hydrogen-ion activity equal to unity.

The conventional standard conditions are defined using specified standard-state activities and gas pressure.

### 5.2 Standard Reduction Potential

Standard reduction potential is the electrode potential measured under standard-state conditions relative to the standard hydrogen electrode.

A more positive reduction potential indicates a greater tendency for reduction under the specified conditions.

## 6. Standard Cell Potential

For a galvanic cell:

\[
\boxed{E^\circ_{\text{cell}}=E^\circ_{\text{cathode}}-E^\circ_{\text{anode}}}
\]

Both values are taken from standard reduction-potential tables.

A positive standard cell potential indicates a spontaneous cell reaction under standard-state conditions.

### 6.1 Worked Example: Daniell Cell

Given:

\[
E^\circ_{Cu^{2+}/Cu}=+0.34\text{ V}
\]

\[
E^\circ_{Zn^{2+}/Zn}=-0.76\text{ V}
\]

Then:

\[
E^\circ_{\text{cell}}=0.34-(-0.76)
\]

\[
\boxed{E^\circ_{\text{cell}}=1.10\text{ V}}
\]

## 7. Relationship Between Cell Potential and Gibbs Energy

The maximum electrical work obtainable from a reversible cell is related to the Gibbs free-energy change.

\[
\boxed{\Delta G=-nFE_{\text{cell}}}
\]

Under standard conditions:

\[
\boxed{\Delta G^\circ=-nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}}}
\]

Where:
- n = number of electrons transferred.
- F = Faraday constant, approximately \(96485\text{ C mol}^{-1}\).
- E = cell potential.

A positive cell potential corresponds to a negative Gibbs free-energy change for the reaction as written.

## 8. Nernst Equation

Electrode potential changes when concentrations or activities differ from standard conditions.

For a cell reaction:

\[
aA+bB\rightleftharpoons cC+dD
\]

The Nernst equation is:

\[
\boxed{E=E^\circ-\frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q}
\]

At 298 K:

\[
\boxed{E=E^\circ-\frac{0.0591}{n}\log Q}
\]

Here, Q is the reaction quotient written for the balanced cell reaction.

## 9. Electrochemical Series

The electrochemical series arranges electrode couples according to their standard reduction potentials.

It helps predict:
- Relative oxidising strengths.
- Relative reducing strengths.
- Direction of redox reactions.
- Standard cell potentials.
- Whether certain displacement reactions are thermodynamically favourable.

Species with more positive reduction potentials generally act as stronger oxidising agents.

Species with more negative reduction potentials generally act as stronger reducing agents in their reduced forms.

## 10. Batteries and Fuel Cells

### 10.1 Primary Batteries

Primary batteries are designed for use until their reactants are substantially consumed.

Example: alkaline batteries.

### 10.2 Secondary Batteries

Secondary batteries can be recharged by driving the cell reaction in the reverse direction.

Examples:
- Lead-acid batteries.
- Lithium-ion batteries.
- Nickel-metal hydride batteries.

### 10.3 Fuel Cells

Fuel cells convert the chemical energy of continuously supplied fuel and oxidant into electrical energy.

A hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell produces water as its overall product.

\[
2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O
\]

## 11. Common Exam Traps

- Oxidation always occurs at the anode.
- Reduction always occurs at the cathode.
- Anode and cathode signs depend on the type of cell.
- In a galvanic cell, the anode is negative and the cathode is positive.
- Standard cell potential is cathode reduction potential minus anode reduction potential.
- A positive cell potential corresponds to a negative Gibbs free-energy change.
- The salt bridge completes the ionic circuit; electrons travel through the external wire.
