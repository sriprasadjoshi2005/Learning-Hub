# Lesson 13: Ionic, Covalent, and Coordinate Bonding

Chemical bonds are attractive forces that hold atoms or ions together in substances. Atoms form bonds to reach more stable electronic arrangements and lower their overall potential energy. The three important types of chemical bonding are ionic bonding, covalent bonding, and coordinate covalent bonding.

Ionic bonding involves the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. Covalent bonding involves the sharing of electron pairs between atoms. Coordinate bonding is a type of covalent bonding in which both electrons in a shared pair are initially contributed by the same atom. Understanding these bonds helps explain the structures, properties, and reactions of chemical substances.

## 1. Ionic Bonding

### 1.1 Definition of Ionic Bonding

Ionic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between positively charged ions (cations) and negatively charged ions (anions).

It generally forms when electrons are transferred from one atom to another.

- The atom losing electrons becomes a positively charged cation.
- The atom gaining electrons becomes a negatively charged anion.
- Oppositely charged ions attract each other through electrostatic forces.

Ionic compounds are commonly formed between metals and non-metals.

### 1.2 Formation of Sodium Chloride (NaCl)

Sodium has the electronic configuration:

\[
Na: 1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^1
\]

It has one valence electron and tends to lose it.

\[
Na\rightarrow Na^++e^-
\]

The sodium ion has the stable electronic configuration of neon.

Chlorine has the electronic configuration:

\[
Cl: 1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^5
\]

It requires one additional electron to complete its octet.

\[
Cl+e^-\rightarrow Cl^-
\]

The chloride ion achieves the stable electronic configuration of argon.

The oppositely charged ions attract each other:

\[
Na^++Cl^-\rightarrow NaCl
\]

The formation can be represented schematically as:

```text
Na  →  Na⁺ + e⁻

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl
```

The resulting compound is electrically neutral because the positive and negative charges cancel.

### 1.3 Formation of Magnesium Oxide (MgO)

Magnesium has two valence electrons and tends to lose both.

\[
Mg\rightarrow Mg^{2+}+2e^-
\]

Oxygen requires two electrons to complete its octet.

\[
O+2e^-\rightarrow O^{2-}
\]

The ions attract each other:

\[
Mg^{2+}+O^{2-}\rightarrow MgO
\]

The charge ratio is 1:1 because one magnesium ion carries a charge of +2 and one oxide ion carries a charge of −2.

### 1.4 Formation of Calcium Chloride (CaCl₂)

Calcium loses two electrons:

\[
Ca\rightarrow Ca^{2+}+2e^-
\]

Each chlorine atom gains one electron:

\[
2Cl+2e^-\rightarrow 2Cl^-
\]

The resulting ions combine in the ratio:

\[
Ca^{2+}+2Cl^-\rightarrow CaCl_2
\]

Two chloride ions are required to balance the +2 charge of one calcium ion.

## 2. Lattice Structure and Lattice Enthalpy

### 2.1 Ionic Crystal Lattice

Ionic compounds generally exist as extended three-dimensional arrangements of positive and negative ions.

This arrangement is called an ionic crystal lattice.

In a crystal lattice:

- Each cation is surrounded by anions.
- Each anion is surrounded by cations.
- Electrostatic attractions act throughout the structure.
- The compound does not normally consist of separate NaCl molecules.

For example, solid sodium chloride has a repeating arrangement of sodium and chloride ions.

<text></text>

### 2.2 Lattice Enthalpy

Lattice enthalpy is associated with the formation or separation of an ionic solid from its gaseous ions.

Two sign conventions are commonly used.

**Lattice formation enthalpy:** The enthalpy change when gaseous ions combine to form one mole of an ionic solid. It is usually negative.

\[
M^+(g)+X^-(g)\rightarrow MX(s)
\]

**Lattice dissociation enthalpy:** The enthalpy required to separate one mole of an ionic solid into its gaseous ions. It is positive.

\[
MX(s)\rightarrow M^+(g)+X^-(g)
\]

The magnitude of lattice enthalpy generally increases with stronger electrostatic attraction.

### 2.3 Factors Affecting Lattice Enthalpy

The strength of electrostatic attraction depends mainly on ionic charges and the distance between ion centres.

A simplified relationship is:

\[
|E|\propto\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r}
\]

Where:

- \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) are the ionic charges.
- \(r\) is the distance between the ion centres.

Therefore:

1. Higher ionic charges generally produce stronger attractions.
2. Smaller ionic radii generally produce stronger attractions when charges are comparable.

For example, MgO generally has a much greater lattice enthalpy magnitude than NaCl because Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ have larger charge magnitudes than Na⁺ and Cl⁻.

## 3. Properties of Ionic Compounds

### 3.1 High Melting and Boiling Points

Ionic compounds generally have high melting and boiling points because considerable energy is needed to overcome the strong electrostatic attractions in their crystal lattices.

### 3.2 Electrical Conductivity

Ionic compounds conduct electricity when their ions are free to move.

- In the solid state, ions occupy fixed positions, so ionic solids generally do not conduct electricity.
- In the molten state, ions become mobile and can carry electric charge.
- In aqueous solution, dissolved ions can move and conduct electricity.

### 3.3 Brittleness

Ionic crystals are generally brittle.

When layers of ions shift under an applied force, ions with the same charge can become adjacent. Their electrostatic repulsion can cause the crystal to fracture.

### 3.4 Solubility

Many ionic compounds dissolve in polar solvents such as water.

However, not all ionic compounds are soluble in water. Solubility depends on the balance between lattice enthalpy and the energy released or absorbed during solvation.

### 3.5 Summary of Ionic Properties

| Property | Typical behaviour |
|---|---|
| Physical state | Usually crystalline solids |
| Melting point | Generally high |
| Electrical conductivity in solid state | Usually poor |
| Conductivity when molten | Conducts |
| Conductivity in aqueous solution | Conducts if soluble |
| Mechanical behaviour | Generally brittle |
| Solubility | Often soluble in polar solvents, but exceptions exist |

## 4. Covalent Bonding

### 4.1 Definition of Covalent Bonding

A covalent bond is formed when two atoms share one or more pairs of electrons.

Covalent bonding commonly occurs between non-metal atoms.

Each atom contributes electrons to the shared pair, allowing the atoms to achieve more stable electronic arrangements.

Examples include:

- Hydrogen (H₂).
- Oxygen (O₂).
- Nitrogen (N₂).
- Water (H₂O).
- Methane (CH₄).

### 4.2 Formation of Hydrogen Molecule

Each hydrogen atom has one valence electron.

When two hydrogen atoms approach each other, they share an electron pair.

\[
H\cdot+\cdot H\rightarrow H:H
\]

The shared pair can also be represented using a line:

```text
H—H
```

Each hydrogen achieves two electrons in its first shell.

### 4.3 Formation of Chlorine Molecule

Each chlorine atom has seven valence electrons.

Two chlorine atoms share one pair of electrons.

```text
..       ..
:Cl—Cl:
..       ..
```

Each chlorine atom achieves an octet.

### 4.4 Single Covalent Bond

A single covalent bond consists of one shared pair of electrons.

Examples:

\[
H-H
\]

\[
Cl-Cl
\]

\[
C-H
\]

### 4.5 Double Covalent Bond

A double covalent bond consists of two shared pairs of electrons.

For example, oxygen molecule contains a double bond.

\[
O=O
\]

Each oxygen atom shares two electron pairs with the other oxygen atom.

### 4.6 Triple Covalent Bond

A triple covalent bond consists of three shared pairs of electrons.

For example, nitrogen molecule contains a triple bond.

\[
N\equiv N
\]

Each nitrogen atom shares three electron pairs.

### 4.7 Comparison of Covalent Bonds

| Bond type | Shared electron pairs | Example |
|---|---:|---|
| Single | 1 | H₂ |
| Double | 2 | O₂ |
| Triple | 3 | N₂ |

For comparable bonds between the same atoms, increasing bond order generally produces a shorter and stronger bond.

## 5. Types of Covalent Bonds Based on Polarity

### 5.1 Non-Polar Covalent Bond

A non-polar covalent bond occurs when the bonding electrons are shared equally or nearly equally between two atoms.

This commonly occurs between identical atoms because they have the same electronegativity.

Examples:

\[
H-H
\]

\[
Cl-Cl
\]

The bonding electron density is distributed symmetrically.

### 5.2 Polar Covalent Bond

A polar covalent bond occurs when electrons are shared unequally between two atoms because of a difference in electronegativity.

The more electronegative atom attracts the shared electrons more strongly and develops a partial negative charge.

The other atom develops a partial positive charge.

For hydrogen chloride:

\[
H^{\delta+}-Cl^{\delta-}
\]

The symbol \(\delta\) represents a partial charge rather than a complete ionic charge.

### 5.3 Electronegativity Difference

Electronegativity is the tendency of an atom in a chemical bond to attract shared electrons.

The electronegativity difference is:

\[
\Delta\chi=|\chi_A-\chi_B|
\]

A larger electronegativity difference generally indicates greater bond polarity.

However, there is no universal sharp boundary separating ionic and covalent bonding. Bonding exists along a continuum.

## 6. Coordinate Covalent Bonding

### 6.1 Definition

A coordinate covalent bond, also called a dative bond, is a covalent bond in which both electrons in the shared pair are initially contributed by the same atom.

The atom donating the electron pair is called the donor.

The atom accepting the electron pair is called the acceptor.

The donor must possess an available lone pair, while the acceptor must have an orbital capable of accepting that pair.

### 6.2 Formation of Ammonium Ion (NH₄⁺)

Ammonia contains a lone pair of electrons on nitrogen.

Hydrogen ion (H⁺) has no electrons and can accept an electron pair.

Nitrogen donates its lone pair to H⁺, forming an additional N—H bond.

\[
NH_3+H^+\rightarrow NH_4^+
\]

The formation can be represented using an arrow:

```text
    H
    |
H—N:  +  H⁺  →  [NH₄]⁺
    |
    H
```

The arrow indicates the direction of electron-pair donation.

After the bond forms, the four N—H bonds in ammonium are equivalent. The coordinate-bond notation describes how the bond originated, not a permanently different type of N—H bond.

### 6.3 Formation of Hydronium Ion (H₃O⁺)

Water contains two lone pairs on oxygen.

A hydrogen ion can accept one of these lone pairs.

\[
H_2O+H^+\rightarrow H_3O^+
\]

Oxygen donates an electron pair to the hydrogen ion, forming a coordinate covalent bond.

### 6.4 Formation of BF₃·NH₃

Boron trifluoride has an electron-deficient boron atom.

Ammonia contains a lone pair on nitrogen.

Nitrogen donates its lone pair to boron:

\[
BF_3+NH_3\rightarrow F_3B\leftarrow NH_3
\]

The nitrogen acts as the electron-pair donor, while boron acts as the electron-pair acceptor.

## 7. Lewis Acids and Lewis Bases

### 7.1 Lewis Acid

A Lewis acid is a species that accepts an electron pair.

Examples:

- H⁺.
- BF₃.
- AlCl₃.
- Metal cations such as Ag⁺.

### 7.2 Lewis Base

A Lewis base is a species that donates an electron pair.

Examples:

- NH₃.
- H₂O.
- OH⁻.
- Cl⁻.

### 7.3 Lewis Acid-Base Reaction

A Lewis acid-base reaction involves the donation of an electron pair from a Lewis base to a Lewis acid.

For example:

\[
BF_3+NH_3\rightarrow F_3B\leftarrow NH_3
\]

Here:

- NH₃ is the Lewis base.
- BF₃ is the Lewis acid.

Coordinate covalent bond formation is closely connected to the Lewis acid-base concept.

## 8. Comparison of Ionic, Covalent, and Coordinate Bonds

| Feature | Ionic bond | Covalent bond | Coordinate covalent bond |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main process | Electrostatic attraction between ions | Sharing of electrons | Sharing of an electron pair initially donated by one atom |
| Electron transfer | Often involved in forming ions | No complete transfer required | Both bonding electrons initially come from one donor |
| Common elements | Metal and non-metal | Usually non-metals | Donor with a lone pair and an electron-pair acceptor |
| Directionality | Generally non-directional electrostatic attraction | Directional | Directional |
| Example | NaCl | H₂ | NH₄⁺ formation |

## 9. Ionic and Covalent Character

### 9.1 Bonding as a Continuum

Ionic and covalent bonding are useful categories, but real chemical bonds can contain characteristics of both.

A bond with unequal electron sharing may have partial ionic character while remaining covalent.

The electronegativity difference provides a useful indication of bond polarity, but it should not be treated as an absolute classification rule.

### 9.2 Polarization and Fajans' Rules

Fajans' rules help explain why some ionic compounds show significant covalent character.

Polarization occurs when a cation distorts the electron cloud of an anion.

Greater distortion generally increases covalent character.

The main factors are:

**1. Small cation size**

A small cation has high charge density and can strongly distort an anion's electron cloud.

**2. High cation charge**

A cation with a larger positive charge generally has greater polarizing power.

**3. Large anion size**

A large anion has a more easily distorted electron cloud and greater polarizability.

**4. High anion charge**

Anions with greater negative charge are generally more easily polarized.

For example, aluminium chloride shows substantial covalent character because the small, highly charged Al³⁺ ion strongly polarizes chloride ions.

## 10. Applications of Chemical Bonding

### 10.1 Materials and Construction

Ionic and covalent bonding influence the hardness, brittleness, melting points, and mechanical properties of materials.

### 10.2 Biological Molecules

Covalent bonds hold atoms together in carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids.

### 10.3 Electrical Applications

Ionic compounds conduct electricity when molten or dissolved, making them important in electrolytes and electrochemical systems.

### 10.4 Coordination Chemistry

Coordinate bonding is important in metal complexes, catalysts, and biological systems.

### 10.5 Chemical Reactions

Understanding electron-pair donation and acceptance helps explain acid-base reactions, molecular interactions, and chemical reactivity.

## 11. Common Exam Traps

1. **Ionic compounds are not normally collections of separate molecules.** They form extended ionic lattices.
2. **Solid ionic compounds generally do not conduct electricity.** Their ions are fixed in the lattice.
3. **Covalent bonding does not always mean equal sharing.** Polar covalent bonds involve unequal sharing.
4. **A coordinate bond is still a covalent bond.** Its distinguishing feature is the original source of the shared electron pair.
5. **Coordinate bonds are not necessarily permanently different after formation.** They may become equivalent to other bonds in the molecule.
6. **Electronegativity difference does not provide an absolute ionic-covalent boundary.**
7. **A Lewis acid accepts an electron pair, while a Lewis base donates one.**
