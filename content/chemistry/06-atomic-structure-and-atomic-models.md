# Lesson 6: Atomic Structure and Atomic Models

All chemical substances are composed of atoms. The properties of elements, the formation of compounds, and the behaviour of chemical reactions can be understood by studying the internal structure of atoms.

The concept of the atom developed through several scientific experiments and theoretical models. Early philosophers proposed that matter consisted of indivisible particles, but experimental discoveries eventually revealed that atoms contain smaller subatomic particles.

This lesson introduces the development of atomic theory, the discovery of electrons, protons, and neutrons, the structure of the nucleus, atomic number, mass number, isotopes, and the major atomic models proposed by scientists.

## 1. Development of Atomic Theory

### 1.1 Early Concept of Atoms

The idea that matter consists of extremely small particles can be traced to ancient Greek philosophers.

Democritus proposed that matter could be divided into smaller and smaller pieces until indivisible particles were reached. He called these particles *atomos*.

However, this was a philosophical proposal rather than an experimentally established scientific theory.

### 1.2 Dalton's Atomic Theory

John Dalton proposed his atomic theory in the early nineteenth century based on experimental observations and the laws of chemical combination.

**Main postulates:**

1. All matter is composed of tiny particles called atoms.
2. Atoms were considered indivisible in Dalton's original model.
3. All atoms of a particular element were considered identical in mass and properties.
4. Atoms of different elements have different properties.
5. Compounds form when atoms of different elements combine in simple whole-number ratios.
6. Chemical reactions involve rearrangement of atoms.
7. Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in ordinary chemical reactions.

### 1.3 Limitations of Dalton's Theory

Dalton's theory was an important foundation of modern chemistry, but later discoveries revealed several limitations.

- Atoms are divisible into subatomic particles.
- Atoms of the same element may have different masses because of isotopes.
- Atoms of different elements may have the same mass number because of isobars.
- The theory does not explain the internal structure of atoms.
- It does not explain the nature of chemical bonding.

## 2. Discovery of Subatomic Particles

Experiments involving electrical discharge, radioactive substances, and atomic radiation led to the discovery of subatomic particles.

The three principal subatomic particles are:

1. Electron.
2. Proton.
3. Neutron.

### 2.1 Discovery of the Electron

The electron was discovered by J. J. Thomson through experiments using cathode ray tubes.

**Cathode Ray Experiment**

A cathode ray tube contains a gas at low pressure and two electrodes connected to a high-voltage source.

When a sufficiently high potential difference is applied, rays emerge from the cathode and travel toward the anode.

These rays are called cathode rays.

**Observations:**

- Cathode rays travel in straight lines.
- They are deflected by electric fields.
- They are deflected by magnetic fields.
- Their behaviour indicates that they carry negative charge.
- Their properties are independent of the nature of the electrode and the gas used.

**Conclusion:**

Cathode rays consist of negatively charged particles called electrons.

### 2.2 Properties of Electrons

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Symbol | e⁻ |
| Charge | \(-1.602\times10^{-19}\) C |
| Mass | \(9.109\times10^{-31}\) kg |
| Relative charge | -1 |
| Relative mass | Approximately 1/1836 |

Electrons are present outside the nucleus and play a major role in chemical bonding and reactions.

### 2.3 Charge-to-Mass Ratio of Electron

J. J. Thomson determined the charge-to-mass ratio of the electron.

\[
\boxed{\frac{e}{m}=1.7588\times10^{11}\text{ C/kg}}
\]

This experiment established that cathode rays contain particles with a characteristic charge-to-mass ratio.

### 2.4 Millikan's Oil Drop Experiment

Robert Millikan determined the elementary electric charge through the oil drop experiment.

Tiny charged oil droplets were suspended between two electrically charged plates.

By adjusting the electric field, he studied the balance between gravitational force and electrical force.

The magnitude of the elementary charge was determined to be approximately:

\[
\boxed{e=1.602\times10^{-19}\text{ C}}
\]

Combining this value with Thomson's charge-to-mass ratio allowed the mass of the electron to be calculated.

## 3. Discovery of the Proton

Positive rays were observed in discharge tube experiments using perforated cathodes.

These rays, called canal rays or anode rays, consisted of positively charged particles.

The lightest positive particle obtained from hydrogen was identified as the proton.

### 3.1 Properties of Protons

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Symbol | p⁺ |
| Charge | \(+1.602\times10^{-19}\) C |
| Mass | \(1.673\times10^{-27}\) kg |
| Relative charge | +1 |
| Relative mass | Approximately 1 |

Protons are located inside the nucleus of an atom.

## 4. Discovery of the Neutron

James Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932.

He bombarded beryllium with alpha particles and observed a highly penetrating radiation.

When this radiation interacted with paraffin wax, energetic protons were released.

The observations were explained by the existence of neutral particles with masses approximately equal to that of protons.

These particles were named neutrons.

### 4.1 Properties of Neutrons

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Symbol | n |
| Charge | 0 |
| Mass | \(1.675\times10^{-27}\) kg |
| Relative charge | 0 |
| Relative mass | Approximately 1 |

Neutrons are located inside the atomic nucleus.

## 5. Structure of the Atom

### 5.1 General Structure

An atom consists of:

1. A small, dense nucleus at the centre.
2. Electrons occupying regions around the nucleus.

The nucleus contains protons and neutrons.

Electrons occupy atomic orbitals, which describe regions of high probability of finding electrons.

### 5.2 Distribution of Mass and Charge

- Protons contribute positive charge.
- Neutrons contribute no electric charge.
- Electrons contribute negative charge.
- Almost all the mass of an atom is concentrated in its nucleus.

An electrically neutral atom contains equal numbers of protons and electrons.

### 5.3 Subatomic Particle Comparison

| Particle | Charge | Approximate relative mass | Location |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Electron | -1 | 1/1836 | Outside nucleus |
| Proton | +1 | 1 | Nucleus |
| Neutron | 0 | 1 | Nucleus |

## 6. Atomic Number and Mass Number

### 6.1 Atomic Number

Atomic number is the number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom.

It is represented by \(Z\).

\[
\boxed{Z=\text{Number of protons}}
\]

For a neutral atom:

\[
\boxed{Z=\text{Number of electrons}}
\]

The atomic number uniquely identifies an element.

Examples:

- Hydrogen: \(Z=1\).
- Carbon: \(Z=6\).
- Oxygen: \(Z=8\).
- Sodium: \(Z=11\).
- Iron: \(Z=26\).

### 6.2 Mass Number

Mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons present in the nucleus.

It is represented by \(A\).

\[
\boxed{A=Z+N}
\]

Where:
- \(A\) = Mass number.
- \(Z\) = Atomic number.
- \(N\) = Number of neutrons.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{N=A-Z}
\]

### 6.3 Atomic Notation

An atom is represented using:

\[
\boxed{^{A}_{Z}X}
\]

Where:
- \(X\) = Chemical symbol.
- \(A\) = Mass number.
- \(Z\) = Atomic number.

Example:

\[
^{23}_{11}Na
\]

For a neutral sodium atom:

- Protons = 11.
- Electrons = 11.
- Neutrons = 23 - 11 = 12.

### 6.4 Numerical Example

An atom has atomic number 17 and mass number 35. Determine the numbers of protons, electrons, and neutrons.

Given:

\[
Z=17,\quad A=35
\]

Protons:

\[
p=Z=17
\]

Electrons in a neutral atom:

\[
e=17
\]

Neutrons:

\[
N=A-Z
\]

\[
N=35-17
\]

\[
\boxed{N=18}
\]

## 7. Ions and Electronic Charge

### 7.1 Formation of Ions

An ion is formed when an atom or group of atoms gains or loses electrons.

The number of protons remains unchanged during ordinary ion formation.

**Cations**

A cation is a positively charged ion formed by losing electrons.

Example:

\[
Na\rightarrow Na^++e^-
\]

A sodium atom loses one electron to form a sodium ion.

**Anions**

An anion is a negatively charged ion formed by gaining electrons.

Example:

\[
Cl+e^-\rightarrow Cl^-
\]

A chlorine atom gains one electron to form a chloride ion.

### 7.2 Calculating the Number of Electrons in Ions

For a positively charged ion:

\[
\text{Electrons}=Z-\text{Positive charge magnitude}
\]

For a negatively charged ion:

\[
\text{Electrons}=Z+\text{Negative charge magnitude}
\]

Example: Calculate the number of electrons in \(Mg^{2+}\).

Magnesium has:

\[
Z=12
\]

It loses two electrons:

\[
e=12-2
\]

\[
\boxed{e=10}
\]

## 8. Isotopes, Isobars, and Isotones

### 8.1 Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element having the same atomic number but different mass numbers.

They have:
- The same number of protons.
- Different numbers of neutrons.
- Similar chemical properties in many ordinary chemical reactions.

Examples:

\[
^{1}_{1}H,\quad ^{2}_{1}H,\quad ^{3}_{1}H
\]

These are the isotopes of hydrogen:

- Protium.
- Deuterium.
- Tritium.

### 8.2 Isobars

Isobars are atoms of different elements having the same mass number but different atomic numbers.

Example:

\[
^{40}_{18}Ar,\quad ^{40}_{20}Ca
\]

Both have mass number 40 but different atomic numbers.

### 8.3 Isotones

Isotones are atoms of different elements having the same number of neutrons.

Example:

\[
^{14}_{6}C
\]

Neutrons:

\[
14-6=8
\]

For:

\[
^{15}_{7}N
\]

Neutrons:

\[
15-7=8
\]

Therefore, these atoms are isotones.

### 8.4 Comparison

| Term | Same quantity | Different quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Isotopes | Atomic number | Mass number |
| Isobars | Mass number | Atomic number |
| Isotones | Number of neutrons | Atomic number |

## 9. Atomic Models

### 9.1 Thomson's Atomic Model

J. J. Thomson proposed a model in which the atom consisted of a positively charged sphere with negatively charged electrons embedded within it.

It is commonly called the plum pudding model.

**Main ideas:**

- Positive charge was distributed throughout the atom.
- Electrons were embedded in the positive region.
- The atom was electrically neutral overall.

**Limitation:**

The model could not explain the results of Rutherford's alpha-particle scattering experiment.

### 9.2 Rutherford's Nuclear Model

Ernest Rutherford proposed the nuclear model based on the alpha-particle scattering experiment.

**Experimental arrangement:**

- Alpha particles were directed toward a thin gold foil.
- A fluorescent screen was used to detect scattered particles.
- The scattering angles were observed.

**Observations:**

1. Most alpha particles passed through the foil without deflection.
2. Some alpha particles were deflected through small angles.
3. A very small number were deflected through large angles.
4. Extremely few particles returned nearly in the direction from which they came.

**Conclusions:**

- Most of the atom is empty space.
- Positive charge is concentrated in a very small central nucleus.
- Nearly all atomic mass is concentrated in the nucleus.
- Electrons occupy the region surrounding the nucleus.

**Limitations:**

- It could not explain the stability of atoms according to classical electromagnetic theory.
- It could not explain discrete atomic line spectra.
- It did not adequately describe the arrangement of electrons.

### 9.3 Bohr's Atomic Model

Niels Bohr proposed an improved atomic model to explain the hydrogen spectrum and atomic stability.

**Main postulates:**

1. Electrons occupy certain permitted stationary orbits around the nucleus.
2. Electrons do not continuously radiate energy while remaining in an allowed stationary orbit.
3. Each permitted orbit has a definite energy.
4. Radiation is absorbed or emitted when an electron transitions between allowed energy levels.
5. The angular momentum of an electron in an allowed orbit is quantized.

The angular momentum condition is:

\[
\boxed{mvr=\frac{nh}{2\pi}}
\]

Where:
- \(m\) = Electron mass.
- \(v\) = Electron velocity.
- \(r\) = Orbit radius.
- \(n\) = Principal quantum number.
- \(h\) = Planck's constant.

For a hydrogen-like atom:

\[
\boxed{r_n=\frac{a_0n^2}{Z}}
\]

Where:
- \(a_0\) = Bohr radius.
- \(n\) = Principal quantum number.
- \(Z\) = Nuclear charge number.

For a hydrogen atom:

\[
E_n=-\frac{13.6}{n^2}\text{ eV}
\]

For a hydrogen-like species:

\[
\boxed{E_n=-\frac{13.6Z^2}{n^2}\text{ eV}}
\]

### 9.4 Limitations of Bohr's Model

- It works well mainly for hydrogen-like one-electron species.
- It cannot adequately explain multi-electron atoms.
- It cannot explain the fine structure of spectral lines.
- It does not account for the wave nature of electrons.
- It does not fully explain the effects of magnetic and electric fields on spectra.

### 9.5 Modern Quantum Mechanical Model

The modern atomic model describes electrons using quantum mechanics.

Unlike Bohr's model, it does not assign electrons definite circular paths.

Instead:
- Electrons are described by wavefunctions.
- Their positions are represented by probability distributions.
- Atomic orbitals describe regions of high electron probability.
- Electron energies are quantized.

The mathematical description of an electron in an atom is based on the Schrödinger equation.

## 10. Practical Applications

### 10.1 Medical Applications

Radioactive isotopes are used in medical imaging, diagnosis, and treatment.

Examples include:
- Technetium-99m in diagnostic imaging.
- Iodine-131 in thyroid-related applications.

### 10.2 Nuclear Energy

Nuclear reactions involve changes in atomic nuclei and can release large amounts of energy.

### 10.3 Carbon Dating

Carbon-14 is used to estimate the age of certain archaeological and biological materials.

### 10.4 Chemical Identification

Atomic number identifies an element, while atomic structure helps explain its chemical behaviour and position in the periodic table.

## 11. Common Exam Traps

1. Atomic number equals the number of protons, not the total number of protons and neutrons.
2. Mass number is the sum of protons and neutrons.
3. A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons.
4. Ion formation involves electron transfer, not ordinary changes in the number of protons.
5. Isotopes have the same atomic number but different mass numbers.
6. Isobars have the same mass number but different atomic numbers.
7. Isotones have the same number of neutrons.
8. Most alpha particles passed through Rutherford's gold foil because atoms are mostly empty space.
9. Bohr's model is especially successful for hydrogen-like species.
10. Modern atomic orbitals are probability distributions, not fixed circular electron paths.
