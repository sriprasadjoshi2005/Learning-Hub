**Biodiversity** refers to the variety and variability of life on Earth. It encompasses diversity at three major levels: **genetic diversity, species diversity, and ecological diversity**.

- **Genetic Diversity:** Variation in genes within a species. Example: Different varieties of rice and mango.
- **Species Diversity:** Variety of species present in a particular region.
- **Ecological Diversity:** Variety of ecosystems, habitats, and ecological processes within a region.

## Patterns of Biodiversity

Biodiversity is not distributed uniformly across the Earth. It varies with geographical location, climate, habitat complexity, and evolutionary history.

### 1. Latitudinal Gradient

Species diversity generally **decreases as we move away from the equator towards the poles**.

Tropical regions near the equator generally have greater species richness than temperate and polar regions.

**Reasons for Higher Tropical Biodiversity:**

- **Longer evolutionary time:** Tropical regions have experienced relatively fewer large-scale climatic disturbances, allowing more time for species diversification.
- **Climatic stability:** Tropical environments generally experience less seasonal variation, favouring niche specialisation.
- **Higher solar energy:** Greater solar radiation supports higher primary productivity, which can support more species.

**Example:** Tropical rainforests contain significantly more species than temperate forests.

### 2. Species-Area Relationship

The species-area relationship was extensively studied by **Alexander von Humboldt**.

It describes the relationship between the area of a habitat and the number of species found within it.

As the explored area increases, species richness generally increases, but the rate of increase gradually slows.

The relationship is represented by:

\[
S=CA^Z
\]

Where:

- \(S\): Species richness.
- \(A\): Area of the habitat.
- \(C\): Constant.
- \(Z\): Slope of the regression line.

Taking logarithms on both sides:

\[
\log S=\log C+Z\log A
\]

**Important Values of Z:**

- For small regions within a country or a local area, \(Z\) generally ranges from **0.1 to 0.2**.
- For very large areas, such as entire continents, \(Z\) generally ranges from **0.6 to 1.2**.

The higher slope for large geographical areas reflects a stronger increase in species richness with increasing area.

### Species-Area Relationship Curve

    Species Richness (S)
          ^
          |                    /
          |                ___/
          |            ___/
          |        ___/
          |    ___/
          |___/
          +--------------------------> Area (A)

The curve rises because larger habitats generally contain more species and a greater variety of ecological niches.

## Importance of Biodiversity

Biodiversity is essential for maintaining ecosystem stability and providing resources and ecological services.

### 1. Ecosystem Services

Biodiversity supports several ecosystem functions:

- Pollination of crops by insects and other animals.
- Purification of air and water.
- Nutrient cycling and decomposition.
- Soil formation and maintenance.
- Regulation of climate and atmospheric gases.
- Prevention of soil erosion and maintenance of ecosystem productivity.

### 2. Economic Importance

Biodiversity provides resources used by humans:

- Food, including fruits, grains, vegetables, and fish.
- Medicines derived from plants, animals, and microorganisms.
- Timber, fibres, fuel, and other raw materials.
- Genetic resources for improving agricultural crops and livestock.

### 3. Ethical and Aesthetic Importance

Every species has an intrinsic value and a right to exist, independent of its usefulness to humans.

Biodiversity also provides recreational, cultural, educational, and aesthetic benefits.

## Threats to Biodiversity: The Evil Quartet

The term **Evil Quartet** describes four major causes of biodiversity loss identified in ecological studies.

### 1. Habitat Loss and Fragmentation

**Habitat Loss:** Destruction or degradation of natural habitats, making them unsuitable for the species that depend on them.

**Habitat Fragmentation:** Breaking a large, continuous habitat into smaller, isolated patches.

Examples:

- Deforestation of tropical rainforests.
- Conversion of forests into agricultural land.
- Urbanisation and construction of roads through natural habitats.

Habitat loss and fragmentation are considered the **most important causes of biodiversity loss worldwide**.

**Effects:**

- Reduction in available food and shelter.
- Isolation of populations.
- Reduced gene flow between fragmented populations.
- Increased vulnerability to extinction.
- Disruption of ecological interactions.

### 2. Over-exploitation

Over-exploitation occurs when humans harvest or use biological resources at a rate faster than their natural populations can recover.

Examples:

- **Steller's sea cow:** Became extinct due to excessive hunting.
- **Passenger pigeon:** Became extinct following extensive commercial hunting and habitat destruction.
- Excessive fishing leading to the depletion of fish populations.

### 3. Alien Species Invasions

Alien species are organisms introduced into geographical regions outside their natural distribution.

Some introduced species become invasive and outcompete, prey upon, or otherwise negatively affect native species.

Examples:

- **Nile perch:** Introduced into Lake Victoria, contributing to the extinction of several native cichlid fish species.
- **Parthenium:** An invasive weed that affects native vegetation and agricultural ecosystems.
- **Water hyacinth (*Eichhornia crassipes*):** An invasive aquatic plant that spreads rapidly and can obstruct waterways.
- **African catfish (*Clarias gariepinus*):** Its introduction into Indian freshwater ecosystems threatens native fish diversity.

### 4. Co-extinctions

Co-extinction occurs when the extinction of one species causes the extinction of another species that depends on it.

Examples:

- A host species and its obligate parasite may disappear together.
- A plant species and its specialised pollinator may become extinct together.

**Example:** Certain fig trees depend on specific fig wasp species for pollination. The loss of an obligate partner can threaten the survival of the other.

## Conservation of Biodiversity

Biodiversity conservation involves protecting species, their genetic diversity, and the ecosystems in which they live.

Conservation strategies are broadly classified into **in-situ conservation** and **ex-situ conservation**.

### 1. In-situ Conservation

In-situ conservation involves protecting species within their natural habitats and ecosystems.

It allows organisms to survive, reproduce, and continue their ecological interactions under natural conditions.

Major methods include:

#### Biodiversity Hotspots

A biodiversity hotspot is a region with exceptionally high species richness and endemism that is also experiencing significant habitat loss.

**Endemic Species:** Species naturally restricted to a particular geographical region.

According to the traditional NCERT classification, the world has **34 biodiversity hotspots**, of which **three** extend into India:

1. **Western Ghats and Sri Lanka**
2. **Indo-Burma**
3. **Himalaya**

*Note: Biodiversity hotspot counts and boundaries can change as scientific assessments are updated.*

#### Protected Areas

| Conservation Area | Description |
|---|---|
| **National Parks** | Protected areas established to conserve wildlife, habitats, and natural ecosystems. |
| **Wildlife Sanctuaries** | Areas where wild animals and their habitats receive legal protection, subject to applicable regulations. |
| **Biosphere Reserves** | Large protected areas designed to conserve biodiversity while supporting sustainable use and research. |
| **Sacred Groves** | Forest patches protected by local communities because of religious or cultural traditions. |

#### Sacred Groves

Sacred groves are forest patches protected by local communities through traditional beliefs and cultural practices.

They act as natural refuges for rare, endemic, and threatened species.

Examples in India include sacred groves in:

- Khasi and Jaintia Hills of Meghalaya.
- Western Ghats of Karnataka and Maharashtra.
- Aravalli Hills of Rajasthan.

### 2. Ex-situ Conservation

Ex-situ conservation involves protecting threatened species outside their natural habitats under controlled conditions.

It is particularly useful when species face severe threats in their natural environments.

Major methods include:

- **Zoological Parks:** Maintain and breed wild animals under human care.
- **Botanical Gardens:** Maintain collections of living plants for conservation, research, and education.
- **Seed Banks:** Store seeds under controlled conditions for future germination and propagation.
- **Gene Banks:** Preserve genetic material such as seeds, pollen, tissues, and reproductive cells.
- **Cryopreservation:** Preserves biological materials at extremely low temperatures, generally using liquid nitrogen at \(-196^\circ\mathrm{C}\).
- **Tissue Culture:** Allows plants to be propagated from small pieces of tissue under sterile laboratory conditions.

### In-situ vs Ex-situ Conservation

| Feature | In-situ Conservation | Ex-situ Conservation |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Conservation within natural habitats | Conservation outside natural habitats |
| Environment | Natural ecosystem | Controlled environment |
| Main focus | Protecting species and their habitats | Protecting individual species and genetic material |
| Examples | National parks, sanctuaries, biosphere reserves, sacred groves | Zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks, cryopreservation |

## Common Exam Traps

- **Three Levels of Biodiversity:** Genetic diversity, species diversity, and ecological diversity.
- **Latitudinal Gradient:** Species richness generally decreases from the equator towards the poles.
- **Species-Area Relationship:** Larger geographical areas generally contain more species.
- **Humboldt's Equation:** Remember \(S=CA^Z\) and its logarithmic form \(\log S=\log C+Z\log A\).
- **Slope Z:** Generally 0.1–0.2 for small regions and 0.6–1.2 for very large regions such as continents.
- **Evil Quartet:** Habitat loss and fragmentation, over-exploitation, alien species invasions, and co-extinctions.
- **Major Threat:** Habitat loss and fragmentation are the leading causes of biodiversity loss.
- **Alien vs Invasive Species:** All invasive species are introduced or non-native in the region, but not every alien species becomes invasive.
- **Co-extinction:** The extinction of one species can cause the extinction of another species dependent on it.
- **In-situ vs Ex-situ:** Sacred groves and biosphere reserves are in-situ; botanical gardens, zoological parks, and cryopreservation are ex-situ.
- **Biodiversity Hotspots:** According to the traditional NCERT classification, there are 34 global hotspots, with three extending into India.
- **Cryopreservation Temperature:** Liquid nitrogen is used at approximately \(-196^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

## Quiz

1. On a logarithmic scale, the species-area relationship studied by Alexander von Humboldt yields a straight line described by which equation?

- [ ] \(S=C+ZA\)
- [x] \(\log S=\log C+Z\log A\)

- [ ] \(S=CA+Z\)

> Humboldt described the species-area relationship using \(S=CA^Z\). Taking logarithms gives \(\log S=\log C+Z\log A\), which represents a straight line.

- [ ] In-situ conservation

- [ ] Sacred grove preservation

> Cryopreservation stores biological materials outside their natural habitats under controlled laboratory conditions. Therefore, it is an ex-situ conservation technique.
