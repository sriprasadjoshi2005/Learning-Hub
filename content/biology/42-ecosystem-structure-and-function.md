An **ecosystem** is a functional unit of nature in which living organisms interact among themselves and with their surrounding physical environment. It consists of **biotic components** (living organisms) and **abiotic components** (non-living factors such as air, water, soil, and sunlight).

## Structure of an Ecosystem

An ecosystem consists of two major components:

### 1. Abiotic Components

These include the non-living physical and chemical factors of the environment:

- **Inorganic substances:** Carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, water, and other minerals.
- **Organic substances:** Proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, and other organic compounds.
- **Climatic factors:** Temperature, light, rainfall, humidity, and wind.

### 2. Biotic Components

These include all living organisms within an ecosystem.

| Component | Role | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Producers** | Convert solar energy into chemical energy through photosynthesis. | Green plants, algae, phytoplankton |
| **Primary Consumers** | Feed directly on producers. | Deer, rabbits, zooplankton |
| **Secondary Consumers** | Feed on primary consumers. | Frogs, small carnivorous fishes |
| **Tertiary Consumers** | Feed on secondary consumers. | Large predatory fishes, snakes |
| **Decomposers** | Break down dead organic matter and release nutrients. | Bacteria, fungi |

## Core Functions of Ecosystems

Ecosystems perform four major functional processes: **productivity, decomposition, energy flow, and nutrient cycling**.

### 1. Productivity

Productivity refers to the rate of biomass production in an ecosystem per unit area over a given period.

It is classified into two major types:

- **Primary Productivity:** Rate at which producers synthesize organic matter through photosynthesis or, in some ecosystems, chemosynthesis.
- **Secondary Productivity:** Rate of formation of new organic matter by consumers.

#### Gross Primary Productivity (GPP)

GPP is the total rate of organic matter production through photosynthesis by producers per unit area per unit time.

#### Net Primary Productivity (NPP)

NPP is the amount of organic matter remaining after producers use some of the energy they capture for respiration. It represents the biomass available to consumers and decomposers.

\[
\mathrm{NPP = GPP - R}
\]

Where:

- \(\mathrm{GPP}\): Gross Primary Productivity.
- \(\mathrm{NPP}\): Net Primary Productivity.
- \(\mathrm{R}\): Respiratory losses by producers.

**Important:** NPP represents the energy stored as new plant biomass after autotrophic respiration.

### 2. Decomposition

Decomposition is the process by which decomposers break down complex organic matter, called **detritus**, into simpler inorganic substances.

Detritus includes dead plant and animal remains, fallen leaves, and faecal matter.

**Detritivores:** Organisms that feed on detritus and help break it into smaller particles. Example: Earthworms.

#### Stages of Decomposition

The major stages generally occur in the following sequence:

1. **Fragmentation:** Detritivores break detritus into smaller particles.
2. **Leaching:** Water-soluble inorganic nutrients move downward into the soil.
3. **Catabolism:** Bacteria and fungi secrete enzymes that break down complex organic substances into simpler compounds.
4. **Humification:** Formation and accumulation of dark-coloured, amorphous organic matter called humus.
5. **Mineralization:** Microorganisms further degrade humus and release inorganic nutrients into the soil.

#### Factors Affecting Decomposition

| Factor | Effect on Decomposition |
|---|---|
| **Temperature** | Warm and moist conditions generally increase microbial activity and decomposition. |
| **Moisture** | Adequate moisture promotes decomposition, while extreme dryness slows it. |
| **Oxygen Availability** | Aerobic conditions generally favour faster decomposition. |
| **Chemical Composition** | Detritus rich in nitrogen and water-soluble substances decomposes faster. |
| **Lignin and Chitin** | Complex structural compounds slow decomposition. |

**Important:** Decomposition is generally faster under warm and moist conditions and slower under cold or dry conditions.

### 3. Energy Flow

Energy flow refers to the transfer of energy through different trophic levels in an ecosystem.

- Solar energy is captured by producers through photosynthesis.
- Producers transfer energy to primary consumers.
- Energy moves through successive consumer levels.
- Decomposers obtain energy from dead organic matter and waste.

**Energy flow is unidirectional:** Energy enters most ecosystems as sunlight, passes through trophic levels, and is eventually dissipated as heat. It is not recycled in the same way as nutrients.

#### Lindeman's 10% Law

According to the **10% law**, only approximately 10% of the energy available at one trophic level is transferred to the next trophic level. The remaining energy is not transferred as biomass to the next level, largely because it is used in metabolism and lost as heat and waste.

Example:

| Trophic Level | Available Energy |
|---|---:|
| Producers | 10,000 kJ |
| Primary Consumers | 1,000 kJ |
| Secondary Consumers | 100 kJ |
| Tertiary Consumers | 10 kJ |

The 10% value is an ecological approximation, not a universal fixed efficiency for every ecosystem.

### 4. Nutrient Cycling

Nutrient cycling refers to the movement and reuse of essential elements between living organisms and the physical environment.

Unlike energy, nutrients are recycled within ecosystems.

Major nutrient cycles include:

- **Carbon Cycle:** Movement of carbon through photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, and combustion.
- **Phosphorus Cycle:** Movement of phosphorus through rocks, soil, water, and living organisms. It has no significant gaseous phase under ordinary conditions.
- **Nitrogen Cycle:** Movement of nitrogen through nitrogen fixation, nitrification, assimilation, ammonification, and denitrification.

## Ecological Pyramids

Ecological pyramids are graphical representations of the trophic structure of an ecosystem. They represent the relationship between producers and consumers at different trophic levels.

There are three major types:

### 1. Pyramid of Numbers

Represents the number of individual organisms present at each trophic level.

- **Upright:** Common in grassland ecosystems, where producers are numerous and support progressively fewer consumers.
- **Inverted:** Can occur in tree ecosystems, where a single tree supports numerous herbivorous insects and parasites.

### 2. Pyramid of Biomass

Represents the total dry mass of living organisms at each trophic level per unit area at a particular time.

- **Upright:** Common in terrestrial ecosystems, where producers generally have greater biomass than consumers.
- **Inverted:** Common in aquatic ecosystems, where the standing crop of phytoplankton may be smaller than the biomass of consumers such as zooplankton and fishes.

**Standing Crop:** The total biomass of organisms present at a particular trophic level at a given time.

### 3. Pyramid of Energy

Represents the amount of energy available at each trophic level per unit area per unit time.

- Always upright.
- Energy decreases at successive trophic levels.
- Cannot be inverted because energy is lost during transfer, mainly through respiration and heat dissipation.

### Ecological Pyramids Summary

| Pyramid Type | What It Represents | Possible Orientation |
|---|---|---|
| **Pyramid of Numbers** | Number of individuals | Upright or inverted |
| **Pyramid of Biomass** | Total dry biomass | Upright or inverted |
| **Pyramid of Energy** | Energy available per unit area per unit time | Always upright |

## Ecological Succession

Ecological succession is the gradual and orderly process through which the species composition and structure of a community change over time.

The sequence of communities that successively replace one another is called a **sere**.

- **Pioneer Community:** The first group of organisms to establish itself in a newly exposed or disturbed habitat.
- **Seral Stages:** Intermediate communities that develop during succession.
- **Climax Community:** A relatively stable community that develops at the final stage of succession under prevailing environmental conditions.

### Types of Ecological Succession

#### 1. Primary Succession

Primary succession occurs in areas where no soil or established biological community previously existed.

Examples:

- Bare rock exposed by a retreating glacier.
- Newly cooled lava surfaces.
- Newly formed sand dunes.

**Pioneer Species on Bare Rock:** Lichens are generally the first organisms to colonise bare rock. They help weather the rock and contribute to the formation of soil.

Primary succession is a slow process because soil must first develop.

#### 2. Secondary Succession

Secondary succession occurs in areas where an existing community has been destroyed or disturbed, but the soil remains.

Examples:

- Abandoned agricultural fields.
- Forests recovering after fires.
- Areas recovering after floods.

Secondary succession is generally faster than primary succession because soil, nutrients, microorganisms, and sometimes seeds or underground plant parts remain.

### Primary vs Secondary Succession

| Feature | Primary Succession | Secondary Succession |
|---|---|---|
| Starting condition | Bare surface without developed soil | Previously inhabited area with soil |
| Soil availability | Initially absent | Already present |
| Pioneer organisms | Lichens on bare rock; other pioneers in different habitats | Grasses, herbs, and other surviving or colonising plants |
| Speed | Generally very slow | Generally faster |
| Example | Newly exposed bare rock | Abandoned farmland |

### Xerarch and Hydrarch Succession

Succession can also be classified according to the initial habitat:

- **Xerarch Succession:** Begins in dry habitats, such as bare rock.
- **Hydrarch Succession:** Begins in aquatic habitats, such as ponds and lakes.

Both types generally progress towards communities adapted to the prevailing climatic conditions.

## Common Exam Traps

- **GPP vs NPP:** GPP is the total organic matter produced by producers; NPP is what remains after respiratory losses. Remember: \(\mathrm{NPP=GPP-R}\).
- **Primary vs Secondary Productivity:** Primary productivity is generated by producers; secondary productivity is generated by consumers.
- **Decomposition Sequence:** Fragmentation → Leaching → Catabolism → Humification → Mineralization.
- **Humification vs Mineralization:** Humification produces humus, while mineralization releases inorganic nutrients.
- **Energy vs Nutrients:** Energy flows unidirectionally and is dissipated as heat; nutrients are recycled.
- **Lindeman's 10% Law:** Approximately 10% of energy is transferred to the next trophic level. It does not mean that exactly 90% is always lost specifically as heat.
- **Pyramid of Energy:** Always upright; it cannot be inverted under natural conditions.
- **Aquatic Biomass Inversion:** The biomass pyramid may be inverted because phytoplankton have a small standing crop but rapid reproduction and turnover.
- **Pyramid of Numbers:** Can be inverted in a tree ecosystem because one large producer may support numerous consumers.
- **Primary vs Secondary Succession:** Primary succession begins without developed soil; secondary succession begins where soil remains.
- **Pioneer Species:** Lichens are important pioneer organisms in xerarch succession on bare rock.
- **Succession Speed:** Secondary succession is generally faster because soil and some biological components are already present.

## Quiz

1. In a terrestrial forest ecosystem, the Gross Primary Productivity (\(\mathrm{GPP}\)) is measured at \(20,000\text{ kcal/m}^2/\text{yr}\), and autotrophic respiration losses (\(\mathrm{R}\)) account for \(8,000\text{ kcal/m}^2/\text{yr}\). What is the Net Primary Productivity (\(\mathrm{NPP}\)) available to primary consumers?

- [ ] \(28,000\text{ kcal/m}^2/\text{yr}\)
- [x] \(12,000\text{ kcal/m}^2/\text{yr}\)
- [ ] \(16,000\text{ kcal/m}^2/\text{yr}\)
- [ ] \(2,000\text{ kcal/m}^2/\text{yr}\)

> Net Primary Productivity is calculated as \(\mathrm{NPP=GPP-R}\). Therefore, \(20,000-8,000=12,000\text{ kcal/m}^2/\text{yr}\).

2. Which ecological pyramid can never exist in an inverted orientation in any natural ecosystem?

- [ ] Pyramid of Numbers in a forest
- [ ] Pyramid of Biomass in a lake
- [x] Pyramid of Energy
- [ ] Pyramid of Biomass in a grassland

> The pyramid of energy is always upright because energy decreases at successive trophic levels due to metabolic use and energy dissipation as heat.

3. During primary autotrophic succession on bare rock (xerarch succession), which organisms function as pioneer species and contribute to rock weathering and soil formation?

- [ ] Mosses
- [x] Lichens
- [ ] Annual grasses
- [ ] Pteridophytes

> Lichens are important pioneer organisms in xerarch succession. They secrete organic acids that contribute to rock weathering and help initiate soil formation.

4. Which of the following correctly represents the sequence of decomposition?

- [ ] Leaching → Fragmentation → Humification → Catabolism → Mineralization
- [x] Fragmentation → Leaching → Catabolism → Humification → Mineralization
- [ ] Fragmentation → Catabolism → Mineralization → Leaching → Humification
- [ ] Humification → Fragmentation → Leaching → Mineralization → Catabolism

> The major stages of decomposition are fragmentation, leaching, catabolism, humification, and mineralization, in that order.

5. In an ecosystem, producers contain \(10,000\text{ kJ}\) of energy. According to Lindeman's 10% law, how much energy is expected to reach the secondary consumers?

- [ ] \(10,000\text{ kJ}\)
- [ ] \(1,000\text{ kJ}\)
- [x] \(100\text{ kJ}\)
- [ ] \(10\text{ kJ}\)

> Primary consumers receive approximately \(1,000\text{ kJ}\), and secondary consumers receive approximately 10% of that amount, which is \(100\text{ kJ}\).

6. Which statement correctly distinguishes primary succession from secondary succession?

- [ ] Primary succession occurs only in aquatic ecosystems.
- [ ] Secondary succession always begins on bare rock.
- [x] Primary succession begins without developed soil, whereas secondary succession occurs where soil remains.
- [ ] Secondary succession is always slower than primary succession.

> Primary succession begins on surfaces without developed soil, while secondary succession occurs after disturbance in areas where soil is already present. Therefore, secondary succession is generally faster.

7. Which stage of decomposition involves the formation and accumulation of dark-coloured, amorphous organic matter called humus?

- [ ] Fragmentation
- [ ] Leaching
- [ ] Catabolism
- [x] Humification

> Humification is the process through which partially decomposed organic matter is converted into humus, a dark-coloured, amorphous substance.

8. Why is the pyramid of biomass sometimes inverted in aquatic ecosystems?

- [ ] Aquatic producers always have greater biomass than consumers.
- [x] Phytoplankton have a low standing crop but reproduce and turn over rapidly.
- [ ] Fish consume inorganic nutrients directly.
- [ ] Energy increases at each successive trophic level.

> In aquatic ecosystems, phytoplankton reproduce rapidly and have a high turnover rate. Their standing biomass at a given time can therefore be lower than the biomass of consumers such as fishes.

9. Which ecological process involves the movement of water-soluble inorganic nutrients downward through the soil during decomposition?

- [ ] Fragmentation
- [x] Leaching
- [ ] Humification
- [ ] Mineralization

> Leaching occurs when water-soluble inorganic nutrients move downward through the soil with percolating water.

10. Which of the following statements about energy flow in an ecosystem is correct?

- [ ] Energy is recycled continuously between trophic levels.
- [ ] Energy flow is bidirectional between producers and consumers.
- [x] Energy flow is unidirectional and energy is eventually dissipated as heat.
- [ ] Decomposers return all energy to producers.

> Energy enters most ecosystems through sunlight, passes through trophic levels, and is eventually dissipated as heat. Unlike nutrients, energy is not recycled within the ecosystem.
