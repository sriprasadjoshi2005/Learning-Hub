The endocrine system coordinates long-term metabolic, developmental, and physiological functions by releasing chemical messengers called **Hormones** directly into the bloodstream. These hormones act on specific target cells or organs that possess appropriate receptors, regulating processes such as growth, metabolism, reproduction, and maintenance of internal homeostasis.

## Key Human Endocrine Glands and Hormones

The human endocrine system consists of several ductless glands that release hormones into the blood or surrounding tissue fluid.

| Endocrine Gland | Primary Hormones | Physiological Functions / Target Organs | Deficiency / Hypersecretion Disorders |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hypothalamus** | Releasing hormones (e.g., $\mathrm{GnRH}$) and inhibiting hormones (e.g., $\mathrm{GIH}$); synthesises $\mathrm{ADH}$ and Oxytocin | Regulates anterior pituitary secretions; controls posterior pituitary hormone production. | Disruption affects pituitary regulation and homeostasis. |
| **Pituitary (Anterior)** | $\mathrm{GH, TSH, ACTH, FSH, LH, Prolactin}$ | Regulates growth, thyroid activity, adrenal cortex activity, reproduction, and lactation. | Gigantism / Acromegaly (excess $\mathrm{GH}$); Pituitary dwarfism (low $\mathrm{GH}$ during childhood). |
| **Pituitary (Posterior)** | Stores and releases $\mathrm{ADH}$ and Oxytocin | $\mathrm{ADH}$ promotes renal water reabsorption; Oxytocin stimulates uterine contractions and milk ejection. | Diabetes Insipidus may result from insufficient $\mathrm{ADH}$ activity. |
| **Thyroid** | Thyroxine ($\mathrm{T_4}$), Triiodothyronine ($\mathrm{T_3}$), Thyrocalcitonin ($\mathrm{TCT}$) | Regulates basal metabolic rate ($\mathrm{BMR}$), growth, and development; $\mathrm{TCT}$ lowers blood $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$. | Goitre and hypothyroidism; congenital hypothyroidism can impair brain development; Graves' disease causes hyperthyroidism. |
| **Parathyroid** | Parathyroid Hormone ($\mathrm{PTH}$) | Hypercalcemic hormone that increases blood $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ levels. | Deficiency can cause hypocalcemic tetany. |
| **Adrenal Cortex** | Glucocorticoids (Cortisol), Mineralocorticoids (Aldosterone), Adrenal Androgens | Cortisol regulates metabolism and suppresses inflammation; Aldosterone regulates sodium and potassium balance. | Addison's disease (adrenocortical insufficiency); Cushing's syndrome (excess cortisol). |
| **Adrenal Medulla** | Adrenaline (Epinephrine), Noradrenaline (Norepinephrine) | Produces emergency responses associated with the sympathetic nervous system: fight or flight. | Altered catecholamine secretion can affect cardiovascular and stress responses. |
| **Pancreas (Islets of Langerhans)** | Insulin ($\beta$-cells), Glucagon ($\alpha$-cells), Somatostatin ($\delta$-cells) | Insulin lowers blood glucose; Glucagon increases blood glucose; Somatostatin inhibits secretion of several hormones. | Diabetes Mellitus is associated with insulin deficiency, impaired insulin action, or both. |

## Major Endocrine Glands and Their Functions

### 1. Hypothalamus and Pituitary Gland

The **Hypothalamus** links the nervous and endocrine systems. It regulates the pituitary gland through releasing and inhibiting hormones.

- **GnRH (Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone)**: Stimulates the anterior pituitary to release FSH and LH.
- **GIH (Growth Hormone-Inhibiting Hormone / Somatostatin)**: Inhibits growth hormone secretion.
- **ADH (Antidiuretic Hormone)**: Synthesised in the hypothalamus and released through the posterior pituitary.
- **Oxytocin**: Synthesised in the hypothalamus and released through the posterior pituitary.

The pituitary gland is divided into two major parts:

**Anterior Pituitary (Adenohypophysis)**

| Hormone | Major Function |
|---|---|
| **Growth Hormone (GH)** | Stimulates growth of bones and body tissues. |
| **Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone (TSH)** | Stimulates thyroid hormone secretion. |
| **Adrenocorticotropic Hormone (ACTH)** | Stimulates the adrenal cortex. |
| **Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH)** | Promotes ovarian follicle development and spermatogenesis. |
| **Luteinising Hormone (LH)** | Triggers ovulation and stimulates testosterone secretion in males. |
| **Prolactin (PRL)** | Stimulates milk production in mammary glands. |

**Posterior Pituitary (Neurohypophysis)**

- **ADH**: Increases water reabsorption in the kidneys, reducing urine volume.
- **Oxytocin**: Stimulates uterine contractions during childbirth and milk ejection during lactation.

The posterior pituitary does not synthesise these hormones; it stores and releases hormones produced by the hypothalamus.

### 2. Thyroid and Parathyroid Glands

**Thyroid Gland**

The thyroid gland produces the iodine-containing hormones **Thyroxine ($\mathrm{T_4}$)** and **Triiodothyronine ($\mathrm{T_3}$)**.

- Regulates basal metabolic rate ($\mathrm{BMR}$).
- Supports normal growth and development.
- Influences carbohydrate, protein, and fat metabolism.
- Requires iodine for the synthesis of thyroid hormones.

The thyroid also produces **Thyrocalcitonin (TCT)**, which helps lower blood calcium levels by inhibiting osteoclast activity and reducing bone resorption.

**Parathyroid Glands**

The parathyroid glands secrete **Parathyroid Hormone ($\mathrm{PTH}$)**.

PTH is a hypercalcemic hormone that increases blood calcium levels through:

- Stimulating bone resorption indirectly by promoting osteoclast activity.
- Increasing calcium reabsorption in the kidneys.
- Promoting activation of vitamin D (calcitriol), which increases intestinal calcium absorption.

### 3. Adrenal Glands

Each adrenal gland is located above a kidney and consists of two major regions.

**Adrenal Cortex**

The adrenal cortex produces three major groups of steroid hormones:

| Hormone Group | Major Hormones | Functions |
|---|---|---|
| **Mineralocorticoids** | Aldosterone | Regulates sodium and potassium balance and helps maintain blood volume and pressure. |
| **Glucocorticoids** | Cortisol | Regulates carbohydrate, protein, and fat metabolism; suppresses inflammatory and immune responses. |
| **Adrenal Androgens** | DHEA and related hormones | Contribute to secondary sexual characteristics and reproductive physiology. |

**Adrenal Medulla**

The adrenal medulla secretes **Adrenaline (Epinephrine)** and **Noradrenaline (Norepinephrine)**.

These hormones prepare the body for emergency situations through the **Fight-or-Flight Response**:

- Increase heart rate and cardiac output.
- Increase blood glucose availability.
- Dilate bronchioles.
- Redirect blood flow towards skeletal muscles.
- Increase alertness and metabolic activity.

### 4. Pancreas and Blood Glucose Regulation

The pancreas acts as both an exocrine and endocrine gland. Its endocrine portion consists of clusters of cells called the **Islets of Langerhans**.

| Cell Type | Hormone | Effect on Blood Glucose |
|---|---|---|
| **$\alpha$-cells** | Glucagon | Increases blood glucose. |
| **$\beta$-cells** | Insulin | Decreases blood glucose. |
| **$\delta$-cells** | Somatostatin | Inhibits the secretion of insulin and glucagon. |

**Insulin**

- Promotes glucose uptake in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue.
- Stimulates glycogenesis in the liver and muscles.
- Promotes fat synthesis and storage.
- Reduces blood glucose concentration.

**Glucagon**

- Stimulates glycogen breakdown (glycogenolysis) in the liver.
- Promotes glucose production through gluconeogenesis.
- Increases blood glucose concentration.

### Diabetes Mellitus vs Diabetes Insipidus

| Feature | Diabetes Mellitus | Diabetes Insipidus |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary Problem** | Insufficient insulin secretion, impaired insulin action, or both. | Insufficient ADH secretion or reduced kidney response to ADH. |
| **Blood Glucose** | Usually elevated. | Usually normal. |
| **Urine** | May contain glucose; increased urine output can occur. | Large volumes of dilute urine. |
| **Major Symptoms** | Polyuria, polydipsia, and sometimes polyphagia. | Polyuria and excessive thirst. |
| **Hormone Involved** | Insulin | ADH |

## Mechanism of Hormone Action

Hormones produce their effects by binding to specific receptors on or inside target cells. Their mechanisms depend largely on their chemical nature and solubility.

### 1. Water-Soluble Hormones

Examples include peptide hormones and catecholamines such as insulin and adrenaline.

- Cannot freely pass through the lipid bilayer of the plasma membrane.
- Bind to receptors located on the cell surface.
- Activate intracellular signalling pathways through second messengers.
- Produce biochemical responses inside the target cell.

Common second messengers include:

- **cAMP (Cyclic Adenosine Monophosphate)**
- **IP$_3$ (Inositol Trisphosphate)**
- **DAG (Diacylglycerol)**
- **$\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ (Calcium Ions)**

### 2. Lipid-Soluble Hormones

Examples include steroid hormones such as estrogen, testosterone, and cortisol, as well as thyroid hormones.

- Can cross the plasma membrane or enter target cells through specific transport mechanisms.
- Bind to intracellular receptors located in the cytoplasm or nucleus.
- The hormone-receptor complex interacts with specific DNA sequences.
- Alters gene transcription and protein synthesis, producing physiological effects.

### Comparison of Hormone Mechanisms

| Feature | Water-Soluble Hormones | Lipid-Soluble Hormones |
|---|---|---|
| **Examples** | Insulin, Glucagon, Adrenaline | Cortisol, Estrogen, Testosterone, Thyroid Hormones |
| **Receptor Location** | Cell membrane | Cytoplasm or nucleus |
| **Second Messengers** | Commonly involved | Generally not required for classical genomic action |
| **Main Mechanism** | Activates intracellular signalling cascades | Regulates gene expression |
| **Response** | Often rapid | Often slower and longer-lasting |

## Common Exam Traps

- **PTH vs TCT Antagonism**: Parathyroid Hormone ($\mathrm{PTH}$) increases blood $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ levels, whereas Thyrocalcitonin ($\mathrm{TCT}$) lowers blood $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ levels.
- **Posterior Pituitary Storage**: The posterior pituitary does **not synthesise ADH or Oxytocin**. Both hormones are synthesised in the hypothalamus and stored and released by the posterior pituitary.
- **Anterior vs Posterior Pituitary**: The anterior pituitary synthesises and secretes its own hormones, whereas the posterior pituitary stores and releases hypothalamic hormones.
- **Insulin vs Glucagon**: Insulin decreases blood glucose, while glucagon increases it. Insulin is secreted by pancreatic $\beta$-cells, whereas glucagon is secreted by $\alpha$-cells.
- **Diabetes Mellitus vs Diabetes Insipidus**: Diabetes Mellitus involves insulin dysfunction and abnormal glucose regulation; Diabetes Insipidus involves ADH deficiency or resistance and excessive production of dilute urine.
- **Adrenal Cortex vs Medulla**: The adrenal cortex produces steroid hormones, whereas the adrenal medulla produces catecholamines.
- **Hormone Solubility**: Water-soluble hormones generally bind to cell-surface receptors, while steroid hormones usually bind to intracellular receptors.
- **Thyroid Hormones**: Both $\mathrm{T_3}$ and $\mathrm{T_4}$ require iodine for synthesis. Thyrocalcitonin is a separate hormone involved in calcium regulation.
- **ADH vs Oxytocin**: ADH promotes renal water reabsorption, whereas Oxytocin stimulates uterine contractions and milk ejection.

## Quiz

1. A patient presents with persistent hyperglycemia, glucosuria (glucose in urine), ketonuria, and excessive thirst (polydipsia). Which endocrine pancreatic dysfunction is most directly associated with these clinical signs?

   - [ ] Hypersecretion of Glucagon by $\alpha$-cells
   - [x] Deficiency of Insulin production by $\beta$-cells, or impaired insulin action (Diabetes Mellitus)
   - [ ] Deficiency of $\mathrm{ADH}$ secretion by the posterior pituitary
   - [ ] Hypersecretion of Thyrocalcitonin

   > Diabetes Mellitus results from insufficient insulin production, impaired insulin action, or both. It causes elevated blood glucose, which can lead to glucosuria, increased urine production, and excessive thirst. Severe insulin deficiency can also promote ketone body formation.

2. Which hormone acts as a hypercalcemic factor by stimulating bone resorption, enhancing renal reabsorption of $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$, and increasing intestinal calcium absorption?

   - [ ] Thyrocalcitonin ($\mathrm{TCT}$)
   - [x] Parathyroid Hormone ($\mathrm{PTH}$)
   - [ ] Aldosterone
   - [ ] Cortisol

   > Parathyroid Hormone increases blood calcium levels by promoting bone resorption, increasing renal calcium reabsorption, and stimulating vitamin D activation, which enhances intestinal calcium absorption.

3. How do steroid hormones such as Estrogen and Testosterone exert their effects on target cells?

   - [ ] Binding cell-surface membrane receptors to activate cyclic AMP ($\mathrm{cAMP}$) as a second messenger.
   - [x] Diffusing across plasma membranes to bind intracellular receptors and regulate gene transcription.
   - [ ] Opening ligand-gated sodium channels to induce membrane depolarization.
   - [ ] Activating extracellular G-protein-coupled cascades as their primary mechanism.

   > Steroid hormones are lipid-soluble and can cross the plasma membrane. They bind intracellular receptors, and the hormone-receptor complex interacts with DNA to regulate gene expression and protein synthesis.

4. Which pancreatic cell type secretes the hormone responsible for increasing blood glucose concentration through glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis?

   - [ ] $\beta$-cells
   - [x] $\alpha$-cells
   - [ ] $\delta$-cells
   - [ ] Acinar cells

   > Pancreatic $\alpha$-cells secrete glucagon, which increases blood glucose by stimulating glycogen breakdown and glucose production, mainly in the liver.

5. Which statement correctly describes the hormonal activity of the posterior pituitary gland?

   - [ ] It synthesises both ADH and Oxytocin.
   - [ ] It synthesises GH and TSH.
   - [x] It stores and releases ADH and Oxytocin synthesised in the hypothalamus.
   - [ ] It produces insulin and glucagon.

   > The posterior pituitary, also called the neurohypophysis, does not synthesise ADH or Oxytocin. These hormones are produced by hypothalamic neurons, transported along their axons, and stored and released from the posterior pituitary.
