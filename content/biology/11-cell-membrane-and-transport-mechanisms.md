The **cell membrane** is a selectively permeable barrier that regulates the movement of substances into and out of the cell, maintaining cellular homeostasis. The accepted model of membrane structure is the **Fluid Mosaic Model**, proposed by Singer and Nicolson in 1972.

## Fluid Mosaic Model

The cell membrane consists of a flexible phospholipid bilayer containing proteins, cholesterol, and carbohydrates.

### Components of the Cell Membrane

| Component | Structure | Functions |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Phospholipids** | Amphipathic molecules with hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails | Form the basic bilayer structure of the membrane |
| **Integral Proteins** | Embedded partially or completely within the bilayer | Function as channels, carriers, receptors, and enzymes |
| **Peripheral Proteins** | Loosely attached to the inner or outer membrane surface | Support the membrane and participate in signaling |
| **Cholesterol** | Located between phospholipid molecules | Regulates membrane fluidity and stability |
| **Carbohydrates** | Attached to proteins and lipids, forming glycoproteins and glycolipids | Help in cell recognition, adhesion, and signaling |

### Phospholipid Bilayer

Phospholipids are **amphipathic molecules**, meaning they contain both hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions.

- **Hydrophilic heads** are polar and face the aqueous environments outside and inside the cell.
- **Hydrophobic tails** are nonpolar and face inward, away from water.

This arrangement creates a selectively permeable barrier that restricts the passage of many polar and charged substances.

### Membrane Proteins

Membrane proteins are classified into two major types:

- **Integral (intrinsic) proteins:** Embedded partially or completely within the phospholipid bilayer. Some span the entire membrane and are called transmembrane proteins.
- **Peripheral (extrinsic) proteins:** Loosely attached to the inner or outer surface of the membrane.

### Cholesterol and Membrane Fluidity

Cholesterol is intercalated between phospholipid molecules in animal cell membranes.

- At high temperatures, it restricts excessive phospholipid movement and reduces membrane fluidity.
- At low temperatures, it prevents phospholipids from packing too closely, helping maintain membrane fluidity.

Therefore, cholesterol acts as a **fluidity buffer**.

### Membrane Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are generally present on the external surface of the plasma membrane.

- **Glycoproteins:** Carbohydrates attached to proteins.
- **Glycolipids:** Carbohydrates attached to lipids.

They contribute to cell-cell recognition, cell adhesion, and cell signaling.

## Mechanisms of Transport Across Membranes

Substances cross the plasma membrane through different transport mechanisms depending on their size, charge, concentration gradient, and energy requirements.

Transport across membranes is broadly classified into:

1. **Passive transport:** Movement down a concentration or electrochemical gradient without direct ATP expenditure.
2. **Active transport:** Movement against a concentration or electrochemical gradient using energy.

### Classification of Membrane Transport

| Transport Type | Direction of Movement | ATP Required? | Transport Protein Required? | Examples |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Simple Diffusion** | Down gradient | No | No | $\mathrm{O_2}$, $\mathrm{CO_2}$ |
| **Facilitated Diffusion** | Down gradient | No | Yes | Glucose through GLUT4, ions through channels |
| **Primary Active Transport** | Against gradient | Yes, directly | Yes | $\mathrm{Na^+/K^+}$ ATPase |
| **Secondary Active Transport** | One substance moves against its gradient, coupled to another moving down its gradient | Indirectly | Yes | Sodium-glucose symporter |

### Passive Transport

Passive transport is the movement of substances across a membrane down their concentration or electrochemical gradient without direct energy expenditure by the cell.

#### 1. Simple Diffusion

**Simple diffusion** is the movement of molecules directly through the phospholipid bilayer from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.

- Does not require ATP.
- Does not require transport proteins.
- Commonly occurs with small nonpolar or lipid-soluble molecules.

**Examples:**
- Oxygen ($\mathrm{O_2}$)
- Carbon dioxide ($\mathrm{CO_2}$)
- Lipid-soluble substances

#### 2. Facilitated Diffusion

**Facilitated diffusion** is the movement of substances down their concentration or electrochemical gradient through specific membrane transport proteins.

- Does not require ATP.
- Requires channel or carrier proteins.
- Allows polar molecules and ions to cross the membrane.

**Examples:**
- Glucose transport through GLUT4 carrier proteins.
- Ion movement through ion channels.

Facilitated diffusion is selective because transport proteins recognise particular substances.

### Active Transport

Active transport moves substances against their concentration or electrochemical gradient, requiring an energy source.

#### 1. Primary Active Transport

Primary active transport uses energy directly from ATP hydrolysis to move substances against their gradients.

**Example: Sodium-Potassium Pump ($\mathrm{Na^+/K^+}$ ATPase)**

The sodium-potassium pump is an important membrane protein found in animal cells.

For every ATP molecule hydrolysed, it:

- Moves **3 sodium ions ($3\mathrm{Na^+}$) out** of the cell.
- Moves **2 potassium ions ($2\mathrm{K^+}$) into** the cell.

This helps maintain ion concentration gradients and contributes to the electrical potential across the membrane.

#### 2. Secondary Active Transport

Secondary active transport uses the energy stored in an electrochemical gradient established by primary active transport.

- One substance moves down its electrochemical gradient.
- The energy released drives another substance against its gradient.
- ATP is not directly consumed by the cotransporter.

**Example: Sodium-Glucose Symporter**

The sodium-glucose symporter uses the inward movement of sodium ions down their electrochemical gradient to transport glucose into the cell against its concentration gradient.

## Osmosis and Tonicity

**Osmosis** is the net movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential.

The direction of water movement depends on the relative concentration of non-penetrating solutes on either side of the membrane.

### Types of Solutions Based on Tonicity

| Solution | Relative Solute Concentration Outside the Cell | Water Movement | Effect on Animal Cell |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Hypotonic** | Lower than inside | Water enters the cell | Cell swells and may burst |
| **Hypertonic** | Higher than inside | Water leaves the cell | Cell shrinks |
| **Isotonic** | Equal to inside | No net water movement | Cell maintains its normal size |

### Effects on Plant Cells

- **Hypotonic solution:** Water enters the cell, increasing turgor pressure. The cell becomes turgid.
- **Hypertonic solution:** Water leaves the cell, causing the plasma membrane to pull away from the cell wall. This is called **plasmolysis**.
- **Isotonic solution:** There is no net water movement, and the cell generally becomes flaccid.

## Bulk Transport Mechanisms

Bulk transport allows cells to move large particles, macromolecules, and substantial quantities of material using membrane-bound vesicles.

### 1. Endocytosis

**Endocytosis** is the process by which a cell takes substances into its interior through the inward folding of the plasma membrane, forming vesicles.

Major types include:

- **Phagocytosis:** Also called cellular eating. The cell engulfs large solid particles, such as bacteria or cellular debris.
- **Pinocytosis:** Also called cellular drinking. The cell takes in extracellular fluid and dissolved substances in small vesicles.

### 2. Exocytosis

**Exocytosis** is the process by which intracellular vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane and release their contents outside the cell.

**Examples:**
- Release of neurotransmitters from nerve cells.
- Secretion of hormones.
- Release of digestive enzymes.

## Common Exam Traps

- **Simple vs. Facilitated Diffusion:** Both occur down a concentration or electrochemical gradient without direct ATP expenditure. However, facilitated diffusion requires transport proteins, whereas simple diffusion does not.
- **Facilitated Diffusion Saturation:** Carrier-mediated facilitated diffusion reaches a maximum transport rate ($V_{\max}$) when all available carriers are occupied. Simple diffusion does not exhibit this carrier-dependent saturation.
- **Channel vs. Carrier Proteins:** Both facilitate passive transport, but carrier proteins bind substances and undergo conformational changes, whereas channel proteins provide pathways through the membrane.
- **Direction of the Sodium-Potassium Pump:** The $\mathrm{Na^+/K^+}$ ATPase moves **3 $\mathrm{Na^+}$ ions out** and **2 $\mathrm{K^+}$ ions in** per ATP molecule hydrolysed.
- **Primary vs. Secondary Active Transport:** Primary active transport uses ATP directly. Secondary active transport uses energy stored in an electrochemical gradient.
- **Hypotonic vs. Hypertonic Solutions:** Water enters a cell in a hypotonic solution and leaves a cell in a hypertonic solution.
- **Osmosis:** Water moves according to water potential, not simply toward the side with more total dissolved particles. Tonicity specifically depends on non-penetrating solutes.
- **Endocytosis vs. Exocytosis:** Endocytosis brings substances into the cell, whereas exocytosis releases substances outside the cell.

## Quiz

1. A human red blood cell is placed in an unknown solution. Within minutes, the cell swells and bursts (hemolysis). How should the surrounding solution be classified relative to the intracellular fluid?

- [ ] Hypertonic
- [x] Hypotonic
- [ ] Isotonic
- [ ] Saturated

> In a hypotonic solution, the surrounding fluid has a lower concentration of non-penetrating solutes than the intracellular fluid. Water enters the cell by osmosis, causing it to swell and potentially burst.

2. Which characteristic distinguishes carrier-mediated facilitated diffusion from simple diffusion across a plasma membrane?

- [ ] Facilitated diffusion moves solutes against their concentration gradient.
- [ ] Facilitated diffusion requires direct metabolic consumption of ATP.
- [x] Facilitated diffusion exhibits saturation kinetics ($V_{\max}$) as solute concentration increases.
- [ ] Facilitated diffusion occurs exclusively through the lipid bilayer without membrane proteins.

> Carrier-mediated facilitated diffusion relies on a limited number of transport proteins. Its rate reaches a maximum ($V_{\max}$) when the available carriers become saturated. Simple diffusion does not exhibit this carrier-dependent saturation.

3. The $\mathrm{Na^+/K^+}$ ATPase pump operates continuously in nerve cell membranes. What is the stoichiometry and direction of ion transport per molecule of ATP hydrolysed?

- [ ] 2 $\mathrm{Na^+}$ ions inward; 3 $\mathrm{K^+}$ ions outward
- [ ] 3 $\mathrm{Na^+}$ ions inward; 2 $\mathrm{K^+}$ ions outward
- [x] 3 $\mathrm{Na^+}$ ions outward; 2 $\mathrm{K^+}$ ions inward
- [ ] 3 $\mathrm{Na^+}$ ions outward; 3 $\mathrm{K^+}$ ions inward

> The primary active $\mathrm{Na^+/K^+}$ ATPase pump expels 3 sodium ions from the cell and imports 2 potassium ions into the cell for every ATP molecule hydrolysed.
