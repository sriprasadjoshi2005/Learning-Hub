title: Structural Organisation in Animals
level: intermediate
---

Animal tissues are groups of similar cells that work together to perform specific functions. The bodies of animals are organised into four basic tissue types: **Epithelial**, **Connective**, **Muscular**, and **Neural** tissues. Each tissue has specialised structural features that enable it to perform particular functions.

## Major Animal Tissue Types

| Tissue Class | Key Characteristics | Sub-types | Key Functions / Locations |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Epithelial** | Tightly packed cells with very little intercellular matrix; possesses a free surface facing a body cavity, fluid, or external environment | Simple (Squamous, Cuboidal, Columnar, Ciliated) and Compound | Protection, absorption, secretion; alveolar lining, kidney tubules, skin |
| **Connective** | Most abundant and widely distributed tissue; cells are embedded in an extracellular matrix | Loose (Areolar, Adipose), Dense (Tendons, Ligaments), Specialised (Cartilage, Bone, Blood) | Structural support, binding, protection, and transport; tendons connect muscles to bones, ligaments connect bones to bones |
| **Muscular** | Made of elongated contractile fibres containing myofilaments (actin and myosin) | Skeletal (Striated, Voluntary), Smooth (Unstriated, Involuntary), Cardiac (Striated, Involuntary) | Movement, locomotion, and blood pumping; intercalated discs are present in cardiac muscle |
| **Neural** | Consists of specialised excitable cells called neurons, supported by neuroglial cells | Neurons and Neuroglia | Transmission of nerve impulses and coordination; brain, spinal cord, and nerves |

## 1. Epithelial Tissue

Epithelial tissue forms the protective outer covering of the body and lines internal organs, body cavities, and ducts. Its cells are closely packed together with very little intercellular material.

**Major characteristics:**

- Cells are tightly packed and arranged in continuous sheets.
- One surface is usually free, while the opposite surface rests on a basement membrane.
- It is generally avascular, meaning it does not contain blood vessels.
- It performs protection, absorption, secretion, and filtration.

### Classification of Epithelial Tissue

Epithelial tissue is classified into simple and compound epithelium based on the number of cell layers.

| Type | Structure | Major Functions / Locations |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Simple Squamous** | Single layer of thin, flat cells | Diffusion and filtration; alveoli of lungs and blood capillaries |
| **Simple Cuboidal** | Single layer of cube-shaped cells | Secretion and absorption; kidney tubules and gland ducts |
| **Simple Columnar** | Single layer of tall, pillar-like cells | Absorption and secretion; lining of the intestine |
| **Ciliated Epithelium** | Epithelial cells bearing hair-like cilia | Movement of materials; respiratory tract and oviducts |
| **Compound Epithelium** | Multiple layers of cells | Protection against mechanical and chemical stress; skin and lining of the mouth |

## 2. Connective Tissue

Connective tissue connects, supports, binds, and protects different structures of the body. It is the most abundant and widely distributed tissue type in animals.

**Major characteristics:**

- Cells are separated by an extracellular matrix.
- The matrix may be fluid, semi-solid, or solid.
- The matrix contains fibres such as collagen and elastin in many connective tissues.
- It performs structural support, transport, storage, and protection.

### Classification of Connective Tissue

| Type | Sub-types / Examples | Major Functions |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Loose Connective Tissue** | Areolar, Adipose | Packing, binding, cushioning, and fat storage |
| **Dense Connective Tissue** | Tendons, Ligaments | Strong attachment and support |
| **Specialised Connective Tissue** | Cartilage, Bone, Blood | Support, protection, and transport |

### Important Connective Tissues

**Areolar Tissue:**

- A loose connective tissue found between the skin and muscles and around blood vessels and nerves.
- Acts as packing material between organs.
- Supports and binds internal structures.

**Adipose Tissue:**

- Consists mainly of fat-storing cells called adipocytes.
- Stores energy in the form of fat.
- Provides insulation and cushioning around internal organs.

**Tendons:**

- Dense regular connective tissues made of closely packed collagen fibres.
- Connect skeletal muscles to bones.
- Provide strong resistance to pulling forces.

**Ligaments:**

- Dense connective tissues containing abundant elastic fibres and collagen.
- Connect bones to other bones at joints.
- Provide stability while allowing controlled movement.

**Cartilage:**

- A specialised connective tissue with a firm but flexible matrix.
- Contains cells called chondrocytes.
- Provides support and reduces friction at joints.

**Bone:**

- A hard connective tissue with a mineralised extracellular matrix.
- Provides structural support and protection.
- Helps in movement and serves as a storage site for minerals such as calcium and phosphorus.

**Blood:**

- A fluid connective tissue.
- Its matrix is called plasma.
- Transports respiratory gases, nutrients, hormones, and waste products.
- Contains red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets.

## 3. Muscular Tissue

Muscular tissue consists of elongated cells or fibres containing contractile proteins called **actin** and **myosin**. Their contraction and relaxation produce movement.

### Types of Muscular Tissue

| Feature | Skeletal Muscle | Smooth Muscle | Cardiac Muscle |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Appearance | Striated | Non-striated | Striated |
| Control | Voluntary | Involuntary | Involuntary |
| Cell Shape | Long, cylindrical fibres | Spindle-shaped cells | Short, branched cells |
| Nuclei | Multiple, usually peripheral | Single, central | Usually one, sometimes two |
| Intercalated Discs | Absent | Absent | Present |
| Location | Attached to bones | Walls of internal organs and blood vessels | Wall of the heart |
| Major Function | Body movement and locomotion | Movement of substances through internal organs | Pumping blood |

### 1. Skeletal Muscle

- Consists of long, cylindrical, unbranched fibres.
- Fibres are multinucleated and striated.
- Usually operates under voluntary control.
- Responsible for locomotion, posture, and movement of body parts.

### 2. Smooth Muscle

- Consists of spindle-shaped cells with a single central nucleus.
- Lacks visible striations.
- Operates under involuntary control.
- Found in the walls of the digestive tract, blood vessels, urinary bladder, and other internal organs.
- Produces movements such as intestinal peristalsis.

### 3. Cardiac Muscle

- Found exclusively in the wall of the heart.
- Consists of branched, striated muscle fibres.
- Usually contains a single centrally located nucleus.
- Operates involuntarily and contracts rhythmically.
- Contains specialised junctions called **intercalated discs**, which help coordinate contraction between neighbouring cardiac muscle cells.

## 4. Neural Tissue

Neural tissue is specialised for receiving stimuli, generating electrical signals, and transmitting information throughout the body. It forms the major components of the nervous system.

**Major components:**

- **Neurons**: Structural and functional units of the nervous system that transmit nerve impulses.
- **Neuroglia**: Supporting cells that protect, nourish, and maintain the environment around neurons.

### Structure of a Neuron

A typical neuron consists of three major parts:

1. **Cell Body (Cyton):** Contains the nucleus and cytoplasm and controls the metabolic activities of the neuron.
2. **Dendrites:** Short, branched extensions that receive signals and carry them towards the cell body.
3. **Axon:** A long extension that carries nerve impulses away from the cell body towards another neuron, muscle, or gland.

**Major functions:**

- Receive information from internal and external environments.
- Transmit electrical impulses.
- Coordinate muscular activity and glandular secretion.
- Enable sensory perception, reflexes, and other nervous system functions.

## Cell Junctions in Epithelial Tissues

Adjacent epithelial cells are connected by specialised structures called **cell junctions**. These junctions maintain tissue integrity and allow communication between neighbouring cells.

### 1. Tight Junctions

- Form close seals between adjacent epithelial cells.
- Prevent leakage of substances through the spaces between cells.
- Help maintain selective barriers in epithelial tissues.

### 2. Adhering Junctions

- Mechanically attach neighbouring cells to one another.
- Help keep adjacent epithelial cells firmly connected.
- Provide structural strength and maintain tissue integrity.

### 3. Gap Junctions

- Form communication channels between neighbouring cells.
- Connect the cytoplasm of adjacent cells.
- Allow the transfer of ions and small molecules.
- Help coordinate cellular activities, particularly in tissues such as cardiac muscle.

## Common Exam Traps

- **Tendon vs Ligament:** Tendons connect skeletal muscles to bones, whereas ligaments connect bones to other bones.
- **Skeletal Muscle:** Skeletal muscle is striated and voluntary, with long cylindrical multinucleated fibres.
- **Smooth Muscle:** Smooth muscle is non-striated and involuntary, with spindle-shaped cells.
- **Cardiac Muscle:** Cardiac muscle is striated and involuntary, with branched fibres and intercalated discs.
- **Intercalated Discs:** These specialised structures are characteristic of cardiac muscle and help coordinate contractions.
- **Tight Junctions:** Prevent leakage of substances across epithelial cell layers.
- **Adhering Junctions:** Hold neighbouring cells together mechanically.
- **Gap Junctions:** Allow communication through the transfer of ions and small molecules.
- **Connective Tissue:** Unlike epithelial tissue, connective tissue generally contains abundant extracellular matrix.
- **Neurons vs Neuroglia:** Neurons transmit nerve impulses, while neuroglial cells provide support and protection.

- [ ] Smooth muscle tissue
- [x] Skeletal muscle tissue
- [ ] Cardiac muscle tissue
- [ ] Dense irregular connective tissue

> Skeletal muscle tissue consists of long, cylindrical, striated, multinucleated fibres that generally operate under voluntary motor control.

2. Which intercellular epithelial junction functions specifically to prevent fluid from leaking across a tissue barrier into surrounding intercellular spaces?

- [x] Tight junction
- [ ] Adhering junction
- [ ] Gap junction
- [ ] Desmosome

> Tight junctions form seals between adjacent epithelial cells, restricting the passage of substances through the spaces between them.

3. Which specialised connective tissue structure connects skeletal muscles directly to bones?

- [ ] Ligament
- [x] Tendon
- [ ] Cartilage
- [ ] Areolar tissue

> Tendons are dense regular connective tissues composed mainly of parallel collagen bundles that attach skeletal muscles to bones. Ligaments, in contrast, connect bones to other bones.
