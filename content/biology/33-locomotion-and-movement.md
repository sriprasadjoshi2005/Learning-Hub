Movement is a fundamental feature of living organisms. In humans, locomotion is achieved through the coordinated action of the **skeletal system**, **joints**, and **skeletal muscles**. The adult human skeleton consists of approximately **206 bones**, while muscle contraction provides the force required for movement.

## Human Skeletal Division

The human skeleton is divided into two major parts: the **Axial Skeleton** and the **Appendicular Skeleton**.

    Human Skeleton (206 Bones)
                  |
    -----------------------------------------
    |                                       |
    Axial Skeleton                     Appendicular Skeleton
    (80 Bones)                         (126 Bones)
    |                                       |
    Skull, Vertebral Column,           Pectoral Girdle,
    Sternum and Ribs                   Pelvic Girdle,
                                       Upper and Lower Limbs

| Skeletal Division | Number of Bones | Major Components |
|---|---:|---|
| **Axial Skeleton** | 80 | Skull, vertebral column, sternum and ribs |
| **Appendicular Skeleton** | 126 | Pectoral girdle, pelvic girdle, and limb bones |
| **Total** | **206** | Complete adult human skeleton |

## Types of Joints

Joints are points of contact between bones or between bones and cartilages. They are classified according to their structure and degree of movement.

| Joint Type | Movement | Main Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fibrous / Synarthrosis** | Immovable | Bones are connected by dense fibrous connective tissue. | Sutures of the skull |
| **Cartilaginous / Amphiarthrosis** | Slightly movable | Bones are connected by cartilage. | Intervertebral discs, pubic symphysis |
| **Synovial / Diarthrosis** | Freely movable | Contains a fluid-filled synovial cavity. | Shoulder, elbow, hip and knee |

### Types of Synovial Joints

Synovial joints allow a wide range of movements and are classified according to the type of movement they permit.

- **Ball and Socket Joint**: Permits movement in multiple axes, including rotation. Examples: Shoulder and hip joints.
- **Hinge Joint**: Permits movement mainly in one plane. Examples: Elbow and knee joints.
- **Pivot Joint**: Allows rotational movement around a central axis. Example: Joint between the atlas and axis vertebrae.
- **Gliding Joint**: Permits bones to slide over one another. Example: Intercarpal joints.
- **Saddle Joint**: Allows movement in two planes with a greater range than a simple gliding joint. Example: Carpometacarpal joint of the thumb.

## Microscopic Structure of Skeletal Muscle

Skeletal muscles are composed of elongated muscle fibres. Each muscle fibre contains numerous myofibrils, which are made up of repeating functional contractile units called **Sarcomeres**.

A sarcomere extends from one **Z-line to the next Z-line**.

### Structure of a Sarcomere

| Component | Composition / Structure | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Z-line** | Protein structures anchoring thin filaments. | Marks the boundaries of a sarcomere. |
| **I-band** | Thin filaments (Actin) only. | Light band; shortens during contraction. |
| **A-band** | Entire length of thick filaments (Myosin), including overlapping regions. | Dark band; remains constant in length. |
| **H-zone** | Thick filaments (Myosin) only. | Central region of the A-band; narrows during contraction. |
| **M-line** | Proteins that hold thick filaments together. | Located at the centre of the sarcomere. |

### Thin and Thick Filaments

**1. Thin Filaments (Actin)**

Thin filaments consist of three major components:

- **F-actin**: Filamentous actin formed by the polymerisation of globular G-actin monomers. Each G-actin contains an active site for myosin binding.
- **Tropomyosin**: A regulatory protein that covers the myosin-binding sites on actin when the muscle is relaxed.
- **Troponin**: A regulatory protein complex that controls the position of tropomyosin in response to calcium ions.

The three subunits of troponin are:

| Subunit | Function |
|---|---|
| **Troponin T (TnT)** | Binds troponin to tropomyosin. |
| **Troponin I (TnI)** | Inhibits actin-myosin interaction in the relaxed state. |
| **Troponin C (TnC)** | Binds calcium ions ($\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$). |

**2. Thick Filaments (Myosin)**

Thick filaments are composed of polymerised myosin molecules.

Each myosin molecule contains:

- **Heavy Meromyosin (HMM)**: Forms the globular head and short arm. The head contains binding sites for ATP and actin and possesses ATPase activity.
- **Light Meromyosin (LMM)**: Forms the long tail portion, contributing to the formation of the thick filament backbone.

## Sliding Filament Theory of Muscle Contraction

The Sliding Filament Theory, proposed by **Huxley and Hanson**, explains muscle contraction through the sliding of thin actin filaments over thick myosin filaments.

The filaments themselves do not significantly shorten; instead, their degree of overlap increases, causing the sarcomere to shorten.

### Mechanism of Muscle Contraction

1. **Neuromuscular Transmission**: A nerve impulse reaches the neuromuscular junction, triggering the release of **Acetylcholine (ACh)**.
2. **Calcium Release**: The action potential spreads along the muscle fibre membrane and through the T-tubules, stimulating the sarcoplasmic reticulum to release $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ into the sarcoplasm.
3. **Exposure of Binding Sites**: Calcium binds to **Troponin C**, causing a conformational change that moves tropomyosin away from the myosin-binding sites on actin.
4. **Cross-Bridge Formation**: The energised myosin head, carrying ADP and inorganic phosphate ($\mathrm{P_i}$), binds to an exposed active site on actin.
5. **Power Stroke**: Release of $\mathrm{P_i}$ and subsequently ADP causes the myosin head to pivot, pulling the actin filament towards the centre of the sarcomere.
6. **Cross-Bridge Detachment**: A new ATP molecule binds to the myosin head, causing it to detach from actin.
7. **Reactivation of Myosin**: ATP is hydrolysed into ADP and $\mathrm{P_i}$, re-energising the myosin head for another contraction cycle.

### Changes During Muscle Contraction

As thin filaments slide towards the centre of the sarcomere, the Z-lines move closer together.

| Sarcomere Component | Change During Contraction |
|---|---|
| **Sarcomere** | Shortens |
| **I-band** | Shortens |
| **H-zone** | Narrows and may disappear |
| **A-band** | Remains unchanged |
| **Z-lines** | Move closer together |
| **Actin-Myosin Overlap** | Increases |

### Muscle Relaxation

Muscle relaxation occurs when stimulation stops and calcium ions are actively transported back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

- Calcium concentration in the sarcoplasm decreases.
- Calcium dissociates from Troponin C.
- Tropomyosin returns to its blocking position over the myosin-binding sites on actin.
- Cross-bridge formation stops, and the muscle fibre relaxes.

ATP is essential for both muscle contraction and relaxation because it enables myosin detachment and powers calcium reuptake into the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

## Common Exam Traps

- **Axial vs Appendicular Skeleton**: The axial skeleton contains **80 bones**, whereas the appendicular skeleton contains **126 bones**.
- **Joint Classification**: Fibrous joints are generally immovable, cartilaginous joints are slightly movable, and synovial joints are freely movable.
- **Sarcomere Boundaries**: A sarcomere extends from one Z-line to the next Z-line, not from one M-line to another.
- **Band Behaviour During Contraction**: The **A-band remains constant in length**. The I-band shortens, while the H-zone narrows or disappears.
- **Calcium Binding**: Calcium ions bind directly to **Troponin C**, not to tropomyosin or actin.
- **ATP Function**: ATP binding causes the myosin head to detach from actin. ATP hydrolysis re-energises the myosin head.
- **Myosin Structure**: Heavy Meromyosin forms the head and short arm, whereas Light Meromyosin forms the tail.
- **Joint Examples**: The shoulder and hip are ball-and-socket joints, while the elbow is a hinge joint.

## Quiz

1. According to the Sliding Filament Theory of skeletal muscle contraction, what structural change occurs to the length of the anisotropic A-band when a sarcomere shortens?

   - [ ] A-band shortens proportionally to the I-band.
   - [ ] A-band expands as filaments overlap.
   - [x] A-band length remains completely unchanged.
   - [ ] A-band completely disappears.

   > During muscle contraction, thin actin filaments slide over thick myosin filaments towards the centre of the sarcomere. The length of the thick filaments remains constant, so the A-band does not change. The I-band and H-zone shorten.

2. During excitation-contraction coupling in skeletal muscle, calcium ions ($\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$) released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum bind directly to which regulatory protein?

   - [ ] Tropomyosin
   - [x] Troponin (subunit Troponin C)
   - [ ] F-actin
   - [ ] Myosin light chain

   > Calcium binds to Troponin C on the thin filaments, producing a conformational change that moves tropomyosin away from the myosin-binding sites on actin.

3. Which type of joint permits free movement in multiple axes and is illustrated by the articulation between the head of the humerus and the glenoid cavity of the pectoral girdle?

   - [x] Ball and Socket Synovial Joint
   - [ ] Hinge Synovial Joint
   - [ ] Cartilaginous Joint
   - [ ] Pivot Joint

   > The shoulder joint is a ball-and-socket synovial joint. It permits movement in multiple axes, including flexion, extension, abduction, adduction and rotation.

4. Which of the following structures marks the boundary of a sarcomere?

   - [ ] M-line
   - [ ] H-zone
   - [x] Z-line
   - [ ] A-band

   > A sarcomere is the functional contractile unit of a myofibril extending from one Z-line to the next. The Z-lines anchor the thin filaments and move closer together during contraction.

5. What is the primary function of ATP binding to the myosin head during the cross-bridge cycle?

   - [ ] It exposes the active sites on actin.
   - [ ] It binds calcium to Troponin C.
   - [x] It causes the myosin head to detach from actin.
   - [ ] It causes the sarcoplasmic reticulum to release calcium.

   > ATP binding to the myosin head reduces its affinity for actin, causing cross-bridge detachment. Subsequent ATP hydrolysis re-energises the myosin head for another contraction cycle.
