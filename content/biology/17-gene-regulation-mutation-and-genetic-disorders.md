Gene expression is regulated to help organisms respond to environmental changes and enable cellular differentiation. Changes in genetic sequences, known as **mutations**, can alter protein structure or function and may lead to metabolic abnormalities or inherited genetic disorders.

## Regulation of Gene Expression: The Lac Operon

The **Lac Operon**, studied by François Jacob and Jacques Monod, is a gene-regulatory system found in *Escherichia coli* (*E. coli*). It controls the expression of genes involved in lactose metabolism.

It is an **inducible operon** that is normally switched off but becomes active when lactose is available.

### Organisation of the Lac Operon

The main components of the lac operon include regulatory and structural regions:

$$
\text{Promoter (P)} - \text{Operator (O)} - \text{lacZ} - \text{lacY} - \text{lacA}
$$

| Component | Function |
| ----- | ----- |
| **Regulatory gene ($i$)** | Produces the repressor protein that controls the operon. |
| **Promoter (P)** | Binding site for RNA polymerase to initiate transcription. |
| **Operator (O)** | Regulatory DNA sequence where the repressor binds. |
| **lacZ** | Encodes $\beta$-galactosidase, which breaks down lactose into glucose and galactose. |
| **lacY** | Encodes permease, which helps transport lactose into the bacterial cell. |
| **lacA** | Encodes transacetylase. |

### Mechanism of Lac Operon Regulation

The lac operon is regulated according to the availability of lactose.

**1. In the Absence of Lactose**

- The regulatory gene ($i$) produces an active repressor protein.
- The repressor binds to the operator region.
- This prevents RNA polymerase from effectively transcribing the structural genes.
- The operon remains switched off.

**2. In the Presence of Lactose**

- A small amount of lactose enters the bacterial cell.
- Some lactose is converted into **allolactose**, which acts as the inducer.
- Allolactose binds to the repressor protein and changes its shape.
- The repressor loses its ability to bind to the operator.
- RNA polymerase can transcribe the structural genes ($lacZ$, $lacY$ and $lacA$).

<text weight="semibold">Summary of Lac Operon Regulation</text>

| Condition | Repressor State | Operon Activity |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Lactose absent | Active; binds operator | Switched off |
| Lactose present | Inactivated by allolactose | Transcription permitted |

## Types of Gene Mutations

A **mutation** is a sudden, heritable change in the nucleotide sequence of genetic material. Mutations can alter the structure or function of proteins and may affect an organism's phenotype.

### Point Mutation

A **point mutation** involves a change in a single nucleotide or base pair in DNA.

- It may involve the substitution of one nitrogenous base with another.
- Depending on the resulting codon, it can produce a different amino acid, a stop codon or no amino acid change.
- Example: Sickle-cell anaemia results from a single base substitution in the $\beta$-globin gene.

**Sickle-cell mutation:**

$$
\text{Normal mRNA: GAG}
$$

$$
\text{Mutant mRNA: GUG}
$$

This changes the sixth amino acid of the $\beta$-globin chain from **Glutamic acid** to **Valine**.

### Frameshift Mutation

A **frameshift mutation** occurs when nucleotides are inserted or deleted in numbers that are not multiples of three.

- It changes the reading frame of the genetic code.
- All subsequent codons may be altered.
- It can produce a significantly different protein or an early stop codon.

| Mutation Type | Mechanism | Effect |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Point Mutation** | Substitution of a single nucleotide | May change one amino acid or have no effect on the protein sequence |
| **Frameshift Mutation** | Insertion or deletion of nucleotides not in multiples of three | Alters the reading frame and downstream amino acid sequence |

## Human Genetic Disorders

Genetic disorders are broadly classified into **Mendelian disorders** and **chromosomal disorders**.

### Mendelian Disorders

Mendelian disorders result from mutations in individual genes and generally follow Mendelian inheritance patterns.

| Disorder | Mode of Inheritance | Cause / Characteristic |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Haemophilia** | X-linked recessive | Deficiency or dysfunction of blood-clotting factor VIII or IX, causing prolonged bleeding. |
| **Phenylketonuria (PKU)** | Autosomal recessive | Deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase, causing accumulation of phenylalanine. |
| **Thalassemia** | Autosomal recessive | Reduced or absent synthesis of $\alpha$- or $\beta$-globin chains. |
| **Sickle-cell Anaemia** | Autosomal recessive | Abnormal haemoglobin caused by substitution of Glutamic acid with Valine in the $\beta$-globin chain. |

### Chromosomal Disorders

Chromosomal disorders result from abnormalities in chromosome number or structure. Numerical abnormalities often arise due to **non-disjunction**, the failure of chromosomes or chromatids to separate properly during cell division.

| Disorder | Chromosomal Abnormality | Key Characteristics |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Down Syndrome** | Trisomy 21 ($47$ chromosomes) | Intellectual disability, characteristic facial features and a single palmar crease in some individuals. |
| **Klinefelter Syndrome** | $47,XXY$ | Affects males; commonly associated with small testes, infertility and gynaecomastia. |
| **Turner Syndrome** | $45,X$ | Affects females; commonly associated with short stature, underdeveloped ovaries and infertility. |

### Important Chromosomal Conditions

**1. Down Syndrome**

- Caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21.
- Also known as **Trisomy 21**.
- Usually results from non-disjunction during meiosis.
- Individuals may have characteristic facial features, developmental differences and intellectual disability.

$$
2n+1=47
$$

**2. Klinefelter Syndrome**

- Caused by an additional X chromosome in males.
- Chromosomal constitution: $47,XXY$.
- Common characteristics include small testes, reduced fertility and gynaecomastia.

**3. Turner Syndrome**

- Caused by the absence of one sex chromosome.
- Chromosomal constitution: $45,X$.
- Common characteristics include short stature, underdeveloped ovaries and infertility.

## Common Exam Traps

- **Sickle-cell Anaemia vs Thalassemia**: Sickle-cell anaemia is a **qualitative defect** in haemoglobin, involving an abnormal amino acid substitution. Thalassemia is a **quantitative defect**, involving reduced synthesis of normal globin chains.
- **Inducer in the Lac Operon**: Allolactose, rather than the repressor, acts as the inducer. It binds to and inactivates the repressor.
- **Lac Operon Without Lactose**: The repressor binds to the operator, preventing transcription of the structural genes.
- **Frameshift Mutation**: Insertions or deletions cause a frameshift only when the number of nucleotides added or removed is not a multiple of three.
- **Down Syndrome**: It is an autosomal aneuploidy caused by trisomy of chromosome 21, not a sex-chromosome abnormality.
- **Klinefelter vs Turner Syndrome**: Klinefelter syndrome has the chromosomal constitution $47,XXY$, whereas Turner syndrome has $45,X$.
- **Phenylketonuria**: The deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase causes phenylalanine accumulation. Early dietary management can prevent or reduce serious complications.

## Quiz

1. In the *E. coli* lac operon, what happens when allolactose binds to the repressor protein?

- [ ] The repressor binds more tightly to the operator, stopping transcription.
- [x] The repressor changes shape and loses its ability to bind to the operator, allowing transcription.
- [ ] RNA polymerase is prevented from binding to the promoter.
- [ ] The $lacZ$ gene undergoes a frameshift mutation.

> Allolactose acts as an inducer. It binds to the repressor protein and causes a conformational change that prevents the repressor from binding to the operator. This allows RNA polymerase to transcribe the structural genes.

2. A child has an extra copy of chromosome 21, giving a total of 47 chromosomes. Which chromosomal abnormality is responsible?

- [ ] Monosomy of sex chromosomes
- [ ] Nullisomy of autosomes
- [x] Autosomal trisomy
- [ ] Sex-chromosome aneuploidy

> Down syndrome is caused by trisomy of chromosome 21. The affected individual has three copies of chromosome 21 instead of the normal two, resulting in a total of 47 chromosomes.

3. Sickle-cell anaemia results from the substitution of Glutamic acid with Valine at the sixth position of the $\beta$-globin chain. Which change occurs in the corresponding mRNA codon?

- [ ] $\text{AUG} \rightarrow \text{UAG}$
- [x] $\text{GAG} \rightarrow \text{GUG}$
- [ ] $\text{UAA} \rightarrow \text{UGA}$
- [ ] $\text{CUC} \rightarrow \text{CAC}$

> The mutation changes the sixth codon of the $\beta$-globin mRNA from GAG, which codes for Glutamic acid, to GUG, which codes for Valine. This is a point mutation involving a single nucleotide substitution.

4. Which enzyme is deficient in individuals with Phenylketonuria (PKU)?

- [ ] Amylase
- [ ] Lactase
- [x] Phenylalanine hydroxylase
- [ ] DNA polymerase

> Phenylketonuria is an autosomal recessive disorder caused most commonly by a deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase. This prevents normal conversion of phenylalanine into tyrosine, resulting in phenylalanine accumulation.

5. Which chromosomal constitution is associated with Klinefelter syndrome?

- [ ] $45,X$
- [x] $47,XXY$
- [ ] $47,XXX$
- [ ] $47,XYY$

> Klinefelter syndrome is caused by an additional X chromosome in an individual with a male phenotype. The characteristic chromosomal constitution is $47,XXY$.

6. An insertion mutation adds two nucleotides to a coding DNA sequence. What is the most likely consequence?

- [ ] No change in the reading frame
- [ ] Removal of an amino acid without affecting downstream codons
- [x] A frameshift affecting downstream codons
- [ ] Formation of an additional chromosome

> Since two is not a multiple of three, the insertion changes the reading frame. Consequently, the downstream codons may be altered, potentially producing a non-functional protein.
