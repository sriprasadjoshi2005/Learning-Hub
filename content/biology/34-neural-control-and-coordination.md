The nervous system controls and coordinates rapid communication throughout the body through **electrical impulses (action potentials)** and **chemical transmission across synapses**. It enables organisms to detect stimuli, process information, and produce appropriate responses.

## Structure of a Neuron

A **neuron** is the structural and functional unit of the nervous system. It is specialised to receive, process, and transmit nerve impulses.

### Major Parts of a Neuron

1. **Cell Body (Soma)**: Contains the nucleus, cytoplasm, and cell organelles. It also contains **Nissl's granules**, which are aggregates of rough endoplasmic reticulum and ribosomes involved in protein synthesis.
2. **Dendrites**: Short, branched processes that receive signals from other neurons or receptors and conduct them towards the cell body.
3. **Axon**: A long process that carries nerve impulses away from the cell body towards another neuron, muscle, or gland.

### Myelinated and Non-Myelinated Axons

| Feature | Myelinated Axon | Non-Myelinated Axon |
|---|---|---|
| **Myelin Sheath** | Present | Absent |
| **Nodes of Ranvier** | Present | Absent as typical myelin-associated gaps |
| **Impulse Conduction** | Saltatory conduction | Continuous conduction |
| **Conduction Speed** | Generally faster | Generally slower |

- **Myelin Sheath**: A lipid-rich insulating covering around certain axons. In the peripheral nervous system, it is formed by Schwann cells.
- **Nodes of Ranvier**: Gaps between adjacent myelin sheath segments where the axonal membrane is exposed.
- **Saltatory Conduction**: The nerve impulse appears to jump from one Node of Ranvier to the next, increasing conduction speed.

## Nerve Impulse Generation and Conduction

A nerve impulse is an **action potential**, which is a rapid, temporary change in the electrical potential across the neuronal membrane.

### Stages of an Action Potential

    1. Resting State              2. Depolarization
       (~ -70 mV)                    (up to ~ +30 mV)
       |                             |
       High K+ inside                Voltage-gated Na+ channels open
       High Na+ outside              Na+ rapidly enters
       |                             |
       ------------------------------|
                                     |
    3. Repolarization             4. Recovery
       |                             |
       Na+ channels inactivate       Ion gradients restored
       K+ channels open              by Na+/K+ pump
       K+ moves outside

### 1. Resting Membrane Potential

When a neuron is not transmitting an impulse, its membrane is said to be **polarized**.

- The inside of the axon is negatively charged relative to the outside.
- The resting membrane potential is approximately $-70\text{ mV}$.
- Potassium ion concentration ($\mathrm{K^+}$) is higher inside the neuron.
- Sodium ion concentration ($\mathrm{Na^+}$) is higher outside the neuron.
- The membrane is more permeable to $\mathrm{K^+}$ than to $\mathrm{Na^+}$ at rest.

The **Sodium-Potassium Pump** ($\mathrm{Na^+/K^+}$ ATPase) actively transports:

- **3 $\mathrm{Na^+}$ ions out** of the neuron.
- **2 $\mathrm{K^+}$ ions into** the neuron.

This pump uses ATP and helps maintain the ionic concentration gradients required for neuronal activity.

### 2. Depolarization

When a stimulus reaches the threshold potential (approximately $-55\text{ mV}$):

- Voltage-gated $\mathrm{Na^+}$ channels open.
- Sodium ions rapidly enter the axon.
- The inside becomes progressively positive relative to the outside.
- The membrane potential may reach approximately $+30\text{ mV}$.

This reversal of membrane polarity is called **Depolarization**.

### 3. Repolarization

After depolarization:

- Voltage-gated $\mathrm{Na^+}$ channels become inactivated.
- Voltage-gated $\mathrm{K^+}$ channels open.
- Potassium ions move out of the axon.
- The inside becomes negative again relative to the outside.

This restoration of negative membrane potential is called **Repolarization**.

### 4. Restoration of Ionic Gradients

The sodium-potassium pump maintains and restores the concentration gradients of sodium and potassium ions over time. The membrane may briefly become more negative than its resting potential, a phase called **Hyperpolarization**, before returning to the resting state.

## Synaptic Transmission

A **synapse** is the functional junction between two neurons or between a neuron and an effector cell such as a muscle or gland.

Synapses are broadly classified into:

- **Chemical Synapses**: Transmit signals through neurotransmitters.
- **Electrical Synapses**: Transmit electrical signals directly between cells through gap junctions.

### Chemical Synaptic Transmission

A chemical synapse contains three major components:

1. **Presynaptic Terminal**: Contains synaptic vesicles filled with neurotransmitters.
2. **Synaptic Cleft**: Narrow gap between the presynaptic and postsynaptic membranes.
3. **Postsynaptic Membrane**: Contains receptors that bind neurotransmitters.

#### Mechanism of Chemical Synaptic Transmission

1. An action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal.
2. Voltage-gated $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ channels open, allowing calcium ions to enter.
3. Calcium triggers synaptic vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane.
4. Neurotransmitters such as **Acetylcholine (ACh)** are released into the synaptic cleft through exocytosis.
5. Neurotransmitters diffuse across the cleft and bind to specific receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.
6. Receptor activation changes the permeability of the postsynaptic membrane to ions, producing a postsynaptic response.
7. Neurotransmitters are removed through enzymatic breakdown, reuptake, or diffusion.

### Chemical vs Electrical Synapses

| Feature | Chemical Synapse | Electrical Synapse |
|---|---|---|
| **Signal Transmission** | Through neurotransmitters | Through direct ionic current |
| **Gap Between Cells** | Synaptic cleft | Gap junction channels |
| **Direction** | Usually unidirectional | Often bidirectional |
| **Speed** | Slight synaptic delay | Very rapid |
| **Mechanism** | Chemical signalling | Direct electrical coupling |

## Human Brain Division

The human brain is the central control centre of the nervous system. It is protected by the **cranium**, three protective membranes called **meninges**, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).

The brain is broadly divided into three major regions:

### 1. Forebrain

The forebrain consists mainly of the cerebrum, thalamus, and hypothalamus.

- **Cerebrum**: Largest part of the brain. Responsible for sensory perception, voluntary motor activity, intelligence, memory, learning, reasoning, and conscious thought.
- **Thalamus**: Major relay station for sensory and motor information. Most sensory signals are relayed through the thalamus before reaching the cerebral cortex.
- **Hypothalamus**: Regulates body temperature, hunger, thirst, sleep, and emotional responses. It also controls pituitary gland activity and helps maintain homeostasis.

### 2. Midbrain

The midbrain connects the forebrain and hindbrain.

- Contains the **Corpora Quadrigemina**, consisting of four rounded elevations.
- The superior colliculi are involved in visual reflexes.
- The inferior colliculi are involved in auditory reflexes.
- Participates in the control of certain eye movements and auditory responses.

### 3. Hindbrain

The hindbrain consists of the cerebellum, pons, and medulla oblongata.

- **Cerebellum**: Coordinates voluntary muscular movements and maintains posture, balance, and equilibrium.
- **Pons**: Contains fibre tracts connecting different regions of the brain and contributes to the regulation of respiration.
- **Medulla Oblongata**: Contains vital autonomic centres regulating cardiovascular activity, respiration, and gastric secretions.

### Summary of Brain Regions

| Brain Region | Major Structures | Principal Functions |
|---|---|---|
| **Forebrain** | Cerebrum, Thalamus, Hypothalamus | Intelligence, sensory processing, voluntary movement, homeostasis |
| **Midbrain** | Corpora Quadrigemina | Visual and auditory reflexes |
| **Hindbrain** | Cerebellum, Pons, Medulla Oblongata | Balance, coordination, respiratory and cardiovascular regulation |

## Common Exam Traps

- **Nissl's Granules Location**: Nissl's granules are present in the **cell body and dendrites**, but absent in the axon and axon hillock.
- **Myelin and Nodes of Ranvier**: Nodes of Ranvier are gaps between myelin sheath segments. They enable saltatory conduction in myelinated axons.
- **Resting Membrane Potential**: The inside of a resting neuron is negative relative to the outside, with a typical potential of approximately $-70\text{ mV}$.
- **Sodium-Potassium Pump**: The pump transports **3 $\mathrm{Na^+}$ out and 2 $\mathrm{K^+}$ in** for every ATP molecule hydrolysed.
- **Depolarization vs Repolarization**: Depolarization mainly involves $\mathrm{Na^+}$ influx, whereas repolarization mainly involves $\mathrm{K^+}$ efflux.
- **Synaptic Transmission**: Calcium ions enter the presynaptic terminal and trigger neurotransmitter release; they do not directly carry the chemical signal across the synaptic cleft.
- **Unidirectional Chemical Synapse**: Chemical transmission is generally one-way because neurotransmitter release occurs at the presynaptic terminal and receptors are located on the postsynaptic membrane.
- **Brain Region Identification**: The cerebellum coordinates posture and balance, whereas the medulla oblongata controls vital autonomic functions such as respiration and cardiovascular activity.
- **Thalamus vs Hypothalamus**: The thalamus acts mainly as a sensory relay station, while the hypothalamus regulates homeostasis and pituitary activity.

## Quiz

1. During the resting state of an unexcited neuronal axon, which mechanism helps maintain the ionic concentration gradients responsible for the resting membrane potential of approximately $-70\text{ mV}$?

   - [ ] Passive influx of positively charged sodium ions ($\mathrm{Na^+}$)
   - [x] Active $\mathrm{Na^+/K^+}$ pump expelling 3 $\mathrm{Na^+}$ for every 2 $\mathrm{K^+}$ imported, alongside differential membrane permeability
   - [ ] Continuous efflux of negatively charged proteins into extracellular fluid
   - [ ] Direct opening of voltage-gated calcium channels

   > The resting potential depends on unequal ion distributions and selective membrane permeability, particularly the greater permeability to $\mathrm{K^+}$ at rest. The electrogenic sodium-potassium pump maintains these gradients by transporting 3 $\mathrm{Na^+}$ out and 2 $\mathrm{K^+}$ in using ATP.

2. Which structural regions of a neuron contain Nissl's granules, which are aggregates of rough endoplasmic reticulum and ribosomes involved in protein synthesis?

   - [ ] Axon hillock and axon terminal
   - [x] Cell body (Soma) and dendrites
   - [ ] Myelin sheath and Nodes of Ranvier
   - [ ] Synaptic vesicles inside presynaptic terminals

   > Nissl's granules are present in the cell body and dendrites, where protein synthesis occurs. They are absent in the axon and axon hillock.

3. Which brain region located in the hindbrain contains vital autonomic centres regulating heart rate, blood pressure, respiration, and gastric secretions?

   - [ ] Cerebellum
   - [ ] Hypothalamus
   - [x] Medulla Oblongata
   - [ ] Thalamus

   > The medulla oblongata contains vital autonomic centres that regulate cardiovascular activity, respiration, and gastric secretions.

4. During the depolarization phase of an action potential, which ion movement is primarily responsible for the rapid reversal of membrane polarity?

   - [ ] Efflux of $\mathrm{K^+}$ ions
   - [x] Influx of $\mathrm{Na^+}$ ions
   - [ ] Influx of $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ ions
   - [ ] Efflux of $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ ions

   > When the threshold potential is reached, voltage-gated sodium channels open, allowing rapid $\mathrm{Na^+}$ influx. This makes the inside of the axon positive relative to the outside.

5. What triggers the release of neurotransmitters from synaptic vesicles into the synaptic cleft at a chemical synapse?

   - [ ] Influx of $\mathrm{K^+}$ ions through voltage-gated potassium channels
   - [ ] Binding of acetylcholine to postsynaptic receptors
   - [x] Influx of $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ ions through voltage-gated calcium channels
   - [ ] Active transport of neurotransmitters across the postsynaptic membrane

   > When an action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels open. Calcium enters the terminal and triggers synaptic vesicle fusion with the presynaptic membrane, releasing neurotransmitters by exocytosis.
