The human respiratory system facilitates the exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide between the atmosphere and the body. Respiration involves three major processes: **breathing (pulmonary ventilation)**, **transport of respiratory gases through blood**, and **cellular respiration**, which releases energy from nutrients.

## Breathing Mechanism and Pressure Dynamics

Breathing involves alternating changes in thoracic cavity volume and intrapulmonary pressure. Air moves from a region of higher pressure to a region of lower pressure.

### Inspiration (Inhalation)

Inspiration is normally an **active process** involving the contraction of respiratory muscles.

1. The **diaphragm contracts** and becomes flattened.
2. The **external intercostal muscles contract**, raising the ribs and sternum.
3. The volume of the thoracic cavity increases.
4. Intrapulmonary pressure falls below atmospheric pressure.
5. Air flows into the lungs.

### Expiration (Exhalation)

Normal expiration is generally a **passive process** caused by the relaxation of respiratory muscles and elastic recoil of the lungs.

1. The diaphragm relaxes and becomes dome-shaped.
2. External intercostal muscles relax, allowing the ribs to return to their resting position.
3. The volume of the thoracic cavity decreases.
4. Intrapulmonary pressure rises above atmospheric pressure.
5. Air flows out of the lungs.

**Forced expiration** is an active process involving the contraction of internal intercostal and abdominal muscles.

### Pressure and Volume Relationship

| Parameter | Inspiration | Expiration |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Diaphragm | Contracts and flattens | Relaxes and becomes dome-shaped |
| External intercostal muscles | Contract | Relax |
| Thoracic volume | Increases | Decreases |
| Intrapulmonary pressure | Falls below atmospheric pressure | Rises above atmospheric pressure |
| Air movement | Into the lungs | Out of the lungs |

## Respiratory Volumes and Capacities

Respiratory volumes represent individual quantities of air inspired or expired, whereas respiratory capacities are combinations of two or more respiratory volumes.

### Major Respiratory Volumes

| Respiratory Volume | Abbreviation | Approximate Adult Value | Definition |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Tidal Volume** | TV | 500 mL | Volume of air inspired or expired during normal quiet breathing |
| **Inspiratory Reserve Volume** | IRV | 2500–3000 mL | Additional volume of air that can be forcibly inspired after a normal inspiration |
| **Expiratory Reserve Volume** | ERV | 1000–1100 mL | Additional volume of air that can be forcibly expired after a normal expiration |
| **Residual Volume** | RV | 1100–1200 mL | Volume of air remaining in the lungs even after forceful expiration |

### Major Respiratory Capacities

| Respiratory Capacity | Abbreviation | Approximate Adult Value | Formula and Definition |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Inspiratory Capacity** | IC | 3000–3500 mL | \(IC = TV + IRV\); maximum volume inspired after a normal expiration |
| **Functional Residual Capacity** | FRC | 2100–2300 mL | \(FRC = ERV + RV\); volume remaining in the lungs after a normal expiration |
| **Vital Capacity** | VC | 3500–4500 mL | \(VC = IRV + TV + ERV\); maximum volume of air expired after a maximal inspiration |
| **Total Lung Capacity** | TLC | 5000–6000 mL | \(TLC = VC + RV\); total volume of air in the lungs after maximal inspiration |

**Important:** Respiratory volume values are approximate and vary with age, sex, body size, posture, and physical fitness.

## Oxygen Transport in Blood

Oxygen diffuses from the alveoli into pulmonary blood capillaries because the partial pressure of oxygen (\(pO_2\)) is higher in alveolar air than in deoxygenated blood.

Approximately **97% of oxygen** is transported bound to haemoglobin inside red blood cells, while the remaining **3%** is transported dissolved in plasma.

### Oxyhaemoglobin Formation

Haemoglobin combines reversibly with oxygen to form oxyhaemoglobin.

\[
\mathrm{Hb + 4O_2 \rightleftharpoons Hb(O_2)_4}
\]

Each haemoglobin molecule can bind up to **four oxygen molecules**.

### Oxygen–Haemoglobin Dissociation Curve

The oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve represents the relationship between the partial pressure of oxygen (\(pO_2\)) and the percentage saturation of haemoglobin with oxygen.

It has a characteristic **sigmoid (S-shaped) curve** because oxygen binding to haemoglobin is cooperative: binding of one oxygen molecule increases the affinity of haemoglobin for subsequent oxygen molecules.

### Factors Affecting Oxygen Release

A **rightward shift** of the dissociation curve indicates reduced haemoglobin affinity for oxygen and enhanced oxygen unloading to tissues.

| Condition | Effect on Haemoglobin |
| ----- | ----- |
| Increased \(pCO_2\) | Promotes oxygen release |
| Increased \(H^+\) concentration (decreased pH) | Reduces oxygen affinity |
| Increased temperature | Promotes oxygen dissociation |
| Increased 2,3-BPG | Reduces oxygen affinity |

The **Bohr effect** describes how increased carbon dioxide concentration and increased hydrogen ion concentration reduce haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen, facilitating oxygen delivery to metabolically active tissues.

A leftward shift occurs under opposite conditions and indicates increased haemoglobin affinity for oxygen.

## Carbon Dioxide Transport in Blood

Carbon dioxide produced during cellular metabolism diffuses from tissues into the blood. It is transported in three major forms:

| Transport Form | Approximate Percentage | Description |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Bicarbonate ions** (\(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\)) | 70% | Formed inside red blood cells and transported mainly in plasma |
| **Carbaminohaemoglobin** | 20–25% | CO₂ binds reversibly to amino groups of haemoglobin |
| **Dissolved CO₂** | 7% | CO₂ is transported directly dissolved in plasma |

### Bicarbonate Formation

Most carbon dioxide entering red blood cells reacts with water to form carbonic acid. This reaction is catalysed by the enzyme **carbonic anhydrase**.

\[
\mathrm{CO_2 + H_2O}
\xrightleftharpoons{\text{Carbonic anhydrase}}
\mathrm{H_2CO_3}
\]

Carbonic acid then dissociates:

\[
\mathrm{H_2CO_3 \rightleftharpoons HCO_3^- + H^+}
\]

The bicarbonate ions move into plasma, while chloride ions enter red blood cells to maintain electrical neutrality. This exchange is known as the **chloride shift (Hamburger phenomenon)**.

In the lungs, the reactions proceed in the reverse direction, converting bicarbonate back into carbon dioxide, which diffuses into the alveoli and is exhaled.

### Carbaminohaemoglobin Formation

A portion of carbon dioxide binds reversibly to the amino groups of globin chains in haemoglobin, forming **carbaminohaemoglobin**.

This binding does not occur at the haem iron where oxygen binds.

## Common Exam Traps

- **Inspiration vs Expiration**: Inspiration increases thoracic volume and decreases intrapulmonary pressure. Expiration decreases thoracic volume and increases intrapulmonary pressure.
- **Active vs Passive Breathing**: Normal inspiration is active, while normal expiration is passive. Forced expiration requires muscular contraction.
- **Vital Capacity vs Total Lung Capacity**: Vital capacity excludes residual volume, whereas total lung capacity includes it.
- **Residual Volume Measurement**: Residual volume cannot be measured directly using standard spirometry because it cannot be exhaled into the instrument. It can be estimated using methods such as helium dilution or body plethysmography.
- **Oxygen Transport**: Approximately 97% of oxygen is transported bound to haemoglobin, not dissolved in plasma.
- **Carbon Dioxide Transport**: The largest fraction of CO₂ is transported as bicarbonate ions, not as carbaminohaemoglobin.
- **Bohr Effect**: Increased \(pCO_2\), increased \(H^+\), and increased temperature promote oxygen unloading and shift the dissociation curve to the right.
- **Carbonic Anhydrase Location**: Carbonic anhydrase is present in high concentrations inside red blood cells, where it rapidly catalyses the conversion of CO₂ and water into carbonic acid.
- **Carbaminohaemoglobin vs Carboxyhaemoglobin**: Carbaminohaemoglobin forms when CO₂ binds to globin chains. Carboxyhaemoglobin forms when carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin.

## Quiz

1. A patient undergoes spirometry evaluation. Which respiratory volume cannot be directly measured using standard spirometric testing?

- [ ] Tidal Volume (TV)
- [ ] Inspiratory Reserve Volume (IRV)
- [x] Residual Volume (RV)
- [ ] Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV)

> Residual volume is the air remaining in the lungs after maximal forced expiration. It cannot be exhaled into a spirometer and must be estimated using techniques such as helium dilution or body plethysmography.

2. Which combination of physiological conditions promotes oxygen dissociation from oxyhaemoglobin in systemic tissue capillaries, shifting the oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve to the right?

- [ ] Low partial pressure of carbon dioxide (\(pCO_2\))
- [x] High \(pCO_2\), increased temperature, and increased \(H^+\) concentration
- [ ] High partial pressure of oxygen (\(pO_2\))
- [ ] Decreased concentration of 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG)

> Increased \(pCO_2\), increased acidity, higher temperature, and increased 2,3-BPG reduce haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen. This promotes oxygen unloading to metabolically active tissues.

3. In human blood, which chemical form accounts for approximately 70% of carbon dioxide transport from peripheral tissues to the lungs?

- [ ] Dissolved CO₂ gas in blood plasma
- [ ] Carbaminohaemoglobin
- [x] Bicarbonate ions (\(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\))
- [ ] Carboxyhaemoglobin

> Most CO₂ enters red blood cells, where carbonic anhydrase catalyses its conversion into carbonic acid. Carbonic acid dissociates into bicarbonate ions and hydrogen ions. Bicarbonate is then transported mainly in plasma.

4. Which respiratory capacity represents the total volume of air that can be accommodated in the lungs after maximal inspiration?

- [ ] Vital Capacity (VC)
- [ ] Functional Residual Capacity (FRC)
- [ ] Inspiratory Capacity (IC)
- [x] Total Lung Capacity (TLC)

> Total lung capacity is the sum of vital capacity and residual volume: \(TLC = VC + RV\). It represents the maximum volume of air present in the lungs after maximal inspiration.

5. What is the name of the phenomenon in which bicarbonate ions move out of red blood cells into plasma while chloride ions move into red blood cells during carbon dioxide transport?

- [ ] Bohr effect
- [ ] Haldane effect
- [x] Chloride shift (Hamburger phenomenon)
- [ ] Cooperative binding

> During CO₂ transport in tissues, bicarbonate ions leave red blood cells and chloride ions enter to maintain electrical neutrality. This exchange is called the chloride shift or Hamburger phenomenon.
