Evolution is the process through which populations change over successive generations. At the genetic level, **evolution occurs when allele frequencies within a population change over time**. These changes can result from natural selection, genetic drift, gene flow, mutation, and genetic recombination.

Population genetics provides the mathematical framework for understanding how genetic variation is distributed within populations and how evolutionary forces influence it.

## Historical Theories of Evolution

Several scientists proposed explanations for how organisms change over generations. Three important historical theories are Lamarckism, Darwinism, and Mutation Theory.

### 1. Lamarckism (Inheritance of Acquired Characters)

Jean-Baptiste Lamarck proposed that organisms evolve through the use and disuse of organs and that acquired characteristics can be inherited by their offspring.

**Main Principles:**

- **Use and Disuse:** Organs used frequently become more developed, while organs that are not used may become reduced.
- **Inheritance of Acquired Characters:** Characteristics acquired during an organism's lifetime were proposed to be passed to its offspring.
- **Environmental Influence:** Environmental changes create new requirements, leading organisms to develop modifications.

**Example: Giraffe's Neck**

- Lamarck proposed that ancestral giraffes had shorter necks.
- Repeated stretching to reach leaves on taller trees was thought to lengthen their necks.
- This acquired increase in neck length was proposed to be inherited by offspring.
- Over generations, giraffes were thought to develop long necks.

**Limitation:** Ordinary acquired changes during an individual's lifetime are generally not inherited through genetic information. Lamarck's explanation is not accepted as the general mechanism of evolution.

### 2. Darwinism (Theory of Natural Selection)

Charles Darwin proposed that evolution occurs through **natural selection**, in which individuals with advantageous heritable variations tend to survive and reproduce more successfully under particular environmental conditions.

**Major Principles:**

1. **Overproduction:** Organisms produce more offspring than can survive with available resources.
2. **Struggle for Existence:** Individuals compete for limited resources such as food, space, and mates.
3. **Variations:** Individuals within a population differ in their characteristics.
4. **Natural Selection:** Individuals possessing advantageous heritable variations tend to have greater reproductive success.
5. **Descent with Modification:** Favorable inherited variations can become more common over successive generations, resulting in evolutionary change.

**Example: Industrial Melanism**

- Before industrialization in England, light-coloured peppered moths were more common in many areas.
- Industrial pollution darkened tree trunks with soot.
- Dark-coloured moths became better camouflaged against predators in polluted environments.
- Their survival and reproductive success increased relative to light-coloured moths.
- The frequency of dark-coloured moths increased in those populations.

**Important:** Natural selection acts on individuals, but evolution is observed as changes in populations across generations.

### 3. Mutation Theory (Hugo de Vries)

Hugo de Vries proposed Mutation Theory based on his observations of the evening primrose (*Oenothera lamarckiana*).

**Major Principles:**

- Mutations are sudden, heritable changes in genetic material.
- Such changes can introduce new characteristics into a population.
- De Vries proposed that major evolutionary changes could occur through sudden, large mutations.
- He used the term **saltation** to describe sudden, discontinuous evolutionary changes.

**Important:** Modern evolutionary biology recognizes mutations as an important source of genetic variation. However, evolution generally involves multiple processes acting over generations rather than requiring a single large mutation to produce a new species.

### Comparison of Evolutionary Theories

| Feature | Lamarckism | Darwinism | Mutation Theory |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Scientist | Jean-Baptiste Lamarck | Charles Darwin | Hugo de Vries |
| Main Idea | Inheritance of acquired characters | Natural selection | Sudden mutations |
| Main Mechanism | Use and disuse | Differential survival and reproduction | Heritable genetic changes |
| Evolutionary Change | Gradual acquisition of characteristics | Accumulation of favorable variations | Emphasis on sudden changes (saltation) |

## Mechanisms of Evolution

Evolutionary change occurs through several genetic and environmental processes.

### 1. Mutation

A mutation is a heritable change in the DNA sequence.

- Mutations introduce new genetic variations.
- They may be beneficial, harmful, or neutral depending on the environment.
- Mutations can occur spontaneously or result from mutagenic agents.
- Only mutations that enter the reproductive gene pool can contribute directly to evolution across generations.

**Examples:**
- Point mutations involving changes in individual nucleotides.
- Insertions or deletions of DNA sequences.
- Chromosomal changes involving large segments of genetic material.

**Important:** Mutation is the ultimate source of new alleles, although recombination reshuffles existing alleles into new combinations.

### 2. Gene Flow (Gene Migration)

Gene flow refers to the movement of alleles between populations through the migration and reproduction of individuals or the transfer of gametes.

- Individuals migrate from one population to another.
- Migrants reproduce and introduce their alleles into the receiving population.
- This can change allele frequencies.
- Gene flow often reduces genetic differences between populations.

**Example:** Pollen transferred from one plant population to another can introduce new alleles into the receiving population.

### 3. Genetic Drift

Genetic drift is the random change in allele frequencies caused by chance events.

- It has a particularly strong effect in small populations.
- Alleles may become fixed or disappear purely by chance.
- It can reduce genetic variation within populations.
- Unlike natural selection, genetic drift does not necessarily favor advantageous alleles.

#### Founder Effect

The founder effect occurs when a small number of individuals establish a new population.

- The founders carry only a subset of the original population's genetic variation.
- Their allele frequencies may differ from those of the original population.
- The new population can therefore have a different genetic composition.

**Example:** A few birds colonize a remote island and establish a population with allele frequencies different from those of the mainland population.

#### Bottleneck Effect

The bottleneck effect occurs when a population undergoes a sudden, substantial reduction in size due to events such as natural disasters, disease, or habitat destruction.

- Only a small number of individuals survive.
- The surviving individuals carry a limited sample of the original gene pool.
- Genetic variation may be permanently reduced.

**Example:** A severe natural disaster reduces a large animal population to a small number of survivors.

### Founder Effect vs Bottleneck Effect

| Feature | Founder Effect | Bottleneck Effect |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Main Event | A small group establishes a new population | Existing population undergoes a drastic reduction |
| Genetic Change | New population inherits a limited sample of source variation | Surviving population retains a limited sample of original variation |
| Main Cause | Migration and colonization | Sudden population reduction |
| Result | Different allele frequencies in the new population | Reduced genetic diversity in the surviving population |

### 4. Genetic Recombination

Genetic recombination produces new combinations of alleles during sexual reproduction.

It occurs through:

- **Crossing Over:** Exchange of genetic material between homologous chromosomes during prophase I of meiosis.
- **Independent Assortment:** Random distribution of homologous chromosomes into gametes during meiosis.
- **Random Fertilization:** Fusion of genetically different gametes creates additional combinations of alleles.

**Important:** Recombination generally reshuffles existing alleles rather than creating entirely new alleles. It increases genetic variation and provides new combinations upon which natural selection can act.

### 5. Natural Selection

Natural selection occurs when individuals with certain heritable characteristics have greater reproductive success than others under specific environmental conditions.

- Favorable alleles may increase in frequency.
- Unfavorable alleles may decrease in frequency.
- Selection depends on environmental conditions.
- A characteristic advantageous in one environment may be disadvantageous in another.

## Types of Natural Selection

Natural selection can alter the distribution of phenotypes within a population in different ways.

### 1. Stabilizing Selection

Stabilizing selection favors intermediate phenotypes and selects against extreme variations.

**Characteristics:**
- Maintains the average phenotype.
- Reduces variation around the mean.
- Does not necessarily change the population's mean significantly.

**Example:** Human birth weight.

- Extremely low birth weight is associated with increased health risks.
- Extremely high birth weight can also create complications.
- Intermediate birth weights generally have higher survival rates.

### 2. Directional Selection

Directional selection favors one extreme phenotype over the other phenotypes.

**Characteristics:**
- Shifts the population's phenotypic distribution toward one extreme.
- Can change the population mean.
- Often occurs when environmental conditions change.

**Example:** Industrial melanism in peppered moths.

- Dark-coloured moths became more common in polluted environments.
- The dark phenotype was favored under those environmental conditions.
- The population shifted toward darker coloration.

### 3. Disruptive Selection

Disruptive selection favors both extreme phenotypes over intermediate phenotypes.

**Characteristics:**
- Intermediate phenotypes are selected against.
- Both extremes have relatively higher reproductive success.
- The population may develop two distinct phenotype groups.

**Example:** Birds in an environment where small and large seeds are abundant but medium-sized seeds are scarce may favor individuals with either small or large beaks.

### Comparison of Natural Selection Patterns

| Type | Favored Phenotype | Effect on Distribution | Example |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Stabilizing** | Intermediate | Narrows around the mean | Human birth weight |
| **Directional** | One extreme | Shifts toward one extreme | Industrial melanism |
| **Disruptive** | Both extremes | May produce two peaks | Different beak sizes favored by different food sources |

## Hardy-Weinberg Principle of Genetic Equilibrium

The Hardy-Weinberg principle describes a theoretical condition in which allele and genotype frequencies remain constant from generation to generation in a population.

It provides a mathematical baseline for identifying evolutionary changes.

### Allele Frequencies

Consider a gene with two alleles:

- **A:** Dominant allele.
- **a:** Recessive allele.

Let:

- \(p\) = frequency of allele A.
- \(q\) = frequency of allele a.

Since these are the only two alleles considered:

\[
p+q=1
\]

### Genotype Frequencies

Under Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, genotype frequencies are calculated by expanding:

\[
(p+q)^2=1
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{p^2+2pq+q^2=1}
\]

Where:

| Expression | Genotype | Meaning |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| \(p^2\) | AA | Homozygous dominant |
| \(2pq\) | Aa | Heterozygous |
| \(q^2\) | aa | Homozygous recessive |

### Numerical Example

Suppose the frequency of the recessive allele \(a\) is 0.3.

**Step 1: Calculate the dominant allele frequency.**

\[
q=0.3
\]

\[
p=1-q=1-0.3=0.7
\]

**Step 2: Calculate the homozygous dominant frequency.**

\[
p^2=(0.7)^2=0.49
\]

**Step 3: Calculate the heterozygous frequency.**

\[
2pq=2(0.7)(0.3)=0.42
\]

**Step 4: Calculate the homozygous recessive frequency.**

\[
q^2=(0.3)^2=0.09
\]

**Final genotype distribution:**

| Genotype | Frequency | Percentage |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| AA | 0.49 | 49% |
| Aa | 0.42 | 42% |
| aa | 0.09 | 9% |
| **Total** | **1.00** | **100%** |

### Conditions for Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

A population remains in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium when the following ideal conditions are satisfied:

1. **No Mutation:** Alleles do not change into other alleles.
2. **Random Mating:** Individuals mate randomly with respect to the gene being considered.
3. **No Migration:** There is no gene flow into or out of the population.
4. **Very Large Population:** Genetic drift is negligible.
5. **No Natural Selection:** All genotypes have equal reproductive success.

When these conditions are maintained, allele and genotype frequencies remain constant across generations.

### Five Factors Disturbing Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

The following factors can alter genetic equilibrium:

| Factor | Effect |
| ----- | ----- |
| **Gene Migration (Gene Flow)** | Introduces or removes alleles through movement between populations. |
| **Genetic Drift** | Randomly changes allele frequencies, especially in small populations. |
| **Mutation** | Creates new alleles or changes existing alleles. |
| **Genetic Recombination** | Produces new combinations of alleles and contributes to genetic variation. |
| **Natural Selection** | Changes allele frequencies through differences in reproductive success. |

**Important:** Genetic recombination can alter genotype combinations without necessarily changing allele frequencies by itself. It is included among the evolutionary factors discussed in population genetics because it generates variation on which selection can act.

## Common Exam Traps

- **Lamarckism:** Proposed inheritance of acquired characters through use and disuse; this is not accepted as the general mechanism of evolution.
- **Darwinism:** Natural selection acts on heritable variations and produces differences in reproductive success.
- **Mutation Theory:** Hugo de Vries proposed sudden evolutionary changes called saltations.
- **Mutation vs Recombination:** Mutation creates new alleles, whereas recombination reshuffles existing alleles into new combinations.
- **Genetic Drift:** Random changes in allele frequencies are especially significant in small populations.
- **Founder Effect:** A small group establishes a new population carrying only a subset of the original gene pool.
- **Bottleneck Effect:** A sudden reduction in population size leaves a limited sample of the original genetic variation.
- **Gene Flow:** Requires movement of alleles between populations, usually through migration and subsequent reproduction.
- **Stabilizing Selection:** Favors intermediate phenotypes and reduces variation around the mean.
- **Directional Selection:** Favors one extreme phenotype and shifts the population distribution.
- **Disruptive Selection:** Favors both extreme phenotypes over intermediate phenotypes.
- **Hardy-Weinberg Equation:** \(p+q=1\) represents allele frequencies, whereas \(p^2+2pq+q^2=1\) represents genotype frequencies.
- **Recessive Genotype:** The frequency of homozygous recessive individuals is \(q^2\), not \(q\).
- **Heterozygous Genotype:** The frequency of heterozygotes is \(2pq\), not \(pq\).
- **Dominant Phenotype:** Its frequency is generally \(p^2+2pq\), assuming complete dominance and the two-allele model.
- **Natural Selection vs Genetic Drift:** Natural selection is non-random with respect to reproductive success, whereas genetic drift is random.

## Quiz

1. In a population at Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, the frequency of the recessive allele \(a\) is 0.3. What is the expected proportion of heterozygous individuals?

- [ ] 0.09
- [ ] 0.49
- [x] 0.42
- [ ] 0.21

> Given \(q=0.3\), the dominant allele frequency is \(p=1-0.3=0.7\). The heterozygote frequency is \(2pq=2(0.7)(0.3)=0.42\), or 42%.

2. A small group of birds is separated from a mainland population and establishes a new colony on a remote island. The new population has allele frequencies that differ from the original population. Which phenomenon is illustrated?

- [ ] Stabilizing selection
- [x] Genetic drift through the founder effect
- [ ] Directional selection
- [ ] Genetic recombination

> The founder effect occurs when a small group establishes a new population. Since the founders carry only a limited sample of the original gene pool, allele frequencies may differ significantly from those of the source population.

3. During the Industrial Revolution in England, dark-coloured peppered moths became more common in polluted environments because they were better camouflaged against soot-darkened tree trunks. Which type of natural selection occurred?

- [ ] Stabilizing selection
- [x] Directional selection
- [ ] Disruptive selection
- [ ] Random genetic drift

> Directional selection favors one extreme phenotype. In polluted environments, dark-coloured moths had a survival advantage, causing the population distribution to shift toward darker coloration.

4. A population experiences a sudden reduction in size after a severe natural disaster. The surviving individuals possess only a small fraction of the original population's genetic variation. What is this phenomenon called?

- [ ] Founder effect
- [x] Bottleneck effect
- [ ] Adaptive radiation
- [ ] Gene flow

> The bottleneck effect occurs when a population undergoes a drastic reduction in size. The surviving individuals represent a limited sample of the original gene pool, often resulting in reduced genetic diversity.

5. Which evolutionary mechanism introduces entirely new alleles into a population?

- [ ] Genetic recombination
- [ ] Independent assortment
- [x] Mutation
- [ ] Random fertilization

> Mutation changes DNA sequences and can produce new alleles. Genetic recombination, independent assortment, and random fertilization primarily generate new combinations of existing alleles.

6. Which type of natural selection favors individuals at both extremes of a phenotypic distribution while selecting against intermediate individuals?

- [ ] Stabilizing selection
- [ ] Directional selection
- [x] Disruptive selection
- [ ] Artificial selection

> Disruptive selection favors both extreme phenotypes over intermediate phenotypes. Under suitable conditions, this can produce two distinct phenotype groups within a population.

7. In a population, the frequency of allele A is 0.6 and the frequency of allele a is 0.4. Assuming Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, what is the expected frequency of homozygous recessive individuals?

- [ ] 0.16
- [ ] 0.36
- [ ] 0.48
- [x] 0.16

> The homozygous recessive genotype frequency is \(q^2\). Since \(q=0.4\), \(q^2=(0.4)^2=0.16\), or 16%.

8. Which of the following is NOT a condition required for Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium?

- [ ] Random mating
- [ ] Very large population size
- [ ] Absence of natural selection
- [x] Continuous migration between populations

> Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium requires no gene flow through migration. Continuous migration introduces or removes alleles and can change allele frequencies, disturbing genetic equilibrium.
