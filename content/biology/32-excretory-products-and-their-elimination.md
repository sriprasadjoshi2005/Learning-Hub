Excretion is the process by which organisms eliminate nitrogenous metabolic wastes such as ammonia, urea, and uric acid. It also plays a vital role in maintaining water and electrolyte balance, a process known as **osmoregulation**.

## Patterns of Nitrogenous Excretion

Organisms eliminate nitrogenous wastes in different forms depending on their habitat, water availability, and physiological adaptations.

### Major Types of Nitrogenous Excretion

| Type | Main Nitrogenous Waste | Toxicity | Water Requirement | Examples |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Ammonotelism** | Ammonia | Highly toxic | Very high | Aquatic invertebrates, bony fishes, tadpoles |
| **Ureotelism** | Urea | Moderately toxic | Moderate | Mammals, adult amphibians, marine fishes |
| **Uricotelism** | Uric acid | Least toxic | Very low | Birds, reptiles, insects, land snails |

### 1. Ammonotelism

**Ammonotelic organisms** eliminate nitrogenous waste primarily as ammonia.

- Ammonia is highly toxic and requires large quantities of water for dilution and elimination.
- It is generally excreted rapidly by diffusion across body surfaces or through specialized excretory structures.
- Common among aquatic organisms because water is readily available.

**Examples:** Many aquatic invertebrates, bony fishes, and tadpoles.

### 2. Ureotelism

**Ureotelic organisms** convert toxic ammonia into less toxic urea, primarily through the **ornithine cycle (urea cycle)** in the liver.

- Urea is transported through the bloodstream to the kidneys.
- The kidneys filter urea and eliminate it through urine.
- This mechanism requires less water than direct ammonia excretion.

**Examples:** Mammals, adult amphibians, and many marine fishes.

### 3. Uricotelism

**Uricotelic organisms** eliminate nitrogenous waste mainly as uric acid.

- Uric acid is relatively non-toxic and poorly soluble in water.
- It is excreted as a concentrated semi-solid paste or crystals.
- This mechanism minimizes water loss and is particularly advantageous in terrestrial environments.

**Examples:** Birds, reptiles, insects, and land snails.

## Human Excretory System

The human excretory system consists of:

1. **Two kidneys**: Filter blood and produce urine.
2. **Two ureters**: Transport urine from the kidneys to the urinary bladder.
3. **Urinary bladder**: Temporarily stores urine.
4. **Urethra**: Carries urine from the bladder to the exterior.

The kidneys are the principal organs responsible for removing nitrogenous wastes and regulating water and electrolyte balance.

## Nephron Structure and Microscopic Anatomy

The **nephron** is the structural and functional unit of the kidney. Each human kidney contains approximately one million nephrons.

A nephron consists of two major components:

### 1. Renal Corpuscle (Malpighian Body)

The renal corpuscle is responsible for the initial filtration of blood.

It consists of:

- **Glomerulus**: A network of capillaries supplied by the afferent arteriole and drained by the efferent arteriole.
- **Bowman's Capsule**: A double-walled cup-shaped structure surrounding the glomerulus that collects the filtrate.

The glomerulus and Bowman's capsule together form the **Malpighian body**.

### 2. Renal Tubule

The renal tubule processes the glomerular filtrate through reabsorption and secretion.

Its main regions are:

$$
\text{PCT} \rightarrow \text{Loop of Henle} \rightarrow \text{DCT} \rightarrow \text{Collecting Duct}
$$

| Nephron Segment | Major Function |
| ----- | ----- |
| **Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT)** | Reabsorbs most filtered water, electrolytes, glucose, and amino acids |
| **Descending Limb of Henle's Loop** | Highly permeable to water; concentrates tubular fluid |
| **Ascending Limb of Henle's Loop** | Reabsorbs electrolytes and is relatively impermeable to water |
| **Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT)** | Regulates electrolyte balance, pH, and water reabsorption |
| **Collecting Duct** | Performs final water reabsorption and concentrates urine under hormonal regulation |

## Physiology of Urine Formation

Urine formation involves three major processes:

1. **Glomerular filtration**
2. **Tubular reabsorption**
3. **Tubular secretion**

The overall relationship can be expressed as:

\[
\text{Excretion} = \text{Filtration} - \text{Reabsorption} + \text{Secretion}
\]

### 1. Glomerular Filtration

Glomerular filtration is the process by which water and small dissolved substances move from glomerular blood into Bowman's capsule under pressure.

The filtration membrane consists of three major components:

1. **Glomerular capillary endothelium**: Contains pores that permit filtration of water and small solutes.
2. **Basement membrane**: Acts as an important filtration barrier, restricting the passage of large proteins.
3. **Podocyte filtration slits**: Narrow gaps between podocyte foot processes that provide additional filtration selectivity.

The normal **Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR)** is approximately:

\[
GFR = 125 \text{ mL/min}
\]

\[
125 \times 60 \times 24 = 180{,}000 \text{ mL/day}
\]

Therefore, approximately **180 litres of filtrate** are formed by both kidneys every day.

### 2. Tubular Reabsorption

Tubular reabsorption is the selective movement of useful substances from the renal tubular fluid back into the blood.

#### Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT)

The PCT is the major site of selective reabsorption.

- Reabsorbs approximately 70–80% of filtered water and electrolytes.
- Normally reabsorbs nearly 100% of filtered glucose and amino acids.
- Reabsorbs most filtered bicarbonate ions.
- Contains simple cuboidal epithelium with a prominent brush border of microvilli, increasing the surface area for reabsorption.

#### Loop of Henle

The Loop of Henle establishes the osmotic gradient in the renal medulla through the **counter-current mechanism**.

| Region | Water Permeability | Electrolyte Movement | Major Effect |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Descending Limb** | Highly permeable | Relatively impermeable to NaCl | Water leaves the filtrate, increasing its concentration |
| **Ascending Limb** | Impermeable to water | NaCl is reabsorbed | Filtrate becomes more dilute |

#### Counter-Current Mechanism

The counter-current mechanism involves the interaction between the Loop of Henle and the vasa recta.

- The descending and ascending limbs of the Loop of Henle carry filtrate in opposite directions.
- The vasa recta carries blood in a counter-current arrangement, helping preserve the medullary osmotic gradient.
- Sodium chloride and urea contribute to the high osmolarity of the renal medulla.
- This gradient allows water to be reabsorbed from the collecting ducts, helping produce concentrated urine.

### 3. Tubular Secretion

Tubular secretion involves the selective movement of substances from blood in peritubular capillaries into the renal tubular fluid.

Important secreted substances include:

- Hydrogen ions (\(H^+\))
- Potassium ions (\(K^+\))
- Ammonium ions (\(NH_4^+\))
- Certain drugs and metabolic wastes

Tubular secretion helps maintain electrolyte balance and acid–base homeostasis.

## Regulation of Kidney Function

Kidney function is regulated by hormones and the juxtaglomerular apparatus, which help maintain blood pressure, filtration rate, and water balance.

### 1. Renin–Angiotensin–Aldosterone System (RAAS)

The **RAAS pathway** is activated when blood pressure or renal blood flow decreases.

The sequence is:

1. Juxtaglomerular (JG) cells release **renin** in response to reduced renal perfusion, reduced NaCl delivery to the macula densa, or sympathetic stimulation.
2. Renin converts liver-produced angiotensinogen into angiotensin I.
3. Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), mainly found on vascular endothelial surfaces, converts angiotensin I into angiotensin II.
4. Angiotensin II causes vasoconstriction and stimulates aldosterone secretion from the adrenal cortex.
5. **Aldosterone** increases sodium reabsorption in the distal nephron, promoting water retention and helping restore blood volume and pressure.

### 2. Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH)

**ADH (vasopressin)** is synthesized in the hypothalamus and stored and released by the posterior pituitary gland.

Its major functions include:

- Increasing water permeability in the late DCT and collecting ducts.
- Promoting the insertion of aquaporin-2 water channels into tubular epithelial cell membranes.
- Increasing water reabsorption into the bloodstream.
- Producing concentrated urine and reducing water loss.

ADH secretion increases during dehydration or when blood osmolarity rises.

### 3. Atrial Natriuretic Peptide (ANP)

ANP is released by the atria of the heart when they are stretched due to increased blood volume.

- Promotes sodium and water excretion.
- Opposes the effects of the RAAS.
- Helps reduce blood volume and blood pressure.

## Common Exam Traps

- **Ammonotelism vs Ureotelism vs Uricotelism**: Ammonia requires the most water for elimination, urea requires less, and uric acid requires the least.
- **Renal Corpuscle vs Malpighian Body**: Both terms refer to the glomerulus together with Bowman's capsule.
- **GFR Calculation**: A GFR of 125 mL/min corresponds to approximately 180 litres of filtrate per day, not 180 litres of urine.
- **PCT Reabsorption**: Nearly all filtered glucose and amino acids are reabsorbed in the PCT under normal conditions.
- **Descending vs Ascending Limb**: The descending limb is highly permeable to water, whereas the ascending limb is impermeable to water and reabsorbs electrolytes.
- **Counter-Current Multiplier vs Exchanger**: The Loop of Henle establishes the medullary osmotic gradient, while the vasa recta helps maintain it.
- **Renin vs Rennin**: Renin is involved in blood pressure regulation through RAAS. Rennin (chymosin) is a milk-coagulating digestive enzyme.
- **ADH vs Aldosterone**: ADH directly increases water permeability through aquaporin insertion. Aldosterone primarily increases sodium reabsorption, with water retention following.
- **ADH Source**: ADH is synthesized in the hypothalamus but released into the bloodstream by the posterior pituitary.
- **Urine Formation**: Filtration, reabsorption, and secretion are distinct processes. Excretion is the final elimination of substances in urine.
