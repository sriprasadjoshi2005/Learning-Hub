All living organisms are composed of organic molecules that perform essential structural, metabolic, and genetic functions. Many of these molecules are **biological macromolecules**, built from smaller molecular units. The four major classes of biomolecules are **carbohydrates**, **proteins**, **lipids**, and **nucleic acids**.

## Major Classes of Biomolecules

The four major biomolecules differ in their building blocks, chemical linkages, and biological functions.

| Biomolecule | Monomer / Building Block | Linkage / Bond Type | Key Functions | Diagnostic Tests |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Carbohydrates** | Monosaccharides (e.g., glucose) | Glycosidic bond | Immediate energy source ($4\ \mathrm{kcal/g}$); structural components such as cellulose and chitin | Iodine test (starch); Benedict's test (reducing sugars) |
| **Proteins** | Amino acids (20 standard types) | Peptide bond | Enzymatic catalysis, structural support, transport, and immunity | Biuret test (violet colour for peptide bonds) |
| **Lipids** | Mainly fatty acids and glycerol | Ester bond (in many common lipids) | Long-term energy storage ($9\ \mathrm{kcal/g}$); major components of cell membranes | Sudan III test; emulsion test |
| **Nucleic Acids** | Nucleotides (sugar + nitrogenous base + phosphate) | Phosphodiester bond | Storage, transmission, and expression of genetic information through DNA and RNA | Dische diphenylamine test (DNA) |

## Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are organic molecules composed mainly of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen. They serve as important energy sources and structural materials.

### Classification of Carbohydrates

| Type | Description | Examples |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Monosaccharides** | Simplest carbohydrates; cannot be hydrolysed into smaller carbohydrates | Glucose, fructose, ribose |
| **Disaccharides** | Formed by the joining of two monosaccharides | Sucrose, lactose, maltose |
| **Polysaccharides** | Long chains of monosaccharide units | Starch, glycogen, cellulose, chitin |

### Important Polysaccharides

- **Starch:** Main storage carbohydrate in plants. It contains amylose and amylopectin.
- **Glycogen:** Storage carbohydrate in animals and fungi. It is highly branched.
- **Cellulose:** Structural component of plant cell walls. It consists of unbranched glucose chains connected by $\beta(1\rightarrow4)$ glycosidic linkages.
- **Chitin:** Structural polysaccharide found in fungal cell walls and the exoskeletons of arthropods. It is composed of N-acetylglucosamine units.

## Proteins

Proteins are polymers of **amino acids** joined by peptide bonds. They perform numerous functions essential for the survival and functioning of cells.

### Structure of Amino Acids

A typical amino acid contains a central carbon atom (the $\alpha$-carbon) attached to four groups:

1. An amino group ($-\mathrm{NH_2}$).
2. A carboxyl group ($-\mathrm{COOH}$).
3. A hydrogen atom.
4. A variable side chain ($R$ group), which determines the amino acid's properties.

Amino acids are **amphoteric**, meaning they can act as both acids and bases.

At a particular pH called the **isoelectric point ($\mathrm{pI}$)**, an amino acid has no net electrical charge and commonly exists as a **zwitterion**:

\[
\mathrm{^{+}NH_3-CHR-COO^{-}}
\]

### Levels of Protein Structure

| Level | Description |
| ----- | ----- |
| **Primary** | Linear sequence of amino acids joined by peptide bonds |
| **Secondary** | Local folding into structures such as $\alpha$-helices and $\beta$-pleated sheets, stabilised mainly by hydrogen bonds |
| **Tertiary** | Overall three-dimensional folding of a single polypeptide chain |
| **Quaternary** | Association of two or more polypeptide chains into a functional protein |

## Lipids

Lipids are a diverse group of mostly hydrophobic organic molecules. They are insoluble or poorly soluble in water but dissolve in many non-polar organic solvents.

Unlike proteins and polysaccharides, **lipids are not true polymers** because they are not generally formed by repeating monomer units linked into long chains.

### Major Types of Lipids

| Type | Structure / Description | Function |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Triglycerides** | One glycerol molecule joined to three fatty acids through ester bonds | Long-term energy storage |
| **Phospholipids** | Glycerol, two fatty acids, and a phosphate-containing polar head | Main structural components of cell membranes |
| **Steroids** | Molecules containing four fused carbon rings | Hormones and membrane components, such as cholesterol |
| **Waxes** | Long-chain fatty acids joined to long-chain alcohols | Waterproofing and protection |

### Saturated and Unsaturated Fatty Acids

- **Saturated fatty acids:** Contain no carbon-carbon double bonds and are generally straight chains.
- **Unsaturated fatty acids:** Contain one or more carbon-carbon double bonds, which can introduce bends into their chains.

## Nucleic Acids

Nucleic acids are polymers of **nucleotides** that store, transmit, and help express genetic information.

### Structure of a Nucleotide

Each nucleotide consists of three components:

1. A five-carbon sugar (ribose or deoxyribose).
2. A nitrogenous base.
3. One or more phosphate groups.

### DNA and RNA

| Feature | DNA | RNA |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Full Form | Deoxyribonucleic Acid | Ribonucleic Acid |
| Sugar | Deoxyribose | Ribose |
| Nitrogenous Bases | Adenine, Guanine, Cytosine, Thymine | Adenine, Guanine, Cytosine, Uracil |
| Typical Structure | Double-stranded helix | Usually single-stranded |
| Main Function | Long-term storage of genetic information | Roles in protein synthesis and gene regulation |

### Types of RNA

- **mRNA (Messenger RNA):** Carries genetic instructions from DNA to ribosomes.
- **tRNA (Transfer RNA):** Brings amino acids to ribosomes during protein synthesis.
- **rRNA (Ribosomal RNA):** Forms a major structural and catalytic component of ribosomes.

## Structure and Function of Enzymes

**Enzymes** are biological catalysts that accelerate chemical reactions by lowering the **activation energy ($E_a$)** required for a reaction. Most enzymes are proteins, although some RNA molecules, called **ribozymes**, also possess catalytic activity.

Enzymes are not consumed during the reactions they catalyse and can be reused.

### Enzyme-Substrate Complex

The substrate binds to a specific region of the enzyme called the **active site**, forming an enzyme-substrate complex.

The general reaction can be represented as:

\[
\mathrm{E + S \rightleftharpoons ES \longrightarrow EP \longrightarrow E + P}
\]

Where:

- $E$ = Enzyme
- $S$ = Substrate
- $ES$ = Enzyme-substrate complex
- $EP$ = Enzyme-product complex
- $P$ = Product

### Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity

1. **Temperature:** Enzyme activity generally increases with temperature up to an optimum value. Beyond this point, the enzyme may denature and lose activity.
2. **pH:** Every enzyme has an optimum pH. Extremely acidic or alkaline conditions can alter the enzyme's structure and active site.
3. **Substrate concentration:** Increasing substrate concentration generally increases reaction velocity until the enzyme becomes saturated.
4. **Enzyme concentration:** When sufficient substrate is available, increasing enzyme concentration generally increases the reaction rate.

## Enzyme Kinetics and Inhibition

Enzyme kinetics studies the rates of enzyme-catalysed reactions under different conditions.

### Michaelis-Menten Equation

For many simple enzyme-catalysed reactions, the reaction velocity is described by the Michaelis-Menten equation:

\[
V = \frac{V_{\max}[S]}{K_m+[S]}
\]

Where:

- $V$ = Initial reaction velocity
- $V_{\max}$ = Maximum reaction velocity
- $[S]$ = Substrate concentration
- $K_m$ = Michaelis constant

The **Michaelis constant ($K_m$)** is the substrate concentration at which the reaction velocity reaches half of its maximum value:

\[
V = \frac{V_{\max}}{2}
\quad \text{when} \quad
[S]=K_m
\]

For many simple enzyme-substrate systems, a lower $K_m$ indicates a higher apparent affinity between the enzyme and substrate, while a higher $K_m$ indicates a lower apparent affinity.

### Enzyme Inhibition Patterns

Enzyme inhibitors reduce enzyme activity by interfering with substrate binding or catalytic function.

| Feature | Competitive Inhibition | Pure Non-Competitive Inhibition |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Binding Site | Active site | Allosteric site |
| Effect on $V_{\max}$ | Unchanged | Decreases |
| Effect on $K_m$ | Increases | Unchanged |
| Effect of Increasing Substrate | Can overcome inhibition | Cannot fully overcome inhibition |

**1. Competitive Inhibition**

- The inhibitor resembles the substrate and competes with it for binding to the enzyme's active site.
- Increasing substrate concentration can overcome the inhibition.
- $V_{\max}$ remains unchanged, while the apparent $K_m$ increases.

**2. Non-Competitive Inhibition**

- In pure non-competitive inhibition, the inhibitor binds to an allosteric site and can bind to both the free enzyme and the enzyme-substrate complex.
- Increasing substrate concentration cannot fully overcome the inhibition.
- $V_{\max}$ decreases, while $K_m$ remains unchanged.

## Common Exam Traps

- **Carbohydrates vs. Lipids:** Carbohydrates provide approximately $4\ \mathrm{kcal/g}$, whereas lipids provide approximately $9\ \mathrm{kcal/g}$.
- **Lipids are not true polymers:** They are not generally constructed from repeating monomeric units linked end-to-end.
- **Cellulose vs. Starch:** Cellulose contains $\beta(1\rightarrow4)$ linkages, while starch contains $\alpha$-glycosidic linkages.
- **Peptide bonds:** These join amino acids to form polypeptide chains.
- **Phosphodiester bonds:** These connect nucleotides within the sugar-phosphate backbone of nucleic acids.
- **Zwitterion:** At the isoelectric point, an amino acid has both positive and negative charges but no net electrical charge.
- **$K_m$ relationship:** A lower $K_m$ generally indicates higher apparent substrate affinity, while a higher $K_m$ generally indicates lower apparent substrate affinity.
- **Competitive inhibition:** $V_{\max}$ remains unchanged, but $K_m$ increases.
- **Pure non-competitive inhibition:** $V_{\max}$ decreases, but $K_m$ remains unchanged.
- **Enzymes and activation energy:** Enzymes lower activation energy; they do not change the overall free-energy difference ($\Delta G$) or the equilibrium position of a reaction.

## Quiz

1. Which of the following biomolecules provides the highest amount of energy per gram when completely oxidised?

   - [ ] Carbohydrates
   - [ ] Proteins
   - [x] Lipids
   - [ ] Nucleic acids

   > Lipids provide approximately $9\ \mathrm{kcal/g}$, whereas carbohydrates and proteins each provide approximately $4\ \mathrm{kcal/g}$. Therefore, lipids are the most energy-dense major biomolecules.

2. Which biomolecule acts as a structural component in plant cell walls and consists of an unbranched polymer of D-glucose units connected by $\beta(1\rightarrow4)$ glycosidic linkages?

   - [ ] Amylose
   - [ ] Glycogen
   - [x] Cellulose
   - [ ] Chitin

   > Cellulose is a linear polysaccharide composed of glucose units connected by $\beta(1\rightarrow4)$ glycosidic bonds. Amylose contains $\alpha(1\rightarrow4)$ linkages, glycogen is a highly branched glucose polymer, and chitin consists of N-acetylglucosamine units.

3. An inhibitor is added to an enzymatic reaction mixture. Experimental studies reveal that adding a large excess of natural substrate restores the maximum reaction velocity ($V_{\max}$) to its original value. What type of inhibition is occurring, and how is the Michaelis constant ($K_m$) affected?

   - [ ] Non-competitive inhibition; $K_m$ decreases
   - [ ] Competitive inhibition; $K_m$ decreases
   - [x] Competitive inhibition; $K_m$ increases
   - [ ] Pure non-competitive inhibition; $K_m$ remains unchanged

   > In competitive inhibition, the inhibitor and substrate compete for the enzyme's active site. Increasing substrate concentration can outcompete the inhibitor and restore $V_{\max}$. However, a higher substrate concentration is required to reach half-maximal velocity, resulting in an increased apparent $K_m$.

4. At the isoelectric point ($\mathrm{pI}$), an amino acid contains both a positively charged amino group and a negatively charged carboxyl group but has no net electrical charge. What is this form called?

   - [ ] Anion
   - [ ] Cation
   - [x] Zwitterion
   - [ ] Free radical

   > A zwitterion contains both positive and negative electrical charges within the same molecule. At the isoelectric point, these charges balance, giving the amino acid an overall net charge of zero.

5. Which type of bond connects adjacent nucleotides in the sugar-phosphate backbone of DNA and RNA?

   - [ ] Peptide bond
   - [ ] Glycosidic bond
   - [x] Phosphodiester bond
   - [ ] Hydrogen bond

   > Phosphodiester bonds connect the phosphate group of one nucleotide to the sugar of another nucleotide, forming the continuous sugar-phosphate backbone of nucleic acids.
