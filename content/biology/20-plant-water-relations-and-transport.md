Plants transport water, inorganic minerals and organic nutrients over long distances using specialized physical gradients and vascular tissues. Water movement is primarily driven by **water potential gradients**, while the transport of sugars occurs through the **phloem**.

## Water Potential ($\Psi_w$)

**Water potential** is the measure of the free energy of water in a system relative to pure water under standard conditions. It determines the direction of water movement.

- Pure water at atmospheric pressure has a water potential of **zero** ($\Psi_w = 0$).
- Adding solutes lowers water potential, making it more negative.
- Water moves from a region of **higher water potential** to a region of **lower water potential**.

The relationship between water potential, solute potential and pressure potential is:

$$
\Psi_w = \Psi_s + \Psi_p
$$

Where:

- **$\Psi_w$ (Water Potential):** Overall potential of water in a system.
- **$\Psi_s$ (Solute Potential):** The component due to dissolved solutes. It is zero for pure water and negative for solutions.
- **$\Psi_p$ (Pressure Potential):** The component due to physical pressure. It is usually positive in turgid plant cells.

### Example: Calculating Water Potential

If a plant cell has:

$$
\Psi_s = -0.8 \text{ MPa}
$$

$$
\Psi_p = +0.3 \text{ MPa}
$$

Then:

$$
\Psi_w = -0.8 + 0.3 = -0.5 \text{ MPa}
$$

The cell's water potential is therefore $-0.5 \text{ MPa}$.

## Osmotic Movements in Plant Cells

Water moves across selectively permeable membranes through **osmosis**, from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential.

### Types of Solutions

| Solution | Relative Solute Concentration | Water Movement | Effect on Plant Cell |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Hypotonic** | Lower outside the cell | Water enters the cell | Cell becomes turgid |
| **Hypertonic** | Higher outside the cell | Water leaves the cell | Cell undergoes plasmolysis |
| **Isotonic** | Equal on both sides | No net water movement | Cell becomes flaccid |

### Important Osmotic Phenomena

- **Plasmolysis:** Shrinkage of the protoplast away from the cell wall when a plant cell is placed in a hypertonic solution.
- **Turgor Pressure:** Hydrostatic pressure developed when water enters a plant cell, causing the cell contents to press against the cell wall.
- **Deplasmolysis:** Reversal of plasmolysis when a plasmolysed cell is placed in a hypotonic solution and takes up water.

## Long-Distance Water Transport: Ascent of Sap

**Ascent of sap** refers to the upward movement of water and dissolved minerals from the roots to the aerial parts of a plant through the xylem.

Water enters root hairs from the soil by osmosis and moves through the root tissues before reaching the xylem.

### Pathways of Water Movement

Water moves across the root cortex through two major pathways:

| Feature | Apoplast Pathway | Symplast Pathway |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Route** | Cell walls and intercellular spaces | Cytoplasm of living cells |
| **Membrane Crossing** | Does not initially require crossing plasma membranes | Requires crossing the plasma membrane initially |
| **Connections** | Continuous cell wall system | Plasmodesmata |
| **Casparian Strip** | Blocked at the endodermis | Continues through the cytoplasm |

- **Apoplast Pathway:** Water moves through the non-living cell walls and intercellular spaces without crossing plasma membranes along the route.
- **Symplast Pathway:** Water moves through the interconnected cytoplasm of living cells via plasmodesmata.

**Important:** The **Casparian strip**, made of suberin in the endodermal cell walls, blocks apoplastic movement. Water is therefore forced to cross a selectively permeable plasma membrane and enter the symplast before reaching the vascular tissue.

### Cohesion-Tension-Transpiration Pull Model

Proposed by **Dixon and Joly**, this model explains the upward movement of water through xylem, even in very tall trees.

It depends on three important properties:

1. **Cohesion:** Attraction between water molecules due to hydrogen bonding, maintaining a continuous water column inside the xylem.
2. **Adhesion:** Attraction between water molecules and the hydrophilic walls of xylem elements, helping water resist gravity.
3. **Transpiration Pull:** Evaporation of water from leaf surfaces through stomata creates negative pressure (tension) that pulls water upward through the xylem.

The continuous water column transmits this tension from the leaves down to the roots.

## Phloem Translocation: Mass Flow Hypothesis

The **Mass Flow Hypothesis**, also known as the **Pressure Flow Hypothesis**, was proposed by Ernst Münch. It explains the movement of organic food materials, especially sucrose, through phloem from source regions to sink regions.

- **Source:** A region where sugars are produced or released, such as photosynthetic leaves.
- **Sink:** A region where sugars are consumed or stored, such as growing roots, fruits and storage organs.

### Mechanism of Pressure Flow

1. **Sucrose Loading:** Sucrose is actively loaded into sieve tube elements at the source, often with the help of companion cells.
2. **Water Entry:** Sucrose loading lowers the solute potential and water potential of the phloem. Water enters from adjacent xylem by osmosis.
3. **Pressure Development:** Water entry increases hydrostatic pressure at the source.
4. **Mass Flow:** The resulting pressure gradient drives phloem sap from the high-pressure source toward the low-pressure sink.
5. **Sucrose Unloading:** Sugars are removed at the sink for utilization or storage. Water potential rises, allowing water to move out of the phloem, often returning to the xylem.

The overall movement can be represented as:

$$
\text{Source (High Pressure)}
\xrightarrow{\text{Phloem Bulk Flow}}
\text{Sink (Low Pressure)}
$$

## Common Exam Traps

- **Water Potential Direction:** Water always moves from higher water potential to lower water potential, not necessarily from higher solute concentration to lower solute concentration.
- **Maximum Water Potential:** Pure water at atmospheric pressure has a water potential of zero. Dissolved solutes make solute potential negative.
- **Pressure Potential:** Pressure potential is generally positive in turgid plant cells and contributes to increasing their water potential.
- **Plasmolysis:** It occurs when a plant cell loses water in a hypertonic solution, causing the protoplast to shrink away from the cell wall.
- **Casparian Strip Barrier:** The suberin-containing Casparian strip blocks the apoplast pathway at the endodermis and forces water to enter the symplast.
- **Cohesion vs Adhesion:** Cohesion is attraction between water molecules, whereas adhesion is attraction between water molecules and xylem walls.
- **Xylem vs Phloem:** Xylem transports water and minerals, primarily upward, whereas phloem transports organic food from source to sink and can operate in different directions in different sieve tubes.
- **Pressure Flow:** Sucrose loading at the source causes osmotic water entry and high hydrostatic pressure. The pressure gradient drives bulk flow toward the sink.

## Quiz

1. A plant cell has a solute potential ($\Psi_s$) of $-0.8 \text{ MPa}$ and a pressure potential ($\Psi_p$) of $+0.3 \text{ MPa}$. It is placed in a solution with a water potential ($\Psi_w$) of $-0.7 \text{ MPa}$. What will be the net direction of water movement?

- [ ] Net water movement into the cell
- [x] Net water movement out of the cell into the solution
- [ ] No net movement of water
- [ ] The cell will undergo immediate lysis

> The cell's water potential is $\Psi_w = -0.8 + 0.3 = -0.5 \text{ MPa}$. Since water moves from higher water potential ($-0.5 \text{ MPa}$) to lower water potential ($-0.7 \text{ MPa}$), water moves out of the cell.

2. Which hydrophobic, suberized structure in the endodermal cell walls of plant roots blocks apoplastic water movement and forces water to enter the symplast?

- [ ] Plasmodesmata
- [x] Casparian strip
- [ ] Tonoplast membrane
- [ ] Middle lamella

> The Casparian strip is a suberin-containing band in the endodermal cell walls. It blocks apoplastic water movement and forces water and dissolved substances to cross the plasma membrane into the symplast.

3. According to the Münch Pressure Flow Hypothesis, what generates the hydrostatic pressure gradient required for sucrose transport through sieve tubes?

- [x] Active loading of sucrose at the source lowers phloem water potential, causing osmotic entry of water.
- [ ] Transpiration pull from stomata creates negative pressure inside phloem vessels.
- [ ] Capillary action inside phloem sieve elements moves sucrose upward.
- [ ] Passive diffusion of sucrose without water movement.

> Active sucrose loading at the source lowers the solute potential and water potential of the phloem. Water enters from adjacent xylem by osmosis, increasing hydrostatic pressure and driving bulk flow toward the sink.

4. Which pair of properties helps maintain a continuous column of water inside the xylem during the ascent of sap?

- [ ] Diffusion and osmosis
- [x] Cohesion and adhesion
- [ ] Plasmolysis and deplasmolysis
- [ ] Active transport and facilitated diffusion

> Cohesion maintains attraction between water molecules, while adhesion helps water molecules remain attached to the xylem walls. Together, they help maintain the continuous water column.

5. What happens to a plant cell when it is placed in a strongly hypertonic solution?

- [ ] Water enters the cell and it becomes turgid.
- [x] Water leaves the cell and the protoplast shrinks away from the cell wall.
- [ ] The cell wall dissolves.
- [ ] The cell immediately undergoes cell division.

> In a hypertonic solution, the surrounding medium has lower water potential than the cell. Water moves out by osmosis, causing the protoplast to shrink away from the cell wall. This process is called plasmolysis.

6. Which statement correctly distinguishes the apoplast pathway from the symplast pathway?

- [ ] Apoplast movement occurs through cytoplasm, while symplast movement occurs through cell walls.
- [x] Apoplast movement occurs through cell walls and intercellular spaces, while symplast movement occurs through interconnected cytoplasm.
- [ ] Both pathways occur exclusively through xylem vessels.
- [ ] Symplast movement does not involve living cells.

> The apoplast pathway uses non-living cell walls and intercellular spaces, whereas the symplast pathway uses the interconnected cytoplasm of living cells through plasmodesmata.
