title: Morphology of Flowering Plants
level: intermediate
---

**Morphology** is the branch of botany that deals with the study of the external structure and form of plants. Flowering plants (angiosperms) generally possess two main structural systems: the **root system** and the **shoot system**. The root system anchors the plant and absorbs water and minerals, while the shoot system consists of the stem, leaves, flowers, fruits and seeds.

## Root System and Modifications

The **root system** usually develops from the radicle of the embryo. Roots primarily anchor the plant, absorb water and minerals, and conduct them to other plant parts.

### Types of Root Systems

| Root System | Description | Examples |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Tap Root System** | Develops from the radicle, which forms the primary root. Lateral roots arise from it. | Mustard, Pea |
| **Fibrous Root System** | The primary root is short-lived and is replaced by a cluster of similarly sized roots arising from the base of the stem. | Wheat, Rice |
| **Adventitious Root System** | Roots arise from plant parts other than the radicle, such as stems or leaves. | *Monstera*, Banyan |

### Root Modifications

Roots may become modified to perform specialised functions such as food storage, mechanical support and respiration.

| Function | Modification | Description / Examples |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Storage** | Conical | Broad at the top and gradually tapering towards the bottom; Carrot |
| **Storage** | Fusiform | Swollen in the middle and tapering at both ends; Radish |
| **Storage** | Napiform | Upper portion greatly swollen and almost spherical, with a thin tapering lower end; Turnip |
| **Support** | Prop Roots | Hanging roots arise from branches and grow downwards into the soil to support heavy branches; Banyan |
| **Support** | Stilt Roots | Roots arise from lower stem nodes and grow obliquely into the soil; Maize, Sugarcane |
| **Respiration** | Pneumatophores | Negatively geotropic respiratory roots that grow upwards above waterlogged soil to obtain oxygen; *Rhizophora* |

### Important Root Modifications

- **Storage roots**: Store reserve food materials and become swollen.
- **Prop roots**: Provide mechanical support to large branches.
- **Stilt roots**: Provide additional support to the stem.
- **Pneumatophores**: Help plants growing in waterlogged, oxygen-deficient soils exchange gases with the atmosphere.

## Stem and Leaf Morphology

### Stem

The **stem** develops from the plumule of the embryo and forms the main aerial axis of the plant. It bears branches, leaves, flowers and fruits.

Stems may undergo modifications to perform specialised functions.

### Stem Modifications

| Function | Modification | Description / Examples |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Storage** | Rhizome | Horizontal underground stem with nodes, internodes and scale leaves; Ginger |
| **Storage** | Tuber | Swollen underground stem storing food, with buds called eyes; Potato |
| **Storage** | Corm | Short, vertical, swollen underground stem; *Colocasia* |
| **Protection** | Thorn | Hard, pointed modified axillary or terminal shoot; *Bougainvillea*, Citrus |
| **Photosynthesis** | Phylloclade | Green, flattened or cylindrical modified stem that performs photosynthesis; *Opuntia*, *Euphorbia* |

### Leaf

A **leaf** is a lateral, flattened structure arising from the node of a stem. It is the primary site of photosynthesis in most plants.

A typical leaf consists of three main parts:

1. **Leaf base**: Attaches the leaf to the stem.
2. **Petiole**: The stalk connecting the leaf blade to the stem.
3. **Lamina**: The broad, flat portion of the leaf.

### Leaf Venation

**Venation** refers to the arrangement of veins and veinlets in the leaf lamina.

| Type | Description | Common Examples |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Reticulate Venation** | Veins form a network-like pattern. | Mustard, Hibiscus |
| **Parallel Venation** | Veins run parallel to one another. | Wheat, Grass |

Reticulate venation is commonly associated with dicotyledonous plants, while parallel venation is commonly associated with monocotyledonous plants.

### Leaf Modifications

Leaves may be modified to perform functions other than photosynthesis.

| Modification | Description / Function | Examples |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Phyllode** | Petiole becomes flattened, green and photosynthetic when the leaf lamina is reduced or shed. | Australian *Acacia* |
| **Leaf Tendrils** | Leaves or leaf parts become slender structures that help the plant climb. | Pea |
| **Leaf Spines** | Leaves are modified into sharp structures that reduce water loss and provide protection. | Cacti |

## Flower Structure and Inflorescence

A **flower** is the reproductive structure of angiosperms. A typical complete flower consists of four concentric whorls arranged on the thalamus or receptacle.

### Four Floral Whorls

$$
\text{Flower Whorls} =
\text{Calyx} + \text{Corolla} + \text{Androecium} + \text{Gynoecium}
$$

| Floral Whorl | Components | Main Function |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Calyx** | Sepals | Protects the flower during the bud stage. |
| **Corolla** | Petals | Often attracts pollinators through colour and scent. |
| **Androecium** | Stamens | Male reproductive whorl that produces pollen grains. |
| **Gynoecium** | Carpels | Female reproductive whorl containing ovules. |

### Structure of a Stamen

A **stamen** is the male reproductive unit of a flower. It consists of:

- **Filament**: Slender stalk that supports the anther.
- **Anther**: Usually bilobed structure containing pollen sacs in which pollen grains develop.

### Structure of a Carpel

A **carpel** is the female reproductive unit of a flower. It consists of:

- **Stigma**: Receives pollen grains.
- **Style**: Connects the stigma to the ovary.
- **Ovary**: Contains ovules, which develop into seeds after fertilisation.

### Aestivation

**Aestivation** refers to the arrangement of sepals or petals in a floral bud with respect to one another.

| Type | Description | Examples |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Valvate** | Margins of sepals or petals touch one another without overlapping. | Calotropis |
| **Twisted** | One margin of each petal overlaps the next petal, while the other margin is overlapped by the previous one. | China rose, Cotton |
| **Imbricate** | Margins overlap irregularly, with no fixed overlapping pattern. | Cassia, Gulmohar |
| **Vexillary** | The largest petal (standard) overlaps the two lateral petals (wings), which overlap the two smallest petals (keel). | Pea, Bean |

### Placentation

**Placentation** refers to the arrangement of ovules within the ovary.

| Type | Description | Examples |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Marginal** | Ovules are arranged along the ventral suture of a monocarpellary ovary. | Pea |
| **Axile** | Ovules are attached to a central axis, with the ovary divided into multiple chambers by septa. | Tomato, Citrus |
| **Parietal** | Ovules are attached to the inner wall of a unilocular ovary. | Mustard |
| **Free-Central** | Ovules are borne on a central axis without internal septa in a unilocular ovary. | *Dianthus*, Primrose |
| **Basal** | A single ovule or a few ovules are attached at the base of the ovary. | Sunflower |

## Common Exam Traps

- **Tap Root vs Fibrous Root**: Tap roots develop from the radicle and are common in dicots, whereas fibrous roots develop from the base of the stem after the primary root dies, and are common in monocots.
- **Prop Roots vs Stilt Roots**: Prop roots arise from branches and grow downwards into the soil, as in Banyan. Stilt roots arise from lower stem nodes and grow obliquely into the soil, as in Maize.
- **Phylloclade vs Phyllode**: A **phylloclade** is a modified photosynthetic stem, as in *Opuntia*. A **phyllode** is a modified photosynthetic petiole, as in Australian *Acacia*.
- **Thorns vs Spines**: Thorns are modified stems or branches, as in Citrus and *Bougainvillea*. Spines are modified leaves or leaf parts, as in Cacti.
- **Pneumatophores**: These are negatively geotropic respiratory roots that project above waterlogged soil to obtain oxygen.
- **Free-Central Placentation**: Ovules are attached to a central axis in a unilocular ovary without septa. It is seen in *Dianthus* and Primrose.
- **Vexillary Aestivation**: The standard petal is the largest and overlaps the wings, which overlap the keel petals.

## Quiz

1. Mangrove plants growing in saline, waterlogged areas develop upright roots that project above the mud surface. What are these specialised roots called?

- [ ] Prop roots
- [ ] Stilt roots
- [x] Pneumatophores
- [ ] Haustorial roots

> Pneumatophores are specialised respiratory roots found in mangrove plants such as *Rhizophora*. They grow upwards above waterlogged soil and help the plant obtain atmospheric oxygen.

2. In an Australian *Acacia* plant, the leaf lamina falls off early, while the petiole becomes expanded, green and photosynthetic. What is this modification called?

- [ ] Cladode
- [ ] Phylloclade
- [x] Phyllode
- [ ] Tendril

> A phyllode is a modified petiole that becomes flattened and green to perform photosynthesis when the normal leaf lamina is reduced or shed.

3. Which type of placentation is characterised by ovules arranged on a central axis without internal septa in a unilocular ovary, as seen in *Dianthus*?

- [ ] Axile placentation
- [ ] Parietal placentation
- [x] Free-central placentation
- [ ] Marginal placentation

> In free-central placentation, ovules are attached to a central axis within a single-chambered ovary. Internal septa are absent. Examples include *Dianthus* and Primrose.

4. Which underground stem modification is characterised by swollen storage tissue and the presence of buds called eyes?

- [ ] Rhizome
- [x] Tuber
- [ ] Corm
- [ ] Prop root

> A tuber is a swollen underground stem that stores food. Potato is a common example, and its buds are known as eyes.

5. In pea plants, the floral petals are arranged in which type of aestivation?

- [ ] Valvate
- [ ] Twisted
- [ ] Imbricate
- [x] Vexillary

> Pea flowers show vexillary aestivation. The largest petal, called the standard, overlaps the two wings, which in turn overlap the two keel petals.

6. Which type of placentation is commonly found in mustard?

- [ ] Marginal
- [ ] Axile
- [x] Parietal
- [ ] Basal

> Mustard exhibits parietal placentation, in which ovules are attached to the inner wall of a unilocular ovary.
