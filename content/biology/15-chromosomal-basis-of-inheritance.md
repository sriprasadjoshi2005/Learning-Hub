The **Chromosomal Theory of Inheritance**, proposed by Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri in 1902, states that genes are located at specific positions called **loci** on chromosomes. The behaviour of chromosomes during meiosis explains how genes are transmitted from parents to offspring and accounts for Mendelian inheritance patterns. This theory was experimentally supported by Thomas Hunt Morgan through his studies on *Drosophila melanogaster* (fruit fly).

## Linkage and Genetic Recombination

### Linkage

**Linkage** is the tendency of genes located on the same chromosome to be inherited together. Linked genes do not assort independently unless crossing over separates them.

- **Complete linkage**: No recombination occurs between the linked genes, so only parental combinations are produced.
- **Incomplete linkage**: Crossing over occurs between linked genes, producing both parental and recombinant combinations.

### Genetic Recombination

**Recombination** is the formation of new combinations of alleles that differ from those present in the parents.

- It occurs through **crossing over** between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes.
- Crossing over takes place during the **pachytene stage of Prophase I** of meiosis.
- Recombinant offspring have combinations of traits different from the parental combinations.

### Gene Mapping

Alfred Sturtevant developed the first genetic map using recombination frequencies. He demonstrated that the recombination frequency between two genes is generally proportional to the distance separating them on a chromosome.

$$
1\% \text{ recombination frequency} = 1 \text{ map unit} = 1 \text{ centimorgan (cM)}
$$

**Example:** If two linked genes show a recombination frequency of $12\%$, their estimated map distance is $12\text{ cM}$.

## Sex Determination Systems

Different organisms use different chromosome-based mechanisms to determine biological sex.

| System | Female Genotype | Male Genotype | Organism Examples |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **XX–XY** | Homogametic ($XX$) | Heterogametic ($XY$) | Humans, *Drosophila*, most mammals |
| **XX–XO** | Homogametic ($XX$) | Heterogametic ($XO$) | Grasshoppers and some insects |
| **ZZ–ZW** | Heterogametic ($ZW$) | Homogametic ($ZZ$) | Birds, butterflies and some reptiles |
| **Haplodiploidy** | Diploid ($2n$) | Haploid ($n$) | Honeybees, ants and wasps |

### Important Terms

- **Homogametic sex**: Produces only one type of sex chromosome-bearing gamete.
- **Heterogametic sex**: Produces two types of gametes with different sex chromosomes.
- **Haplodiploidy**: Females develop from fertilised diploid eggs, while males develop from unfertilised haploid eggs.

## Human Pedigree Analysis

A **pedigree chart** represents the inheritance of a particular trait across several generations of a family. It helps identify whether a trait is dominant, recessive, autosomal or sex-linked.

### Common Pedigree Symbols

| Symbol | Meaning |
| ----- | ----- |
| Square | Male |
| Circle | Female |
| Shaded symbol | Affected individual |
| Unshaded symbol | Unaffected individual |
| Half-shaded symbol | Carrier (when represented in the pedigree) |
| Horizontal line connecting two individuals | Mating |
| Vertical line | Descent to offspring |

### Major Inheritance Patterns

| Inheritance Pattern | Key Characteristics | Examples |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Autosomal Dominant** | Usually appears in every generation; affected individuals generally have at least one affected parent. | Myotonic dystrophy, Huntington's disease |
| **Autosomal Recessive** | May skip generations; affected individuals can be born to unaffected carrier parents. | Sickle cell anaemia, cystic fibrosis |
| **X-Linked Recessive** | More common in males; carrier mothers can pass the affected allele to their sons. | Haemophilia, red-green colour blindness |

### Recognising Inheritance Patterns

- **Autosomal dominant**: An affected individual usually has an affected parent. Both males and females can inherit the trait.
- **Autosomal recessive**: Two unaffected carrier parents can produce an affected child.
- **X-linked recessive**: A father cannot pass his X-linked allele directly to his son because sons inherit the father's Y chromosome.
- **Criss-cross inheritance**: An X-linked allele can pass from an affected father to his daughter and subsequently to her son.

## Common Exam Traps

- **Maximum Recombination Frequency**: The observable recombination frequency between two genes cannot exceed **$50\%$**. At this value, the genes behave as though they assort independently. This does not necessarily mean that they are located on different chromosomes; genes far apart on the same chromosome can also show approximately $50\%$ recombination.
- **Crossing Over Stage**: Crossing over occurs during the **pachytene stage of Prophase I**, not metaphase I.
- **Heterogametic Sex**: Human males are heterogametic ($XY$), whereas female birds are heterogametic ($ZW$).
- **X-Linked Inheritance**: Fathers pass their X chromosome to daughters and their Y chromosome to sons. Therefore, fathers do not directly transmit X-linked traits to their sons.
- **Haplodiploidy**: Male honeybees develop from unfertilised haploid eggs, while females develop from fertilised diploid eggs.

## Quiz

1. Two genes, $A$ and $B$, are located on the same chromosome. A dihybrid test cross produces $84\%$ parental-type offspring and $16\%$ recombinant-type offspring. What is the genetic map distance between the two genes?

- [ ] $84\text{ cM}$
- [ ] $42\text{ cM}$
- [x] $16\text{ cM}$
- [ ] $8\text{ cM}$

> Recombination frequency directly determines the estimated genetic map distance. Since $1\%$ recombination equals $1\text{ cM}$, a recombination frequency of $16\%$ corresponds to $16\text{ cM}$.

2. A human male with red-green colour blindness ($X^cY$) marries a woman with normal vision whose father was colour-blind. Assuming the woman is a carrier ($X^CX^c$), what is the probability that their first biological son will be colour-blind?

- [ ] $0\%$
- [ ] $25\%$
- [x] $50\%$
- [ ] $100\%$

> A son inherits the Y chromosome from his father and one X chromosome from his mother. The carrier mother has an equal probability of passing either $X^C$ or $X^c$. Therefore, the probability that a son will be colour-blind is $50\%$.

3. In domestic poultry, which chromosome composition determines female biological sex?

- [ ] $XX$
- [ ] $XY$
- [x] $ZW$
- [ ] $ZZ$

> Birds follow the $ZZ$–$ZW$ sex determination system. Females are heterogametic ($ZW$), while males are homogametic ($ZZ$).

4. During which stage of meiosis does crossing over occur?

- [ ] Leptotene
- [ ] Zygotene
- [x] Pachytene
- [ ] Diplotene

> Crossing over occurs during the pachytene stage of Prophase I, when non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes exchange corresponding segments.

5. In humans, which individual is heterogametic?

- [ ] Female ($XX$)
- [x] Male ($XY$)
- [ ] Both sexes
- [ ] Neither sex

> Human males are heterogametic because they produce two types of sperm: one carrying an X chromosome and another carrying a Y chromosome. Females are homogametic because their eggs carry only an X chromosome.
