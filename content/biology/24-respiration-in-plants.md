Cellular respiration is a **catabolic oxidation process** in which complex organic molecules, primarily glucose, are broken down to release energy stored in the form of ATP. This energy is used to support various metabolic activities within living cells.

## Overall Equation of Aerobic Respiration

The overall balanced equation for aerobic respiration is:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2 \rightarrow 6CO_2 + 6H_2O + Energy}
$$

The energy released is captured in ATP through substrate-level phosphorylation and oxidative phosphorylation.

## Stages of Aerobic Respiration

Aerobic respiration involves four major stages:

1. Glycolysis
2. Link Reaction (Pyruvate Oxidation)
3. Krebs Cycle
4. Electron Transport Chain and Oxidative Phosphorylation

## 1. Glycolysis (EMP Pathway)

Glycolysis is the first stage of cellular respiration. It occurs in the **cytosol** of all living cells and does not directly require oxygen.

The term glycolysis means the breakdown of glucose.

### Process

- One glucose molecule containing six carbon atoms is converted into two molecules of pyruvate, each containing three carbon atoms.
- The process involves an energy investment phase and an energy payoff phase.
- Two ATP molecules are consumed during the initial steps.
- Four ATP molecules are produced during the payoff phase.
- Two NADH molecules are also generated.

### Net Yield per Glucose

| Product | Quantity |
| ----- | ----- |
| Pyruvate | 2 molecules |
| ATP | 2 molecules (net) |
| NADH | 2 molecules |
| CO2 | 0 molecules |

The net ATP yield is:

$$
\mathrm{4\ ATP - 2\ ATP = 2\ ATP}
$$

**Important**: Glycolysis occurs in both aerobic and anaerobic conditions. It does not consume oxygen directly.

## 2. Link Reaction (Pyruvate Oxidation)

The link reaction connects glycolysis with the Krebs cycle.

It occurs in the **mitochondrial matrix** in eukaryotic cells.

### Process

- Two pyruvate molecules enter the mitochondrial matrix.
- Each pyruvate undergoes oxidative decarboxylation.
- One carbon atom is released as carbon dioxide.
- NAD$^+$ is reduced to NADH.
- The remaining two-carbon acetyl group combines with coenzyme A to form acetyl-CoA.

### Reaction

$$
\mathrm{Pyruvate + CoA + NAD^+}
$$

$$
\mathrm{\rightarrow Acetyl\text{-}CoA + CO_2 + NADH}
$$

### Yield per Glucose

| Product | Quantity |
| ----- | ----- |
| Acetyl-CoA | 2 molecules |
| NADH | 2 molecules |
| CO2 | 2 molecules |

**Key concept**: The link reaction produces acetyl-CoA, which enters the Krebs cycle. No ATP is directly synthesized during this stage.

## 3. Krebs Cycle (Tricarboxylic Acid Cycle)

The Krebs cycle, also called the **Citric Acid Cycle** or **TCA Cycle**, occurs in the mitochondrial matrix.

It oxidizes the acetyl group of acetyl-CoA and transfers energy to electron carriers.

### Initial Reaction

- Acetyl-CoA contains a two-carbon acetyl group.
- It combines with oxaloacetic acid (OAA), a four-carbon compound.
- This forms citric acid, a six-carbon compound.

$$
\mathrm{Acetyl\text{-}CoA\ (2C) + OAA\ (4C)}
$$

$$
\mathrm{\rightarrow Citrate\ (6C) + CoA}
$$

### Main Events

During one turn of the cycle:

- Two molecules of carbon dioxide are released.
- Three NADH molecules are produced.
- One FADH$_2$ molecule is produced.
- One GTP or ATP molecule is generated.
- Oxaloacetic acid is regenerated to begin another cycle.

### Yield per Glucose

Since one glucose molecule produces two acetyl-CoA molecules, the Krebs cycle completes two turns per glucose molecule.

| Product | Per Turn | Per Glucose (2 Turns) |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| CO2 | 2 | 4 |
| NADH | 3 | 6 |
| FADH2 | 1 | 2 |
| GTP/ATP | 1 | 2 |

**Important**: The Krebs cycle generates reduced electron carriers (NADH and FADH$_2$), which deliver high-energy electrons to the electron transport chain.

## 4. Electron Transport Chain and Oxidative Phosphorylation

The electron transport chain (ETC) is located in the **inner mitochondrial membrane**.

It consists of protein complexes and mobile electron carriers that transfer electrons from NADH and FADH$_2$ to oxygen.

### Electron Transport Chain Components

| Component | Function |
| ----- | ----- |
| **Complex I** | Accepts electrons from NADH and transfers them to ubiquinone. |
| **Complex II** | Accepts electrons from FADH$_2$ and transfers them to ubiquinone. |
| **Ubiquinone (CoQ)** | Mobile electron carrier that transfers electrons to Complex III. |
| **Complex III** | Transfers electrons to cytochrome c. |
| **Cytochrome c** | Mobile electron carrier that transfers electrons to Complex IV. |
| **Complex IV** | Transfers electrons to oxygen, the final electron acceptor, forming water. |
| **Complex V (ATP Synthase)** | Uses the proton gradient to synthesize ATP. |

FADH$_2$ enters the electron transport chain through Complex II and transfers electrons to ubiquinone.

### Proton Gradient and ATP Synthesis

- Complexes I, III, and IV pump protons from the mitochondrial matrix into the intermembrane space.
- This creates an electrochemical proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane.
- Protons flow back into the matrix through ATP synthase.
- The energy released drives ATP synthesis from ADP and inorganic phosphate.

This mechanism is called **chemiosmosis**.

### Role of Oxygen

Oxygen acts as the **final terminal electron acceptor** in aerobic respiration.

At Complex IV, oxygen combines with electrons and protons to form water.

$$
\mathrm{\frac{1}{2}O_2 + 2e^- + 2H^+ \rightarrow H_2O}
$$

Without oxygen, the electron transport chain cannot continue functioning normally.

### ATP Yield from Electron Carriers

Modern estimates of ATP production use the following approximate values:

| Electron Carrier | Approximate ATP Yield |
| ----- | ----- |
| 1 NADH | 2.5 ATP |
| 1 FADH2 | 1.5 ATP |

Older textbook calculations often use:

- 1 NADH = 3 ATP
- 1 FADH$_2$ = 2 ATP

These older values explain the traditional estimate of 36–38 ATP per glucose molecule.

## Anaerobic Respiration and Fermentation

When oxygen is unavailable or insufficient, cells may use fermentation to regenerate NAD$^+$ so that glycolysis can continue.

Fermentation occurs in the **cytoplasm** and does not involve the mitochondrial electron transport chain.

### 1. Alcoholic Fermentation

Occurs in yeast and certain microorganisms.

### Steps

1. Pyruvate loses carbon dioxide to form acetaldehyde.
2. Acetaldehyde is reduced by NADH to form ethanol.
3. NADH is oxidized back to NAD$^+$.

$$
\mathrm{Pyruvate \rightarrow Acetaldehyde + CO_2}
$$

$$
\mathrm{Acetaldehyde + NADH + H^+}
$$

$$
\mathrm{\rightarrow Ethanol + NAD^+}
$$

**Products**: Ethanol and carbon dioxide.

### 2. Lactic Acid Fermentation

Occurs in certain bacteria, such as *Lactobacillus*, and in animal muscle cells under conditions of limited oxygen availability.

### Reaction

$$
\mathrm{Pyruvate + NADH + H^+}
$$

$$
\mathrm{\rightarrow Lactate + NAD^+}
$$

**Products**: Lactate and regenerated NAD$^+$.

### Comparison of Fermentation Types

| Feature | Alcoholic Fermentation | Lactic Acid Fermentation |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Main product | Ethanol | Lactate |
| CO2 release | Yes | No |
| Common organisms/cells | Yeast | *Lactobacillus*, muscle cells |
| NAD+ regeneration | Yes | Yes |
| ATP from fermentation pathway | No additional ATP beyond glycolysis | No additional ATP beyond glycolysis |

**Key concept**: Fermentation itself does not produce additional ATP. The net yield remains two ATP molecules per glucose from glycolysis.

## Respiratory Quotient (RQ)

The Respiratory Quotient is the ratio of the volume of carbon dioxide released to the volume of oxygen consumed during respiration.

### Formula

$$
\mathrm{RQ = \frac{Volume\ of\ CO_2\ evolved}{Volume\ of\ O_2\ consumed}}
$$

The RQ value depends on the respiratory substrate being oxidized.

### RQ Values for Different Substrates

| Respiratory Substrate | Approximate RQ | Explanation |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Carbohydrates | 1.0 | CO2 produced and O2 consumed are approximately equal. |
| Fats/Lipids | 0.7 | More oxygen is required for complete oxidation. |
| Proteins | 0.8–0.9 | RQ varies with the protein and its metabolism. |
| Organic acids | Greater than 1.0 | Relatively more CO2 is released compared with oxygen consumed. |

### Examples

- **Carbohydrates**: Glucose has an RQ of 1.0.
- **Fats**: Tripalmitin has an RQ of approximately 0.7.
- **Proteins**: Usually have an RQ close to 0.8–0.9.
- **Organic acids**: Malic acid generally has an RQ greater than 1.0.

## Common Exam Traps

- **Glycolysis location**: Glycolysis occurs in the cytosol, not inside mitochondria.
- **Oxygen requirement**: Glycolysis does not directly require oxygen.
- **Net glycolytic yield**: Glycolysis produces 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose molecule.
- **Link reaction**: Pyruvate is converted into acetyl-CoA, releasing CO2 and producing NADH.
- **Krebs cycle location**: In eukaryotic cells, the Krebs cycle occurs in the mitochondrial matrix.
- **Krebs cycle yield**: One glucose produces two acetyl-CoA molecules, so the cycle completes two turns.
- **ETC location**: The electron transport chain is located in the inner mitochondrial membrane.
- **Final electron acceptor**: Oxygen is the final electron acceptor in aerobic respiration, forming water.
- **ATP yield estimates**: Modern estimates are approximately 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 ATP per FADH2; older textbooks use 3 and 2 ATP, respectively.
- **Fermentation**: Fermentation regenerates NAD$^+$ but does not produce ATP beyond the glycolytic yield.
- **Substrate-level phosphorylation**: ATP or GTP is directly produced during glycolysis and the Krebs cycle, independently of the electron transport chain.
- **Respiratory quotient**: Carbohydrates have an RQ of 1.0, fats approximately 0.7, and organic acids generally greater than 1.0.

## Quiz

1. What is the net yield of ATP and NADH molecules produced directly per glucose molecule during glycolysis?

- [ ] 4 ATP and 2 NADH
- [x] 2 ATP and 2 NADH
- [ ] 2 ATP and 4 NADH
- [ ] 36 ATP and 0 NADH

> Glycolysis consumes 2 ATP during its preparatory phase and produces 4 ATP during its payoff phase. Therefore, the net yield is 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose molecule.

2. What is the approximate Respiratory Quotient (RQ) when tripalmitin, a typical fat molecule, is completely oxidized during aerobic respiration?

- [ ] 1.0
- [x] 0.7
- [ ] 0.9
- [ ] Greater than 1.3

> Fats contain relatively less oxygen than carbohydrates and require more oxygen for complete oxidation. Therefore, their RQ is approximately 0.7.

3. In the mitochondrial electron transport chain, which component acts as the final terminal electron acceptor during aerobic respiration?

- [ ] Cytochrome c
- [ ] Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)
- [x] Molecular oxygen (O2)
- [ ] Ubiquinone

> Molecular oxygen is the final electron acceptor in the electron transport chain. It combines with electrons and protons at Complex IV to form water.

4. Where does the Krebs cycle occur in eukaryotic cells?

- [ ] Cytosol
- [x] Mitochondrial matrix
- [ ] Inner mitochondrial membrane
- [ ] Mitochondrial intermembrane space

> The Krebs cycle occurs in the mitochondrial matrix, where enzymes catalyse the oxidation of acetyl-CoA.

5. Which enzyme complex directly synthesizes ATP using the proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane?

- [ ] Complex I
- [ ] Complex II
- [ ] Complex IV
- [x] Complex V (ATP synthase)

> Complex V, or ATP synthase, allows protons to flow back into the mitochondrial matrix and uses the released energy to synthesize ATP.

6. What is the primary function of fermentation in cells under anaerobic conditions?

- [ ] To produce large amounts of ATP directly
- [ ] To release oxygen for glycolysis
- [x] To regenerate NAD+ so glycolysis can continue
- [ ] To activate the Krebs cycle

> Fermentation oxidizes NADH back to NAD+, allowing glycolysis to continue producing ATP when the electron transport chain cannot operate normally due to insufficient oxygen.
