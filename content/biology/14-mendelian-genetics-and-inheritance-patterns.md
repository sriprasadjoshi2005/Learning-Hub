**Mendelian genetics** describes the fundamental principles governing the transmission of hereditary traits from parents to offspring. Gregor Mendel established the basic laws of inheritance through experiments on garden pea plants. Later discoveries expanded these principles to explain additional inheritance patterns, collectively called **non-Mendelian inheritance**.

## Monohybrid and Dihybrid Crosses

Genetic crosses are used to predict the possible genotypes and phenotypes of offspring.

### 1. Monohybrid Cross

A **monohybrid cross** involves the inheritance of a single pair of contrasting traits.

Consider a cross between a pure-breeding tall pea plant and a pure-breeding dwarf pea plant.

**Parental Generation (P):**

$$
TT \times tt
$$

**First Filial Generation (F₁):**

All offspring are heterozygous tall plants:

$$
Tt
$$

**Second Filial Generation (F₂):**

Self-pollination of the F₁ plants produces:

$$
Tt \times Tt
$$

|  | $T$ | $t$ |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **$T$** | $TT$ | $Tt$ |
| **$t$** | $Tt$ | $tt$ |

The resulting ratios are:

- **Genotypic ratio:**

$$
1TT:2Tt:1tt
$$

- **Phenotypic ratio:**

$$
3\text{ Tall}:1\text{ Dwarf}
$$

### 2. Dihybrid Cross

A **dihybrid cross** involves the inheritance of two pairs of contrasting traits simultaneously.

Consider seed shape and seed colour in pea plants:

- $R$ = Round seeds (dominant)
- $r$ = Wrinkled seeds (recessive)
- $Y$ = Yellow seeds (dominant)
- $y$ = Green seeds (recessive)

**Parental Generation (P):**

$$
RRYY \times rryy
$$

**First Filial Generation (F₁):**

All offspring are heterozygous for both traits:

$$
RrYy
$$

Each F₁ plant produces four types of gametes in equal proportions, assuming independent assortment:

$$
RY,\ Ry,\ rY,\ ry
$$

Self-pollination of the F₁ plants produces the following F₂ phenotypic ratio:

| Phenotype | Expected Proportion |
| ----- | ----- |
| Round Yellow | $9/16$ |
| Round Green | $3/16$ |
| Wrinkled Yellow | $3/16$ |
| Wrinkled Green | $1/16$ |

Therefore, the F₂ phenotypic ratio is:

$$
9:3:3:1
$$

## Fundamental Mendelian Laws

Mendel proposed three fundamental laws to explain the inheritance of characteristics.

### 1. Law of Dominance

The **Law of Dominance** states that characters are controlled by discrete hereditary factors called genes, which occur in pairs in diploid organisms.

When two different alleles are present in an individual, one allele may express itself while the other remains masked.

**Example:**

In pea plants:

- $T$ represents the dominant allele for tallness.
- $t$ represents the recessive allele for dwarfness.

The heterozygous genotype $Tt$ produces a tall plant because $T$ is dominant over $t$ under complete dominance.

### 2. Law of Segregation (Purity of Gametes)

The **Law of Segregation** states that the two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete receives only one allele of the pair.

For example, a heterozygous plant with genotype $Tt$ produces two types of gametes:

$$
T \quad \text{and} \quad t
$$

Each type is produced with an expected probability of $50\%$.

This separation occurs during meiosis when homologous chromosomes segregate.

### 3. Law of Independent Assortment

The **Law of Independent Assortment** states that alleles of different genes assort independently during gamete formation when the genes are unlinked.

For example, a plant with genotype $RrYy$ can produce four types of gametes:

$$
RY,\ Ry,\ rY,\ ry
$$

Under independent assortment, each gamete type occurs with an expected probability of $25\%$.

**Important:** Independent assortment is generally applicable to genes located on different chromosomes. Genes located close together on the same chromosome may show linkage and tend to be inherited together.

## Deviations from Mendelian Inheritance

Not all inheritance patterns follow complete dominance. Several genetic mechanisms produce different phenotypic relationships.

### 1. Incomplete Dominance

**Incomplete dominance** occurs when neither allele completely masks the other, resulting in an intermediate phenotype in the heterozygous condition.

**Example: Four O'Clock Plant (*Mirabilis jalapa*)**

- $RR$ = Red flowers
- $WW$ = White flowers
- $RW$ = Pink flowers

Crossing a red-flowered plant with a white-flowered plant:

$$
RR \times WW
$$

All F₁ offspring are pink:

$$
RW
$$

Self-pollination produces:

$$
RW \times RW
$$

The F₂ phenotypic ratio is:

$$
1\text{ Red}:2\text{ Pink}:1\text{ White}
$$

In incomplete dominance, the F₂ phenotypic ratio is identical to the genotypic ratio.

### 2. Codominance

**Codominance** occurs when both alleles express themselves independently and equally in a heterozygous individual.

**Example: ABO Blood Group System**

In the ABO blood group system:

- $I^A$ produces A antigen on red blood cells.
- $I^B$ produces B antigen on red blood cells.
- $i$ produces neither A nor B antigen.

The alleles $I^A$ and $I^B$ are codominant to each other, while both are dominant over $i$.

An individual with genotype:

$$
I^AI^B
$$

has blood group AB because both A and B antigens are expressed.

### 3. Multiple Alleles

**Multiple alleles** refers to the existence of more than two alternative forms of a gene within a population.

However, a diploid individual can carry only two alleles of a particular gene at a time.

**Example: ABO Blood Group System**

The ABO blood group gene has three major alleles:

$$
I^A,\ I^B,\ i
$$

The six possible genotypes produce four blood group phenotypes:

| Genotype | Blood Group |
| ----- | ----- |
| $I^AI^A$ or $I^Ai$ | A |
| $I^BI^B$ or $I^Bi$ | B |
| $I^AI^B$ | AB |
| $ii$ | O |

### Comparison of Non-Mendelian Inheritance Patterns

| Pattern | Allelic Relationship | Heterozygous Phenotype | Example |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Incomplete Dominance** | Neither allele is completely dominant | Intermediate phenotype | Pink flowers in *Mirabilis jalapa* |
| **Codominance** | Both alleles are expressed | Both traits expressed together | AB blood group |
| **Multiple Alleles** | More than two alleles exist in the population | Depends on the two alleles inherited | ABO blood group system |

## Diagnostic Crosses

A **test cross** is used to determine the genotype of an organism showing a dominant phenotype but having an unknown genotype.

The organism is crossed with a **homozygous recessive** individual.

For example:

$$
T\_ \times tt
$$

The unknown parent may be either $TT$ or $Tt$.

### Case 1: Unknown Parent Is Homozygous Dominant

$$
TT \times tt
$$

All offspring will be heterozygous:

$$
100\%\ Tt
$$

Therefore, all offspring exhibit the dominant phenotype.

### Case 2: Unknown Parent Is Heterozygous

$$
Tt \times tt
$$

|  | $t$ | $t$ |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **$T$** | $Tt$ | $Tt$ |
| **$t$** | $tt$ | $tt$ |

The offspring show:

- $50\%$ dominant phenotype ($Tt$).
- $50\%$ recessive phenotype ($tt$).

Therefore, the phenotypic ratio is:

$$
1:1
$$

### Dihybrid Test Cross

A dihybrid test cross involves crossing an individual heterozygous for two genes with an individual homozygous recessive for both genes.

$$
TtRr \times ttrr
$$

Assuming independent assortment, the heterozygous parent produces four gamete types:

$$
TR,\ Tr,\ tR,\ tr
$$

The recessive parent produces only:

$$
tr
$$

The resulting offspring phenotypes occur in equal proportions:

$$
1:1:1:1
$$

- Tall Round
- Tall Wrinkled
- Dwarf Round
- Dwarf Wrinkled

## Common Exam Traps

- **Monohybrid Ratios:** Under complete dominance, the F₂ genotypic ratio is $1:2:1$, while the phenotypic ratio is $3:1$.
- **Dihybrid Ratio:** The expected F₂ phenotypic ratio is $9:3:3:1$ under complete dominance and independent assortment.
- **Incomplete Dominance vs. Codominance:** In incomplete dominance, the heterozygote shows an intermediate phenotype. In codominance, both alleles are expressed.
- **Phenotypic and Genotypic Ratios:** In incomplete dominance, the F₂ phenotypic ratio matches the genotypic ratio ($1:2:1$).
- **ABO Blood Groups:** $I^A$ and $I^B$ are codominant, while both are dominant over $i$.
- **Multiple Alleles:** More than two alleles can exist in a population, but a diploid individual carries only two alleles at a given locus.
- **Test Cross:** An individual with an unknown dominant genotype is crossed with a homozygous recessive individual.
- **Independent Assortment:** This law applies to unlinked genes. Closely linked genes on the same chromosome may not assort independently.
- **Law of Segregation:** The two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation. This is a fundamental consequence of chromosome segregation in meiosis.

## Quiz

1. A botanist crosses a true-breeding red-flowered snapdragon (*Antirrhinum majus*) with a true-breeding white-flowered plant. All F₁ offspring produce pink flowers. Self-pollination of the F₁ plants yields an F₂ phenotypic ratio of 1 Red : 2 Pink : 1 White. What mode of inheritance is demonstrated?

- [ ] Complete dominance
- [x] Incomplete dominance
- [ ] Codominance
- [ ] Epistasis

> In incomplete dominance, neither allele completely dominates the other. The heterozygote exhibits an intermediate phenotype (pink). The F₂ phenotypic and genotypic ratios are both $1:2:1$.

2. An individual with blood group AB marries a partner with blood group O ($ii$). What are the possible blood group phenotypes of their biological children?

- [ ] $100\%$ AB
- [ ] $50\%$ AB and $50\%$ O
- [x] $50\%$ A and $50\%$ B
- [ ] $25\%$ A, $25\%$ B, $25\%$ AB, and $25\%$ O

> The AB parent produces gametes carrying either $I^A$ or $I^B$, while the O parent produces only $i$ gametes. The possible offspring genotypes are $I^Ai$ (blood group A) and $I^Bi$ (blood group B), in a $1:1$ ratio.

3. What is the expected phenotypic ratio resulting from a test cross between a heterozygous tall, heterozygous round pea plant ($TtRr$) and a homozygous dwarf, wrinkled plant ($ttrr$), assuming independent assortment?

- [ ] $9:3:3:1$
- [ ] $3:1$
- [x] $1:1:1:1$
- [ ] $1:2:1$

> The dihybrid test cross ($TtRr \times ttrr$) produces four types of offspring in equal proportions: 1 Tall Round : 1 Tall Wrinkled : 1 Dwarf Round : 1 Dwarf Wrinkled. This occurs because the heterozygous parent produces four equally probable gamete types under independent assortment.
